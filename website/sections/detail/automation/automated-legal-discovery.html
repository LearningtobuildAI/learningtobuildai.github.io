9Engineering Breakdown
  • Ingestion and parsing — brings documents from email, file shares, and case systems, and turns scans and PDFs into text. Key parts: connectors, OCR engine, metadata extractor, de-duplication.
  • Enrichment and indexing — extracts entities, dates, and relationships, and builds a searchable semantic index. Key parts: named entity recognition, language detection, embedding model, vector database.
  • Query and ranking — translates a legal question into a search and ranks documents by likely relevance. Key parts: query parser, hybrid search (keyword + vector), relevance model, active learning loop.
  • LLM synthesis — summarizes documents, answers questions, and flags anomalies, grounded in retrieved passages. Key parts: large language model, retrieval-augmented generation, prompt templates, citation grounding.
  • Review interface — where lawyers read, tag, redact, and export documents. Key parts: document viewer, tagging and coding, redaction tools, privilege log.
  • Security and audit — keeps data confidential, controls access, and records every action for defensibility. Key parts: encryption, role-based access, audit trail, data residency controls.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the discovery protocol. lawyers specify the legal questions, date ranges, custodians, and what counts as relevant or anomalous.
  2. Ingest and clean documents. connectors pull data from email, shared drives, and case management systems; OCR converts scans; duplicates and system files are removed.
  3. Build the semantic index. every passage is converted into a vector embedding and stored alongside keyword indexes for hybrid retrieval.
  4. Configure relevance ranking. a seed set of documents is manually coded; the ranking model learns to prioritize similar material, and thresholds are set for review.
  5. Integrate LLM synthesis. retrieval is connected to a large language model with prompts that require citations; outputs are constrained to the retrieved text to reduce hallucination.
  6. Test and validate. performance is measured against a manually reviewed sample, checking recall, precision, and consistency across document types and languages.
  7. Deploy and monitor. the system is rolled out to the review team; logs and feedback are used to retrain models and catch drift or bias.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Optical character recognition (OCR) and document parsing — many legal documents are scanned images or complex PDFs that must be converted to text before any AI can read them.
  • EXISTS Vector databases and semantic search — they let a system find passages by meaning rather than exact keywords across millions of documents.
  • PARTIAL Large language models and retrieval-augmented generation — they can synthesize and summarize, but without careful grounding they hallucinate and may mishandle privileged or confidential material.
  • EXISTS Technology-assisted review (predictive coding) — it is a proven method to prioritize documents for human review and is accepted in several legal systems.
  • PARTIAL Legal-domain knowledge bases and ontologies — understanding precedents, privileges, and anomalies requires domain-specific training or curated legal concepts, which are still being built.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: AI-assisted review with human oversight: systems flag relevant documents, summarize them with citations, and draft privilege logs. — faster and cheaper discovery for large document sets, letting lawyers focus on judgment.
  2. Next: proactive anomaly detection across entire document sets without explicit queries, such as spotting inconsistent statements or missing dates. — earlier case strategy, compliance risk detection, and investigation leads.
  3. Next: multimodal and multi-jurisdictional reasoning, handling audio, video, and legal concepts across different countries. — global compliance and cross-border investigations in one workflow.
  4. Longer term: end-to-end discovery agents that conduct review, draft privilege logs, and produce defensible outputs with minimal human intervention. — near-instant discovery for routine matters, freeing legal teams for complex judgment and negotiation.
