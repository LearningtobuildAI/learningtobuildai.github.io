PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

9Engineering Breakdown
  • World model — an explicit, updatable model of the organisation, its market and its competitors, used to predict what a decision would do. Key parts: digital twin of operations, causal/statistical model, scenario generator, market and competitor data feeds.
  • Perception and data layer — turns messy organisational signals into structured state the agent can reason over. Key parts: system connectors (ERP, CRM, finance), document and email parsing, retrieval index, data quality checks.
  • Reasoning core — decomposes a strategic question into sub-problems and proposes candidate actions. Key parts: foundation model, planning/search algorithms, tool-calling interface, chain-of-thought and reflection prompts.
  • Memory — stores past decisions and their outcomes so the agent does not repeat mistakes. Key parts: vector store of documents, knowledge graph of entities and relations, case history of decisions and results.
  • Verification and self-correction loop — replaces the executive's judgement with simulated consequence-checking and adversarial critique. Key parts: critic model, constraint and feasibility solver, scenario replay against history, uncertainty estimates.
  • Action and tool layer — lets proposals actually change systems, under permissions. Key parts: API integrations, workflow/RPA execution, approval gates, rollback mechanisms.
  • Governance and audit — keeps decisions traceable, reviewable and accountable to humans and regulators. Key parts: policy engine, immutable decision log, escalation rules, explanation output.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Instrument the organisation. Connect financial, operational and market systems into one consistent schema, so the agent sees the same numbers the executives do.
  2. Build a simulatable model of the business. Fit a causal or simulation model that can be calibrated against past decisions and asked 'what would have happened if...'.
  3. Assemble the reasoning core. Take a large foundation model, give it tools (search, calculation, database queries) and fine-tune it on the organisation's own decision records where they exist.
  4. Add memory and retrieval. Index past memos, board papers and outcomes so proposals are grounded in the organisation's real history rather than generic advice.
  5. Wire in self-correction. Have the agent generate several candidate plans, run each through the simulator and a critic model, and revise until constraints and forecasts hold.
  6. Run in shadow mode. For months, let the agent produce recommendations that are never acted on, and compare them against what the humans actually chose and what happened.
  7. Grant bounded authority. Give the agent write access only to reversible, well-modelled decisions — pricing rules, staffing rosters, inventory — with human approval above a threshold.
  8. Monitor and govern. Track accuracy, drift in the model's assumptions and every action taken, with an escalation path when confidence is low.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Long-horizon planning in AI agents — current agents handle tasks of minutes to hours; strategy spans quarters and feedback arrives too late for naive reinforcement learning.
  • PARTIAL Organisational digital twins — few firms have a coherent, live model of their own operations to simulate decisions against.
  • PARTIAL Causal inference and counterfactual modelling — correlation is not enough; the agent must estimate what a decision causes, not just what it correlates with.
  • NOT YET Evaluation of open-ended judgement — unlike a game with a score, strategic quality may only be knowable years later, making it hard to train or verify.
  • NOT YET Accountability and liability frameworks — someone must answer legally and ethically for a decision, and that attribution is unresolved for autonomous agents.
  • EXISTS Compute and data infrastructure — training and serving large agentic models at enterprise scale is expensive but technically feasible today.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: copilots that summarise, forecast and draft analysis while a human decides — time savings and better-prepared human judgement, with no delegation of authority.
  2. Next: agents executing bounded, reversible workflows with approval gates — measurable reliability data on which decisions can safely be delegated.
  3. Next: closed-loop optimisation in narrow operational domains such as pricing, scheduling and supply chain — fast feedback loops where outcomes are measurable within days.
  4. Longer term: agents advising on genuine strategy, with simulation-checked reasoning and full audit trails — faster strategy cycles — still with human executives accountable for the call.
  5. Speculative: delegated authority for executive functions — possible only if verification, liability and evaluation problems are solved; otherwise it remains assistance, not replacement.
