PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Qubit chip — the processor itself: a lattice of superconducting loops whose circulating currents represent problem variables, with couplers between them. Key parts: niobium/aluminium superconducting loops, Josephson junctions (Al-AlOx-Al), inductive couplers between qubits, flux-bias lines.
  • Cryogenic system — keeps the chip at millikelvin temperatures so the circuits are superconducting and thermal noise is negligible. Key parts: dilution refrigerator, cryogenic wiring and attenuators, thermal shields.
  • Magnetic shielding — blocks Earth's field and external interference that would corrupt the qubits. Key parts: mu-metal enclosure, superconducting shield.
  • Control and readout electronics — sets the initial transverse field, the problem biases and couplers, then reads out each qubit's final state. Key parts: room-temperature digital-to-analog converters, SQUID readout amplifiers, multiplexing electronics.
  • Software stack — turns a user's optimisation problem into a schedule of magnetic fields the hardware can run. Key parts: problem mapping to quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation (QUBO), minor embedding onto the chip's connectivity graph, anneal schedule control.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the qubit topology. Designers choose the layout of qubits and couplers — for example a chimera or pegasus graph — which sets how many variables a problem can hold and how easily it embeds.
  2. Fabricate the chip. Niobium and aluminium films are deposited on a silicon wafer and patterned by lithography; Josephson junctions are formed by oxidising a thin aluminium layer between two electrodes.
  3. Package and wire-bond. The chip is mounted in a superconducting package and connected to control lines that carry magnetic flux to each qubit and coupler.
  4. Cool and shield. The package is loaded into a dilution refrigerator, cooled to around 10–20 millikelvin, and enclosed in magnetic shielding.
  5. Calibrate the qubits. Each qubit's persistent current, inductance and coupler strength are measured and tuned so that the intended problem Hamiltonian can be realised.
  6. Characterise the anneal. Engineers run the transverse field down over a chosen time and map how often the system lands in the lowest-energy state, adjusting schedules to reduce errors.
  7. Embed and run problems. A user's problem is mapped to the chip's graph, embedded in software, then submitted; the chip anneals once per sample and returns many readouts.
  8. Interpret results. Readouts are corrected for bias and returned as candidate solutions; classical software then checks and refines them.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Superconducting Josephson junction fabrication — qubits and couplers are superconducting loops broken by Josephson junctions; without reliable junctions there is no annealer.
  • EXISTS Dilution refrigeration — the chip must stay at millikelvin temperatures to remain superconducting and resist thermal noise.
  • EXISTS Low-noise cryogenic control and readout — setting and reading thousands of flux biases requires precise, low-noise electronics.
  • PARTIAL Embedding algorithms for sparse connectivity — chip graphs are not fully connected, so problems must be mapped onto them — often with large overhead that limits practical problem size.
  • NOT YET Error correction or error mitigation for annealing — current devices can be trapped by noise or settle in excited states; no scalable error-correction scheme has been demonstrated for annealing.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: commercial annealers with thousands of qubits (for example D-Wave's Advantage systems) used in hybrid quantum-classical workflows — hands-on exploration of scheduling, portfolio and materials-search problems, though clear quantum speedup is still unproven.
  2. Next: higher-connectivity topologies (such as D-Wave's Zephyr design) and longer coherence — less embedding overhead, so larger and more natural problems can be represented directly.
  3. Next: integrated error mitigation or logical qubits in annealing hardware — answers that are reliable enough to trust on hard, real-world instances.
  4. Longer term: demonstrated quantum advantage on a commercially relevant optimisation task — adoption in logistics, finance, energy and materials discovery, and a clearer role alongside gate-based quantum computers.
