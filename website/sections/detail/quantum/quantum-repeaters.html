PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Entangled photon source — creates pairs of photons whose states are correlated, the raw material of every link. Key parts: nonlinear crystal for spontaneous parametric down-conversion, or semiconductor quantum dot, pump laser, filters and fibre coupling.
  • Quantum memory — stores a photon's quantum state and releases it later on demand, so links can be joined. Key parts: diamond nitrogen-vacancy centre, trapped ion, cold atom cloud or rare-earth-doped crystal, optical cavity to boost light-matter coupling, control lasers.
  • Wavelength interface — matches the emitter's wavelength to the 1550 nm band where fibre loss is lowest. Key parts: quantum frequency converter, low-loss fibre components.
  • Bell-state measurement station — the swapping operation — entangles two memories that never interacted by measuring two photons jointly. Key parts: beamsplitter and interferometer, single-photon detectors, coincidence timing electronics.
  • Purification and error handling — trades many lower-fidelity links for fewer better ones. Key parts: entanglement distillation optics, classical feedback channel.
  • Control and synchronisation — keeps distant nodes phase-locked and timed to picoseconds. Key parts: atomic clocks or GPS-disciplined clocks, classical telecom links.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a memory platform. Each candidate — diamond colour centres, rare-earth crystals, cold atoms, trapped ions — trades coherence time against bandwidth and ease of fibre coupling.
  2. Make efficient single photons. A pumped nonlinear crystal or an electrically driven quantum dot emits photon pairs on demand with high purity and near-unity collection into fibre.
  3. Store and retrieve. A photon is absorbed into the memory's collective excitation, held for microseconds to milliseconds, and read out on command as a photon with the original state.
  4. Link two nodes. Photons from two memories interfere on a beamsplitter; a successful joint detection signals that the memories are now entangled.
  5. Swap and extend. At the midpoint of a longer span, a Bell-state measurement on photons from two adjacent links joins them into one longer entangled link.
  6. Purify. Because each swap adds noise, several copies of a link are combined so that the surviving one has higher fidelity than any input.
  7. Multiplex. Many memory modes and wavelength channels are operated in parallel so that the low probability of success per attempt still yields a usable rate.
  8. Deploy in fibre. Nodes, detectors and timing hardware are installed in real ducts with classical synchronisation traffic sharing the same cable.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Superconducting nanowire single-photon detectors — near-unity efficiency and low dark counts are needed to catch the fragile photons, and these are commercially supplied today.
  • EXISTS Low-loss telecom fibre and QKD hardware — the transmission medium and much of the optics already exist and are deployed for quantum key distribution.
  • PARTIAL Long-coherence quantum memories with efficient light interfaces — laboratory memories reach useful coherence times, but combining that with high retrieval efficiency and telecom compatibility is unsolved.
  • PARTIAL Entanglement swapping and purification at high fidelity — swapping between two memory nodes has been demonstrated, but fidelity and rates remain far below what a network needs.
  • PARTIAL Quantum frequency conversion — visible-band emitters must be shifted to 1550 nm without destroying entanglement; shown in labs with added noise to control.
  • PARTIAL Integrated photonic and cryogenic engineering — thousands of stable interferometers and detectors must fit into field-deployable, serviceable hardware.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: two-node entanglement across installed metro fibre and small multi-node laboratory networks — proof that memories, photons and swapping work together outside a bench.
  2. Next: three-to-five-node chains with swapping at one or two intermediate stations and memory multiplexing — rates high enough for device-independent cryptography and early quantum-network services.
  3. Next: field-deployed repeater segments operating at the distances where direct fibre transmission fails — city-to-city entanglement without trusted intermediate nodes.
  4. Longer term: repeater chains combined with free-space satellite links (as demonstrated by the Micius mission) — continental-scale entanglement distribution and, eventually, a quantum internet linking processors.
