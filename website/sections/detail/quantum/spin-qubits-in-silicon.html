PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Isotopically purified silicon host — the material the spins live in; removing silicon-29 nuclear spins greatly extends coherence. Key parts: silicon-28 enriched wafer or Si/SiGe heterostructure, strained silicon quantum well.
  • Gate-defined quantum dots — electrostatic cages that trap one or a few electrons each. Key parts: plunger and barrier gates, tunnel barriers to an electron reservoir, oxide or SiGe insulating layers.
  • Spin control structures — let the spin be rotated electrically rather than by a bulky microwave coil. Key parts: micromagnet or stripline for a magnetic field gradient, exchange-coupling regions between neighbouring dots.
  • Readout — senses whether a spin is up or down. Key parts: single-electron transistor, radio-frequency reflectometry circuit, Pauli spin-blockade sensor dot.
  • Cryogenic environment — keeps the device at millikelvin temperatures so the spins stay coherent. Key parts: dilution refrigerator, filtered and shielded wiring.
  • Control electronics — delivers precise nanosecond voltage pulses to many gates without cooking the fridge. Key parts: room-temperature pulse generators, cryogenic CMOS multiplexers, high-density cabling.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Enrich the silicon. Natural silicon is about 5% silicon-29; isotopic separation raises the silicon-28 fraction above 99%, so far fewer nuclear spins disturb the qubits.
  2. Grow the wafer stack. On a silicon wafer, layers of silicon germanium and a thin strained silicon well are grown by epitaxy, or a gate oxide is grown for a silicon-MOS style device.
  3. Pattern the gates. Semiconductor lithography defines nanometre-scale metal gate electrodes; this is the step that could in principle run on existing CMOS production lines.
  4. Cool and load electrons. The chip is mounted in a dilution refrigerator at around 0.1 K, and gate voltages are tuned so single electrons tunnel in from a reservoir and are held in place.
  5. Tune to the single-electron regime. Automated tuning sweeps gate voltages until each dot holds exactly one electron and its charge state is stable.
  6. Calibrate spin control and readout. Microwave bursts on the gates flip spins; the sensor dot detects the resulting charge change, and gate fidelities are measured.
  7. Build small arrays. Two, four or more dots are coupled in a line or grid so neighbouring spins can interact for two-qubit gates.
  8. Multiplex the wiring. Cryogenic control chips and crossbar addressing reduce the number of wires running from room temperature, which is the main obstacle to larger arrays.
  9. Operate error-corrected logical qubits. Many physical spins are combined into one protected logical qubit — a milestone not yet reached in silicon.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Advanced CMOS fabrication — the key promise is that these qubits are made with the same lithography, oxides and metal gates as ordinary microchips.
  • PARTIAL Isotopically enriched silicon-28 — it is produced in small quantities at high cost; large, uniform wafers would be needed for manufacturing.
  • EXISTS Millikelvin refrigeration — dilution refrigerators are standard laboratory equipment, but each one supports only a limited number of qubits and wires.
  • PARTIAL Cryogenic control electronics — cryo-CMOS multiplexers have been demonstrated at a few kelvin, but addressing thousands of gates is unsolved.
  • NOT YET Quantum error correction at scale — no platform, including silicon, has yet run enough qubits with low enough error rates to make a reliably protected logical qubit.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: laboratory devices with a handful of spins; single-qubit gate fidelities above 99.9% in purified silicon — confidence that spins are a viable qubit, and that some devices work near 1 K.
  2. Next: tens to hundreds of qubits with integrated cryogenic control and automated tuning — system-level tests of wiring, calibration and reliability on a chip.
  3. Next: a demonstrated error-corrected logical qubit in silicon — the first credible route to scaling by adding more dots rather than more fridges.
  4. Longer term: fab-compatible production of many-qubit chips, with modules linked by spin shuttling or microwave cavities — large quantum processors built on existing semiconductor supply chains.
