PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Cold-atom interferometers measure gravity and acceleration without the drift that limits classical instruments, which matters most where satellite navigation is unavailable. Instruments are already deployed for geophysical survey and are being ruggedised for vehicle use.

4How It Works

Atoms are cooled and launched, then split and recombined with laser pulses; the interference phase depends on the gravitational field difference along the two interferometer arms.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cold-atom source — produces a cloud of ultra-cold atoms that can be manipulated coherently. Key parts: ultra-high-vacuum chamber, alkali metal dispenser (rubidium-87 or caesium-133), magneto-optical trap coils.
  • Laser and optical system — cools, launches, splits and recombines the atoms. Key parts: frequency-stabilised diode lasers, acousto-optic modulators, optical fibre distribution, photodetectors.
  • Interferometer and detection — turns the atoms into a sensitive gravity or acceleration probe. Key parts: Raman or Bragg pulse optics, state-selective fluorescence detection, magnetic shielding.
  • Vibration and environmental isolation — keeps platform motion and field noise from swamping the atomic signal. Key parts: passive or active isolation mount, seismometer or classical accelerometer, tiltmeter, thermal enclosure.
  • Control and signal processing — runs the pulse sequence and extracts the phase shift. Key parts: field-programmable gate array timing controller, fringe-fitting algorithms, real-time data output.
  • Navigation integration — combines the drift-free atomic measurement with high-bandwidth classical sensors. Key parts: classical inertial measurement unit, Kalman or similar fusion filter, GNSS receiver where available.
  • Ruggedised enclosure — makes the laboratory instrument survive vehicles, ships and aircraft. Key parts: shock and vibration mounts, compact vacuum pumps and getters, laser and electronics packaging.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Make and hold the vacuum. The chamber is baked and sealed to ultra-high vacuum, then an alkali metal source is loaded so atoms can be trapped without collisions.
  2. Cool and trap atoms. Six laser beams and a magnetic field form a magneto-optical trap, slowing atoms to microkelvin temperatures — a few millionths of a degree above absolute zero.
  3. Launch the atoms. The cloud is thrown upwards or released in free fall, giving a clean vertical measurement path.
  4. Split, redirect and recombine. Laser pulses act as beam splitters and mirrors, placing each atom in a superposition of two momentum states that travel along different paths before recombining.
  5. Read out the interference. Laser light detects how many atoms ended in each state; the resulting fringe pattern carries the phase shift.
  6. Convert phase to gravity or acceleration. The measured phase is compared with the known laser pulse timings to give an absolute value of gravitational acceleration or platform acceleration.
  7. Fuse with classical sensors. Because the atomic measurement is absolute but slower and noisier at high frequency, it is blended with accelerometers and gyroscopes to give a drift-free navigation solution.
  8. Ruggedise and calibrate. The instrument is shock-mounted, thermally stabilised, magnetically shielded and checked against a reference gravity site before field deployment.
  9. Deploy and operate. It runs from a vehicle, ship or aircraft, either surveying gravity or feeding a navigation filter where satellite signals are degraded or unavailable.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Laser cooling and trapping of atoms — without microkelvin atoms the interference time is too short to be useful.
  • EXISTS Frequency-stabilised, narrow-linewidth lasers — the atomic phase depends on laser frequency and timing being controlled to extreme precision.
  • EXISTS Ultra-high-vacuum and compact atomic sources — cold atoms must be isolated from background gas for the measurement to survive.
  • EXISTS Vibration isolation and classical inertial sensors — platform motion is often larger than the gravity signal, so it must be measured and removed.
  • PARTIAL Ruggedised, compact, low-power laser systems — today's field instruments are still bulky and power-hungry compared with conventional sensors.
  • PARTIAL Dynamic-environment operation and real-time fusion — the hard step for navigation is keeping accuracy while the platform is accelerating and turning.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: commercial cold-atom gravimeters used for geophysical survey, volcano and groundwater monitoring — absolute gravity measurements with no drift, which classical relative gravimeters cannot give over long surveys.
  2. Next: shipborne and airborne cold-atom gravimetry and gravity gradiometry — faster, cheaper mapping of mineral, oil and gas prospects under water and ice.
  3. Next: compact quantum inertial measurement units tested for GNSS-denied navigation — drift-free position keeping for submarines, underground vehicles and urban operations where satellite signals are jammed or blocked.
  4. Longer term: hybrid quantum-classical navigation systems in routine use — resilient navigation that does not depend on external signals, plus precise geodetic reference data.
