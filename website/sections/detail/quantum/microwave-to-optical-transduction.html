PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Superconducting qubits operate at microwave frequencies that attenuate rapidly in optical fibre, so a device converting single photons between microwave and telecom wavelengths is essential for linking quantum processors over distance.

4How It Works

Electro-optic or optomechanical devices couple a microwave cavity to an optical cavity so that a single microwave photon shifts the optical resonance, transferring the quantum state without adding thermal noise.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Microwave resonator — holds the qubit's microwave excitation in a superconducting circuit. Key parts: high-Q superconducting thin-film inductor and capacitor, coupling port to the qubit, ground-plane shielding.
  • Coupling element — the active medium that shifts optical behaviour in response to a microwave field. Key parts: thin-film lithium niobate waveguide or ring (electro-optic), or a suspended nanobeam/membrane with a shared optical and mechanical mode, drive electrodes.
  • Optical cavity — enhances the interaction and defines the telecom wavelength mode. Key parts: photonic-crystal mirrors or ring resonator, fibre taper or grating coupler, on-chip filters.
  • Cryogenic package — keeps thermal photons far below one per mode and brings signals in and out. Key parts: dilution refrigerator stage, superconducting wiring to the qubit chip, optical fibre feedthroughs with low heat leak, radiation shielding.
  • Pump and control — supplies the laser pump and microwave tones that drive the conversion. Key parts: frequency-stabilised fibre laser, phase and amplitude modulators, circulators and optical filters.
  • Verification chain — proves the transfer is quantum, not classical. Key parts: single-photon detectors, qubit state readout, added-noise measurement.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design matched modes. Choose the microwave and optical cavity linewidths so the two decay rates are equal — the condition for impedance matching and the highest conversion efficiency.
  2. Fabricate the microwave circuit. Superconducting niobium or aluminium films are deposited on a low-loss substrate and patterned into resonators with lithography.
  3. Fabricate the photonic chip. Thin-film lithium niobate or silicon-on-insulator is patterned by electron-beam lithography and etched into waveguides and ring resonators.
  4. Integrate the two. The photonic chip and superconducting circuit are aligned with sub-micron precision and bonded, so one optical mode and one microwave mode overlap in the same volume.
  5. Package and fibre-couple. Optical fibres are attached to the chip and superconducting wiring run to the qubit, all inside a shielded enclosure.
  6. Cool to millikelvin. The assembly goes into a dilution refrigerator so that thermal photons cannot swamp the single-photon signal.
  7. Characterise. Laser light is sent in and microwave photons out (and vice versa); efficiency, bandwidth and added noise are measured.
  8. Close the link. Two qubits are connected through optical fibre and entanglement between them is verified.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-Q superconducting microwave resonators — they hold the microwave photon long enough to interact with the optical mode.
  • PARTIAL Low-loss integrated photonics (thin-film lithium niobate, silicon) — needed for strong electro-optic coupling and long optical storage times on a chip.
  • EXISTS Dilution refrigeration — thermal noise must be well under one photon at microwave frequencies.
  • EXISTS Single-photon detection and qubit readout — without them a quantum conversion cannot be distinguished from a classical one.
  • NOT YET Efficient, low-noise coupling at the single-photon level — no device yet combines high efficiency with added noise below one photon at useful bandwidth.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: table-top demonstrations converting microwave and optical photons, mostly at efficiencies from a fraction of a per cent up to tens of per cent with narrow bandwidth — proof that the two frequency domains can be linked.
  2. Next: devices with high efficiency, near-quantum-limited added noise and megahertz-scale bandwidth — faithful transfer of qubit states over metres of fibre.
  3. Next: entanglement distributed between two superconducting qubits via optical fibre — modular quantum processors and the first quantum repeater nodes.
  4. Longer term: transducers integrated into repeater chains and networked quantum computers — a quantum internet in which microwave qubits talk over today's telecom fibre.
