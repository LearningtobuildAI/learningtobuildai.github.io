PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Qubits encoded in superpositions of coherent states of a microwave resonator are intrinsically biased against bit-flip errors, giving a hardware-level advantage that simplifies the error-correction stack.

4How It Works

A nonlinear element stabilises two coherent states of opposite phase inside the cavity; photon loss causes predominantly phase flips, so a cheap repetition code corrects the dominant error channel while bit flips remain exponentially suppressed.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Storage cavity — holds the quantum state as a superposition of two coherent microwave states of opposite phase. Key parts: superconducting λ/2 or lumped-element microwave resonator, coupling ports and drive lines.
  • Nonlinear stabilising element — supplies the two-photon drive and two-photon dissipation that pump the two coherent states apart and keep them apart. Key parts: Josephson junctions (often in a SNAIL or similar loop), pump line at twice the cavity frequency.
  • Ancilla and readout — a separate superconducting qubit that measures the cavity's parity without destroying the encoded state. Key parts: transmon ancilla, readout resonator, dispersive coupling to the storage cavity.
  • Cryogenic and microwave infrastructure — keeps the chip cold and delivers clean, filtered signals. Key parts: dilution refrigerator, attenuators, filters and circulators, room-temperature microwave generators and digitisers.
  • Bias-preserving logic — gates that manipulate logical states without converting the rare bit flips into common phase flips. Key parts: bias-preserving two-qubit gate, parity-measurement circuits.
  • Classical decoding — reads the parity syndromes and tracks the phase errors they reveal. Key parts: repetition-code decoder, real-time control electronics.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the circuit. Choose the cavity frequency, the strength of the nonlinear element and the two-photon drive amplitude. Larger coherent-state separation means exponentially rarer bit flips, but a more demanding resonator.
  2. Fabricate the chip. Deposit a niobium film on a sapphire or silicon wafer and pattern the resonators with lithography; build aluminium–aluminium oxide–aluminium Josephson junctions by shadow evaporation in a single vacuum step.
  3. Cool the package. Mount the chip in a dilution refrigerator and cool it to around 10 millikelvin, below the superconducting transition and low enough to empty the microwave modes of thermal photons.
  4. Characterise the bare hardware. Measure cavity resonance, photon lifetime and the ancilla qubit's frequency and dispersive coupling, then calibrate the pump line.
  5. Stabilise a cat state. Drive the nonlinear element at twice the cavity frequency so the two-photon processes create and maintain the two-lobed state; tomograph its Wigner function to confirm the lobes.
  6. Measure the error bias. Run the state over time and count bit flips versus phase flips to verify that bit flips are suppressed exponentially with the size of the coherent states.
  7. Add error-correcting logic. Chain several cat qubits and repeatedly measure parity to detect phase flips, while gates are tuned to be bias-preserving.
  8. Scale the processor. Wire many cavities together, route pumps and readout lines, and move control electronics into the fridge or into cryogenic integrated circuits to keep the wiring manageable.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Superconducting qubit fabrication — the cavity, ancilla and nonlinear element all come from standard Josephson-junction processes.
  • PARTIAL Reservoir engineering with two-photon dissipation — stabilising coherent-state superpositions by driving a nonlinear cavity was demonstrated in the mid-2010s, but with modest lifetimes and few devices.
  • PARTIAL Long-lived microwave resonators — bit-flip protection grows with the number of photons the cavity can hold before one leaks away.
  • EXISTS Biased-noise quantum error correction theory — repetition codes tailored to a one-sided error channel were worked out in the 2010s.
  • NOT YET Bias-preserving multi-qubit gates — most two-qubit gates leak phase errors into bit errors, which would erase the hardware advantage.
  • PARTIAL Low-loss, high-coherence ancilla qubits — the ancilla's own errors set a floor on how well parity can be measured.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: single cat qubits in dilution refrigerators with bit-flip lifetimes far longer than their phase-flip lifetimes, and small parity-measurement demonstrations — evidence that the bias is real and tunable in hardware.
  2. Next: a few-qubit repetition code that corrects phase flips and shows a logical lifetime longer than its best physical component — first proof that the biased-error architecture saves hardware.
  3. Next: bias-preserving entangling gates and a second, outer code layer for the residual bit flips — a fully protected logical qubit built from far fewer physical elements than an unbiased design would need.
  4. Longer term: arrays of logical qubits with integrated cryogenic control — a route to fault-tolerant machines small enough to build and cool.
