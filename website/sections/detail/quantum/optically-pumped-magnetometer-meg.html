PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Atomic-vapour magnetometers detect the femtotesla magnetic fields of neuronal currents without cryogenics, allowing a helmet the patient can wear and move in. This replaces room-sized cryogenic SQUID arrays in clinical magnetoencephalography.

Category
Quantum sensing
Best use
Wearable brain imaging, epilepsy mapping, neuroscience

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

A rubidium vapour cell is optically pumped and probed by a detuned laser; the spin precession rate responds linearly to the ambient magnetic field, which is read optically.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • OPM sensor — converts femtotesla-scale magnetic fields from neuronal currents into an optical signal. Key parts: rubidium vapour cell, 795 nm vertical-cavity or distributed-feedback laser, optics and polarisation filters, photodetector, heater and temperature controller, internal magnetic shields.
  • Magnetic shielding and field control — suppresses Earth's field and environmental noise so the sensors can work in the spin-exchange relaxation-free (SERF) regime. Key parts: multilayer mu-metal shielded room, active nulling coils, degaussing system, reference magnetometers.
  • Sensor helmet and cabling — holds dozens to hundreds of sensors close to the scalp in a wearable arrangement. Key parts: 3D-printed or adjustable lightweight helmet, sensor mounts, flexible signal cables, optical fibres.
  • Laser distribution — delivers stabilised pump and probe light to every sensor. Key parts: narrow-linewidth laser source, polarisation-maintaining fibre splitters, beam splitters, optical power monitors.
  • Data acquisition and real-time control — digitises sensor outputs, keeps fields stable, and reconstructs brain sources. Key parts: low-noise amplifiers, analog-to-digital converters, real-time field controllers, source-reconstruction software.
  • Subject interface and motion tracking — presents stimuli, records responses, and tracks head position for movement-tolerant recordings. Key parts: stimulus presentation system, response devices, head-tracking markers and cameras.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Fabricate the vapour cells. Glass cells a few millimetres across are filled with a droplet of rubidium and a buffer gas, then sealed. They must hold a controlled alkali-metal vapour for years.
  2. Assemble the sensors. Each sensor packs a small laser, optics, a vapour cell, heaters and a photodetector into a housing roughly the size of a matchbox, with internal shields to reject stray fields.
  3. Build the shielded room. Several layers of mu-metal and aluminium enclose the recording space. Active coils cancel the remaining static field down to a few nanotesla or less, and degaussing coils remove trapped magnetism.
  4. Make the helmet. A participant's head is scanned to design a helmet that places each sensor within a few millimetres of the scalp. The helmet is 3D-printed in lightweight material with pockets for the sensors.
  5. Install and cable the array. Sensors are fitted into the helmet and their optical fibres and electrical cables are routed so they do not tug on the head or add magnetic noise.
  6. Calibrate the system. The room is degaussed, residual fields are nulled, and each sensor's noise floor (often around 5–15 fT/√Hz) and cross-talk are measured before use.
  7. Commission acquisition and software. Real-time field control, head-motion tracking and source-reconstruction pipelines are set up and tested with a phantom — a known magnetic source placed at known positions.
  8. Validate with human subjects. Recordings of evoked responses and spontaneous brain rhythms are compared against cryogenic SQUID MEG and EEG, followed by clinical validation for conditions such as epilepsy.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Alkali-vapour cells and SERF physics — the sensors only reach femtotesla sensitivity when operated near zero field and at high spin-exchange relaxation-free temperature.
  • EXISTS Narrow-linewidth lasers near 795 nm — rubidium must be pumped and probed with light of a precise wavelength and very stable frequency.
  • EXISTS Magnetic shielding and active field cancellation — Earth's field is about 50 µT, roughly a billion times stronger than the brain signals being measured.
  • EXISTS Low-noise electronics and real-time control — signals at the femtotesla level must be digitised without adding noise and with active field stabilisation.
  • PARTIAL Head-motion tracking and compensation — wearable sensors allow movement, but software must track and correct for it so images stay sharp.
  • PARTIAL Clinical validation and regulatory clearance — systems are sold for research and used in pilot clinical studies, but widespread clinical adoption awaits formal approvals.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: commercial OPM-MEG systems used in research and pilot clinical studies, including epilepsy mapping — wearable, cryogen-free brain imaging that can record subjects while they move.
  2. Next: regulatory clearance for clinical use, especially pre-surgical epilepsy evaluation and paediatric imaging — routine hospital deployment where SQUID MEG is too costly or impractical.
  3. Next: integration with EEG and fMRI, plus real-time neurofeedback and natural-movement paradigms — richer multimodal brain mapping and new neuroscience experiments.
  4. Longer term: portable, lower-cost MEG helmets and long-duration monitoring — wider access to functional brain imaging in clinics and research settings.
