9Engineering Breakdown
  • Qubit chip — the processor itself — a millimetre-scale silicon or sapphire die carrying tens to hundreds of qubits. Key parts: superconducting metal films, coplanar waveguide resonators, readout resonators, coupling buses.
  • Josephson junctions — the non-linear element that makes a circuit behave like an artificial atom with distinct energy levels. Key parts: aluminium electrodes, thin aluminium-oxide tunnel barrier.
  • Cryogenics — cools the chip to around 10–20 millikelvin, where the circuits become superconducting and thermal noise disappears. Key parts: dilution refrigerator, pulse tubes and compressors, millikelvin mixing chamber stage.
  • Control and readout electronics — generate the microwave pulses that drive gates and interpret the faint signals coming back. Key parts: room-temperature microwave sources, arbitrary waveform generators, parametric amplifiers, digitizers.
  • Shielding and filtering — keeps stray heat, infrared light and magnetic fields from destroying the quantum state. Key parts: copper and cryoperm shields, attenuators and filters, infrared-blocking absorbers.
  • Wiring and packaging — carries hundreds of control lines from room temperature down to the chip without overheating the fridge. Key parts: superconducting ribbon cables, sample holder, multiplexed readout lines.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Grow and clean the substrate. A high-purity silicon or sapphire wafer is polished and cleaned so films will grow with very few defects — defects cause qubit decoherence.
  2. Deposit superconducting films. Niobium or aluminium is deposited by sputtering or evaporation, then patterned with photolithography and etching to form resonators and wiring.
  3. Fabricate Josephson junctions. A shadow-evaporation technique — often the Dolan bridge — lays down one aluminium electrode, oxidises its surface to form a nanometre-thin barrier, then lays down the second electrode at a different angle.
  4. Dice and mount the chip. The wafer is diced and the chip wire-bonded into a shielded sample package with well-controlled microwave ports.
  5. Wire the cryostat. The package is mounted at the coldest stage of a dilution refrigerator, with carefully attenuated and filtered lines for every qubit and readout channel.
  6. Cool down. Over roughly a day the fridge steps down through several temperature stages to millikelvin, and the metal films become superconducting.
  7. Calibrate the qubits. Each qubit's frequency, pulse amplitudes and readout thresholds are measured and tuned; cross-talk between neighbours is characterised.
  8. Run gates and correct errors. Single- and two-qubit gates are executed, measured, and repeated in error-correcting cycles that combine many physical qubits into one more reliable logical qubit.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Dilution refrigeration — superconducting qubits only behave quantum-mechanically near absolute zero, and the fridge must cool hundreds of wires without adding noise.
  • EXISTS Nanoscale superconducting thin-film fabrication — junction barriers a nanometre thick must be uniform, or qubits vary too much to control.
  • EXISTS Low-noise microwave control and amplification — gates are microwave pulses, and reading a single qubit requires amplifiers near the quantum limit.
  • PARTIAL Quantum error correction — physical qubits are too error-prone alone; many must be combined into one logical qubit, and this has been shown to work better as the code grows only recently.
  • PARTIAL Cryogenic control electronics — thousands of room-temperature cables per chip do not scale; control must move into the fridge.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: cloud-accessible processors with tens to a few hundred noisy physical qubits — early experiments in quantum simulation, optimisation and algorithm research.
  2. Next: error-corrected logical qubits that clearly outperform the best physical ones — longer, more reliable circuits than any single qubit could run.
  3. Next: machines with hundreds of logical qubits, wired and controlled in three dimensions — quantum chemistry and materials problems beyond classical simulation.
  4. Longer term: fault-tolerant processors running millions of gate operations — practical attacks on some cryptography and design of new catalysts and drugs.
