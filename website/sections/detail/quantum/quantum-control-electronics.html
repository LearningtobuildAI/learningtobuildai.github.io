9Engineering Breakdown
  • Pulse synthesis — generates the microwave and baseband waveforms that flip qubit states. Key parts: multi-gigabit-per-second digital-to-analogue converters, analogue up-conversion mixers, local oscillators, variable attenuators.
  • Timing and synchronisation — keeps every channel phase-locked to one shared reference so pulses across the machine align to picoseconds. Key parts: oven-controlled crystal or atomic clock reference, clock distribution tree, trigger and sync buses.
  • Readout acquisition — captures the faint microwave signal reflected from each qubit and extracts its state. Key parts: low-noise amplifiers, analogue-to-digital converters, digital down-conversion and demodulation.
  • Real-time control core — sequences pulses, applies corrections and closes feedback loops without software in the way. Key parts: field-programmable gate array or custom ASIC, waveform memory, numerically controlled oscillators, feedback logic.
  • Cryogenic signal path — carries signals from room temperature down to the qubit chip without adding noise. Key parts: shielded coaxial lines and twisted pairs, attenuators, low-pass and infrared filters, circulators and isolators, cryogenic amplifiers.
  • Cryo-CMOS multiplexers — operates inside the fridge at a few kelvin so one cable serves many qubits. Key parts: cryogenic CMOS ASIC, low-temperature packaging, on-chip multiplexing and memory.
  • Control software — turns gate sequences into calibrated waveforms and manages daily recalibration. Key parts: pulse-level compiler, device calibration and characterisation routines, experiment scheduling.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Specify the signal chain. Set the channel count, bandwidth and noise budget. A transmon qubit near 5 GHz needs pulses tens of nanoseconds long, so each channel must carry hundreds of megahertz of clean instantaneous bandwidth.
  2. Design the digital core. Define an FPGA or ASIC containing the pulse sequencer, oscillators, mixers and decimation filters, with enough waveform memory for long experiments.
  3. Lay out the radio-frequency board. Route controlled-impedance traces, place converters next to the logic, shield aggressively and manage heat so channels do not drift into each other.
  4. Assemble and skew-align. Build the units, then measure and correct the arrival-time difference between channels against a single clock until they agree to picoseconds.
  5. Write the control stack. Compile gate sequences into sample-level waveforms, and build calibration routines that find the right amplitudes, frequencies and phases for each qubit.
  6. Characterise the hardware. Measure phase noise, spurious tones, harmonic distortion, crosstalk and noise floor across temperature and time, since qubit fidelity tracks directly with these numbers.
  7. Fabricate cryo-CMOS versions. Tape out the multiplexer chips at a CMOS foundry, package them for deep-cryogenic operation, and re-characterise at 4 K and below, because transistor thresholds and noise shift markedly when cold.
  8. Integrate with the fridge. Wire the controller through attenuators, filters and circulators down the dilution refrigerator stages, then calibrate the whole chain end to end against the qubit chip.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Superconducting qubit fabrication — microwave-driven qubits set the frequency, bandwidth and fidelity requirements the control hardware must meet.
  • EXISTS High-speed data converters — pulse shaping needs multi-gigabit-per-second DACs and ADCs with enough resolution to keep quantisation noise below qubit error rates.
  • EXISTS Low-phase-noise clock references — qubit phase coherence comes straight from the reference oscillator's stability, and channel alignment depends on one shared clock.
  • EXISTS Dilution refrigeration — both the qubits and any in-fridge control chips need millikelvin and 4 K stages with limited cooling power.
  • PARTIAL Cryogenic CMOS electronics — moving multiplexing and memory into the fridge cuts the wiring bottleneck, but cold transistor behaviour is still being mapped.
  • PARTIAL Real-time decoding logic — error-corrected operation needs measurement and feedback within microseconds, faster than general-purpose software allows.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: room-temperature rack controllers with tens to hundreds of synchronised channels — hands-on control of machines in the hundreds-of-qubits range, with most effort going into calibration.
  2. Next: cryo-CMOS multiplexers running at about 4 K, cutting cable count by roughly an order of magnitude — machines with thousands of qubits whose wiring and heat load stay manageable.
  3. Next: controllers co-designed and co-packaged with qubit chips, with microsecond-latency feedback loops — error-corrected logical qubits, where decoding happens before the quantum state decays.
  4. Longer term: modular, networked control with optical links into the cryostat and standard interfaces between qubit and control vendors — fault-tolerant machines needing millions of physical qubits, where control cost and power, not qubit count alone, decide feasibility.
