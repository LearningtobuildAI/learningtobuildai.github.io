PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Source — injects carriers into the channel by quantum tunnelling rather than by thermal emission. Key parts: heavily doped semiconductor, narrow-bandgap material.
  • Channel — the region between source and drain whose energy barrier is controlled by the gate. Key parts: intrinsic or lightly doped semiconductor, quantum well.
  • Drain — collects the tunnelled carriers and passes them to the interconnect. Key parts: doped region, metal contact.
  • Gate stack — applies an electric field that aligns the energy bands so tunnelling can occur at low voltage. Key parts: high-k dielectric, metal gate.
  • Tunnel barrier — the engineered interface where band-to-band tunnelling takes place. Key parts: abrupt heterojunction, thin insulating or broken-gap layer.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the material system. Select semiconductors with suitable band alignment — for example a broken-gap pair such as InAs and GaSb, or a staggered-gap pair such as germanium on silicon — so that the valence and conduction bands overlap when biased.
  2. Grow the layers. Use molecular beam epitaxy or chemical vapour deposition to deposit ultra-thin layers with atomic precision on a silicon or III-V substrate.
  3. Dope the source. Introduce very high concentrations of dopants, by ion implantation or in-situ during growth, to create a degenerate source region.
  4. Pattern the device. Use electron-beam or extreme-ultraviolet lithography to define the source, channel and gate areas.
  5. Deposit the gate stack. Grow a high-k dielectric by atomic layer deposition, then deposit a metal gate on top.
  6. Form contacts. Deposit metals such as nickel or titanium and anneal to make low-resistance contacts to the source and drain.
  7. Isolate and interconnect. Etch isolation trenches, fill them with oxide, and build multi-level metal wiring to connect the transistors.
  8. Characterise. Measure current–voltage curves, subthreshold swing and on/off ratio to verify that tunnelling switches the device below the thermal limit.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-quality heteroepitaxy — growing defect-free III-V or germanium layers directly on silicon is difficult and defect states degrade tunnelling.
  • EXISTS Atomic layer deposition of high-k dielectrics — the gate stack must be thin, uniform and free of traps to control the tunnel barrier precisely.
  • PARTIAL Ultra-shallow doping and activation — abrupt, highly doped junctions are needed for efficient band-to-band tunnelling.
  • PARTIAL Two-dimensional semiconductors — materials such as molybdenum disulfide or black phosphorus can form atomically sharp junctions with no dangling bonds.
  • PARTIAL Low-resistance contacts — contact resistance often dominates the total resistance and masks the low-voltage switching benefit.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: silicon-based tunnelling transistors show sub-60 mV/decade switching in laboratories, but only at very low currents — proof that the thermal limit of conventional transistors can be beaten.
  2. Next: III-V heterojunction devices reach higher on-currents while keeping steep switching — performance close to that needed for logic circuits.
  3. Next: two-dimensional material tunnelling transistors with abrupt, defect-free junctions — scalable devices that can be stacked in three dimensions.
  4. Longer term: commercial ultra-low-power processors — battery-limited and energy-harvesting electronics that run for years without replacement.
