4How It Works

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Drill and sample core — interrogates rock up to 2 m beneath the surface, below the layer where radiation and oxidants destroy organic molecules. Key parts: rotary-percussive drill string, core bit, sample transfer mechanism.
  • Sample handling and sealed laboratory — moves each core without contamination into instruments that analyse it internally. Key parts: crushing and powdering mill, sample ovens, sealed distribution carousel.
  • Pasteur analytical instruments — detects organic molecules and characterises minerals and isotopes. Key parts: gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer, Raman laser spectrometer, infrared imaging spectrometer, close-up camera and microscope.
  • Mobility and chassis — carries the drill and laboratory across rough terrain to chosen drill sites. Key parts: six or more wheels with independent steering, rocker-bogie style suspension, solar arrays, body structure.
  • Navigation and subsurface sensing — decides where to drive and where to drill, largely without help from Earth. Key parts: navigation and hazard cameras, ground-penetrating radar, onboard autonomy software.
  • Thermal and power — keeps electronics alive through Martian nights and manages heat from the drill. Key parts: solar arrays and batteries, radioisotope heater units, thermal insulation and louvers.
  • Communications and lander interface — relays commands and data and provides the descent and landing platform. Key parts: UHF relay radio to an orbiter, X-band direct link, descent module, parachutes and retrorockets.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the rover and its instrument suite. Engineers fix the mass, power and volume budget across a drill, a sealed laboratory and a mobility system that all have to fit inside a single lander.
  2. Fabricate the structure and mobility system. The chassis and suspension are built and the wheels and drive motors mated to it.
  3. Build the drill. A rotary-percussive mechanism, proven on Earth in Mars-analogue rock, is assembled so it can extract and retain a core for transfer.
  4. Integrate the analytical laboratory. The sealed sample path — mill, ovens and instrument inlet — is assembled and cleaned to remove terrestrial organic contamination.
  5. Assemble instruments. Each spectrometer and camera is built, calibrated against known reference materials and mounted on the rover.
  6. Environmental testing. The rover goes through thermal-vacuum, vibration and acoustic testing that simulates launch and the Martian surface; the drill is rehearsed in a low-pressure chamber.
  7. Build the lander and descent system. A European landing platform, parachutes and retro-rockets are assembled and qualified for the thin Martian atmosphere.
  8. Launch, cruise and land. The rover would fly on a launcher arranged with a partner agency, coast to Mars and touch down at Oxia Planum, a clay-rich plain.
  9. Drill and analyse. The rover drives to targets picked from orbit and from its own ground-penetrating radar, drills a core, powders it and delivers it to the sealed instruments for a result sent back over the relay link.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Deep rotary-percussive drilling — a 2 m drill has been built and tested on Earth in Mars-like rock, but no rover has drilled that deeply on Mars.
  • PARTIAL Ultra-clean sample handling — organic molecules at part-per-billion levels are easy to fake with contamination from the spacecraft itself, so every surface must be cleaned and verified.
  • EXISTS Raman and mass spectrometry miniaturised for space — flight versions of these instruments have already operated on Mars.
  • EXISTS Autonomous surface navigation — driving and target selection without human real-time control has been demonstrated by earlier rovers.
  • EXISTS Entry, descent and landing in the thin atmosphere — large parachutes plus powered descent are a solved, if difficult, problem for medium-sized rovers.
  • EXISTS Long-lived surface power — solar arrays with radioisotope heaters have sustained rovers through many Martian winters.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: rover assembled and tested, awaiting a launch opportunity; originally planned for 2022, now targeted at 2028 — a proven, drilled-and-analysed-in-place subsurface mission.
  2. Next: launch and landing on the clay-rich plain at Oxia Planum — access to ancient sediments most likely to hold preserved organic matter.
  3. Next: first deep cores analysed inside a sealed laboratory on Mars — a decisive test of whether complex organic chemistry survived below the oxidised surface.
  4. Longer term: sample return and precursor work for human missions — laboratory-grade analysis on Earth and knowledge of subsurface resources and hazards.
