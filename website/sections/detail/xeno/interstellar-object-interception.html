1Definition

Bodies such as 1I/Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov are unmodified remnants of other planetary systems, but they are discovered only weeks before passing perihelion. A pre-positioned interceptor could reach a newly found object and measure its composition directly.

4How It Works

A spacecraft parked in a low-energy orbit uses chemical or solar-electric propulsion to fly past the object, trading launch mass against arrival speed and available observation time.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Detection and tracking network — find incoming interstellar objects early enough to plan an intercept. Key parts: wide-field survey telescopes, orbit-determination software, alert and follow-up systems.
  • Interceptor spacecraft bus — a pre-positioned platform that can loiter for years then execute a high-speed flyby. Key parts: structural frame, avionics and flight computer, power system (solar arrays or radioisotope generator), thermal control.
  • Propulsion system — provide the large velocity change needed to reach an object moving fast relative to the Sun. Key parts: chemical main engine, solar-electric ion thrusters, propellant tanks, gravity-assist planning.
  • Science instrument suite — measure composition, structure and dust environment at encounter. Key parts: visible and infrared cameras, imaging spectrometer, mass spectrometer, dust detector and impact sensors.
  • Communications and navigation — return data and steer the spacecraft autonomously at high closing speed. Key parts: high-gain antenna, deep-space transponder, star trackers and sun sensors, autonomous navigation software.
  • Encounter sub-probes — increase chances of a close look by releasing small probes before flyby. Key parts: small deployable probes, independent cameras and spectrometers.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a waiting strategy. Decide where to park the interceptor — for example, a stable orbit at the Sun–Earth L2 point or a heliocentric orbit — so it can reach a range of incoming objects with limited fuel.
  2. Design the spacecraft. Trade propulsion type against target reach; a high-speed flyby needs tens of kilometres per second of velocity change, which may require chemical stages plus gravity assists.
  3. Build and test the bus. Assemble the structure, avionics, power and thermal systems, then test in thermal-vacuum and vibration facilities to survive launch and deep space.
  4. Integrate the instruments. Mount cameras, spectrometers and dust detectors, and calibrate them before launch.
  5. Launch and park. Launch on a rocket to the waiting orbit; the spacecraft enters a low-activity mode, keeping systems alive for years.
  6. Detect a target. Ground survey telescopes discover a new interstellar object and quickly determine its orbit; an alert triggers the interceptor.
  7. Compute and execute the intercept. Within days, calculate a trajectory that intercepts the object, then fire engines or begin ion-thrusting, possibly using a planetary flyby for extra speed.
  8. Flyby and return data. At encounter, instruments take rapid measurements and the spacecraft transmits them back to Earth over weeks or months.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Wide-field survey telescopes — they exist, but an interstellar object is usually found only weeks before it is closest to the Sun, leaving little time to react.
  • PARTIAL High-delta-v propulsion — ion engines give high efficiency but low thrust; chemical rockets give thrust but need large tanks to reach the tens of km/s required.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous navigation and rapid trajectory design — a spacecraft must target a fast-moving object with little ground intervention, and the orbit must be computed within days.
  • EXISTS Long-duration deep-space power — solar arrays work near the Sun; radioisotope generators power missions far out, as used by past outer-planet probes.
  • EXISTS High-speed data downlink — deep-space antennas can receive data, but the encounter is brief and data rates are low.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: survey telescopes discover interstellar objects (1I/Oumuamua in 2017, 2I/Borisov in 2019) but no spacecraft can reach them. — catalogs of orbits and growing evidence that such objects are common.
  2. Next: a European mission (Comet Interceptor) plans to wait at L2 for a comet, with the possibility of an interstellar object, but it will not intercept a specific known object. — experience in parking and rapid targeting.
  3. Next: dedicated interceptor studies (such as Project Lyra) model high-speed flybys using Jupiter gravity assists. — concrete mission designs and propulsion requirements.
  4. Longer term: pre-positioned interceptors ready for launch on short notice, perhaps using solar sails or nuclear thermal propulsion. — direct composition measurements of material from other planetary systems.
