PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Low-frequency antenna array — collects the faint metre-wave signal; the whole technique only works below roughly 200 MHz, where the emission is bright enough and the sky is quiet. Key parts: dipole or tile elements, station of tens to hundreds of elements, ground plane and low-noise amplifiers.
  • Receiver and digitiser — turns each antenna's analogue signal into sampled digital data across tens of megahertz of bandwidth. Key parts: analogue bandpass filters, high-speed ADCs.
  • Beamformer and correlator — combines the elements into one or more pointed beams: one on the target star, others pointed nearby to measure the background. Key parts: per-station beamformers, central correlator.
  • RFI monitoring and excision — removes human-made interference, which at these frequencies is far stronger than any astrophysical signal. Key parts: reference antennas, flagging software, ionospheric calibration.
  • Spectrometer and polarimeter — resolves the narrow emission band and measures its circular polarisation. Key parts: high spectral-resolution channeliser, long coherent integration.
  • Detection and vetting pipeline — searches stacked spectra for drifting, polarised narrowband features and filters out false positives. Key parts: drift-rate search, multi-epoch cross-checks.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select target systems. Theory predicts the strongest signals from close-in giant planets orbiting stars with strong magnetic fields and dense stellar winds.
  2. Build or schedule the array. At least a few hundred dipoles spread over hundreds of metres to a kilometre, on a radio-quiet site, tuned for roughly 10–200 MHz.
  3. Observe for hours. Several hours of integration per system, repeated over multiple nights and epochs to beat down noise and to distinguish a persistent source from a transient.
  4. Form simultaneous beams. One beam tracks the star while reference beams track sky positions a few degrees away and an empty patch, all at the same frequency; this measures the local confusion limit.
  5. Excise interference. Flag channels contaminated by FM, digital audio, aircraft and lightning, then re-derive the bandpass and correct for the ionosphere.
  6. Search for narrowband drifting features. Emission is expected at a cyclotron frequency set by the planet's magnetic field, strongly circularly polarised and drifting slowly in frequency as the field sweeps past the observer.
  7. Vet candidates. Require the signal to track the star's sidereal position, to persist across epochs, to be absent in the reference beams, and to have a polarisation signature no known interference source produces.
  8. Interpret magnetism. Convert the detected frequency to a magnetic field strength at the emission site and compare with models of the star–planet interaction and of atmospheric escape.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Metre-wave radio arrays — LOFAR, NenuFAR, the upgraded GMRT and the Murchison Widefield Array already cover 10–300 MHz; SKA-Low extends this with far greater sensitivity.
  • EXISTS Cyclotron maser emission theory — the same mechanism is measured from Jupiter's interaction with its moon Io, giving the template for what to look for.
  • PARTIAL Radio-quiet sites and interference excision — at these frequencies interference can outshine the target by orders of magnitude, so sensitivity is limited by flagging quality as much as by dish area.
  • PARTIAL Coherent long integrations and beamforming — signals are weak and may drift, so hours of phase-coherent averaging and precise ionospheric calibration are needed.
  • PARTIAL Stellar wind and magnetosphere models — predictions of how much power a star–planet interaction radiates span several orders of magnitude, which sets how long a search must be.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: surveys of nearby systems with existing metre-wave arrays, yielding mostly non-detections plus a handful of contested candidates including a red-dwarf system — first observational limits on exoplanet magnetic fields and a growing catalogue of the best targets.
  2. Next: SKA-Low and upgraded low-frequency arrays reaching microjansky sensitivity over wide fields — faint enough to test the full range of predicted emission strengths for hundreds of nearby stars, not just the most optimistic few.
  3. Next: confirmed detections cross-matched with other magnetospheric tracers such as ultraviolet aurorae and star–planet interaction in stellar chromospheric lines — a calibrated method for measuring exoplanet magnetic fields from the ground.
  4. Longer term: routine magnetic-field measurements for planets in habitable zones, feeding atmospheric-escape and habitability models — knowledge of which planets keep their atmospheres and water over billions of years.
