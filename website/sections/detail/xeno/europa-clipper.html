PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

NASA spacecraft launched in October 2024 to study Jupiter's moon Europa, which hides a salt-water ocean beneath its ice. It arrives in 2030 and will judge whether that ocean could be habitable.

4How It Works

Dozens of close flybys with ice-penetrating radar, a mass spectrometer, a dust analyser and cameras map the ice shell and sample material thrown up near the surface.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Power system — generates electricity at Jupiter, where sunlight is only about 4% as strong as at Earth. Key parts: two five-panel solar arrays, more than 30 m tip to tip when deployed, radiation-hardened triple-junction solar cells, batteries for eclipse and peak loads, power electronics.
  • Radiation vault and avionics — keeps the spacecraft's brain alive through repeated passes through Jupiter's radiation belts. Key parts: thick-walled metal vault, flight computers and memory, power switches, avionics for pointing and instrument control.
  • Science payload — nine instruments that together judge whether Europa's ocean could support life. Key parts: EIS wide- and narrow-angle cameras, MISE infrared spectrometer, E-THEMIS thermal imager, MASPEX and SUDA for sampling gas and dust, ECM magnetometer and PIMS plasma instrument, radio science for gravity measurements.
  • REASON radar booms — ice-penetrating radar that sounds the shell and looks for the ocean below it. Key parts: two long folded antennas, about 17.6 m each, dual-frequency transmitter and receiver, deployment mechanisms.
  • Propulsion and thermal control — flies the gravity-assist trajectory, inserts into Jupiter orbit and keeps fuel and instruments at working temperature. Key parts: hydrazine thrusters, propellant tanks, multi-layer insulation and louvres, heaters and thermostats.
  • Communications and navigation — points the antenna at Earth and knows where it is in deep space. Key parts: high-gain dish antenna, radio transponder, star trackers and Sun sensors.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the mission around flybys, not orbit. Engineers choose to orbit Jupiter and dive past Europa roughly 49 times, so the spacecraft spends as little time as possible inside the worst radiation.
  2. Build the spacecraft body. The bus, vault and structural frame are assembled and its electronics tested before anything delicate goes in.
  3. Make and fold the solar arrays. Large panels of radiation-tolerant solar cells are built and then folded tight against the body for launch; at Jupiter they will yield only a few hundred watts.
  4. Fit the radar antennas. The two long REASON booms are stowed in their launch configuration, since they cannot be repaired or unfolded by hand once in space.
  5. Integrate and test. Instruments are mounted, then the whole spacecraft goes through vibration, thermal-vacuum and electromagnetic testing to prove it can survive launch and the cold of cruise.
  6. Launch. A Falcon Heavy lifted off from Kennedy Space Center on 14 October 2024, throwing the roughly 6,000 kg spacecraft onto a trajectory that will swing past Mars in March 2025 and Earth in December 2026.
  7. Cruise and check out. Over five and a half years the team flies the spacecraft, keeps its propellant lines and instruments warm, and rehearses the flyby sequences.
  8. Arrive and fly by. Jupiter orbit insertion in April 2030 begins a four-year prime mission of close passes — some as low as 25 km above the ice — returning radar, magnetic, spectral and dust data.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Solar power in the outer solar system — Jupiter is far too dim for older missions, but modern solar arrays made solar-powered Jupiter exploration practical.
  • EXISTS Heavy-lift launch with escape energy — the spacecraft is large and heavy, and needs to reach Jupiter without a nuclear power source.
  • EXISTS Radiation-tolerant electronics — Jupiter's belts would destroy ordinary computers within hours of the planned flybys.
  • EXISTS Deep Space Network tracking — data comes back as a faint radio signal over hundreds of millions of kilometres.
  • EXISTS Ice-penetrating radar — sounding radar flown at Mars and used on Earth's ice sheets shows the technique works, though Europa's shell is a harder target.
  • PARTIAL Trace-gas and dust mass spectrometry — detecting organic molecules at very low concentrations has been done at Saturn and comets, but the sensitivity needed to detect life-related chemistry is at the edge of what has flown.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: cruise phase with gravity assists from Mars (2025) and Earth (2026) — a low-cost way to reach Jupiter without a massive launch vehicle.
  2. 2030: Jupiter orbit insertion and the first Europa flybys — close-up radar, magnetic and imaging data from a moon never seen at this resolution.
  3. 2030–2034: roughly 49 flybys measuring ice-shell thickness, ocean salinity and surface chemistry — a defensible answer on whether Europa's ocean is habitable.
  4. Beyond: results feeding a possible Europa lander and other ocean-world missions — the step from assessing habitability to directly sampling the ice and, eventually, searching for life itself.
