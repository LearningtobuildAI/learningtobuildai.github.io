PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Photometric instrument — records the tiny dip in starlight as a planet crosses its star. Key parts: space telescope optics, CCD or CMOS detector, fine guidance sensors for stable pointing.
  • Data reduction pipeline — turns raw images into clean light curves. Key parts: calibration frames, aperture photometry, systematics correction (pointing drift, thermal effects).
  • Transit fitting — measures the centre time and duration of each transit precisely. Key parts: limb-darkening models, Bayesian parameter estimation, uncertainty quantification.
  • Signal search — looks for periodic offsets in transit times or durations caused by a moon. Key parts: periodograms and wavelets, model comparison statistics, injection-recovery tests.
  • False-positive vetting — rules out other explanations for the signal. Key parts: stellar activity analysis, background eclipsing binary checks, dynamical stability checks.
  • Follow-up observations — uses other telescopes to confirm or refute a candidate. Key parts: scheduling and target-of-opportunity triggers, multi-wavelength photometry, comparison with radial-velocity data.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose target systems. Select transiting planets with many recorded transits and long observation baselines, because a moon's signal accumulates over time.
  2. Obtain high-precision photometry. Collect continuous, high-cadence light curves from space telescopes to reach the parts-per-million precision needed.
  3. Correct instrument and stellar noise. Remove spacecraft pointing drifts, detector systematics and the star's own brightness variations.
  4. Measure transit times and durations. Fit each transit to find its mid-point and length, then compile the series of variations.
  5. Search for periodic signals. Apply periodograms or Bayesian model comparison to detect small, repeating offsets in timing or duration.
  6. Test against false positives. Run injection-recovery tests and check whether stellar activity, a second planet, or a background binary could mimic the signal.
  7. Model the planet–moon dynamics. Simulate the gravitational interaction to see if a stable moon orbit reproduces the observed variations.
  8. Follow up with other telescopes. Schedule additional transits with Hubble, JWST or CHEOPS to see if the signal repeats and holds up.
  9. Validate or set limits. Combine all evidence to either claim a detection or publish an upper limit on moon size in that system.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Space-based photometry at parts-per-million precision — instruments like Kepler, TESS and CHEOPS can detect the tiny brightness changes involved.
  • EXISTS Long, uninterrupted observation baselines — transit timing variations grow over many orbits, so years of data are needed.
  • EXISTS Dynamical modelling of planet–moon systems — N-body simulations predict which signals are physically possible and stable.
  • PARTIAL Understanding of astrophysical false positives — several stellar and instrumental effects can imitate transit timing signals.
  • PARTIAL Next-generation long-stare telescopes — PLATO and JWST offer the precision and cadence to confirm candidates, but are not yet fully exploited.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: Reanalysis of Kepler and TESS data; candidate signals around gas giants such as Kepler-1625b and Kepler-1708b remain disputed — proven methodology and published upper limits.
  2. Next: JWST and CHEOPS follow-up of the best candidate systems — confirmation or refutation of existing exomoon claims.
  3. Next: PLATO's long, uninterrupted stares at bright stars — a systematic survey capable of finding the first confirmed exomoon.
  4. Longer term: Dedicated space telescopes or extremely large ground observatories with high-contrast imaging — direct study of exomoons and their atmospheres.
