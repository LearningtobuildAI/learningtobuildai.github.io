9Engineering Breakdown
  • Dual-view telescope — images two patches of sky about 106° apart at the same instant on one shared focal plane, so small drifts in pointing cancel out of the measurement. Key parts: silicon-carbide mirrors, rigid optical bench, common focal plane assembly.
  • Focal plane — detects the stars; a wide, faint strip finds them, a large central array measures their positions, and flanking instruments measure colour and radial velocity. Key parts: large CCD mosaic (about a billion pixels), sky-mapper detection strips, blue and red spectro-photometers, radial-velocity spectrometer.
  • Scanning attitude control — spins the spacecraft slowly and precesses the spin axis so the whole sky is swept repeatedly in a fixed pattern. Key parts: cold-gas micro-thrusters, star trackers, reaction-less pointing logic.
  • Thermally stable structure — keeps the optics aligned to nanometres, because a wobble in the telescope looks exactly like a wobble in a star. Key parts: silicon-carbide structural frame, sunshield, passive and active thermal control.
  • Downlink and reduction pipeline — turns billions of individual CCD transits into a single self-consistent catalogue. Key parts: onboard storage, ground stations, global iterative solver, relativistic and geometric models.
  • Reference frame and validation — defines the zero point of the sky and checks the result against independent data. Key parts: quasar-based celestial reference frame, ground-based radial-velocity spectrographs, VLBI tie.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Decide the error budget. Microarcsecond positions demand a single focal plane shared by both telescope views, a thermally inert structure, and a scan pattern that revisits every star at many angles.
  2. Fabricate optics and bench. Mirrors and the structural frame are machined from silicon carbide, polished, and assembled into a bench whose dimensions must not drift.
  3. Build the focal plane. A mosaic of large CCDs is bonded into a plane of roughly a billion pixels, with wide detection strips feeding the high-precision astrometric array.
  4. Integrate and thermal-vacuum test. The payload is assembled, then run end to end in a thermal-vacuum chamber so its optical distortion can be measured and calibrated before launch.
  5. Launch and cruise to L2. The observatory is placed in a Lissajous orbit around the Sun–Earth L2 point, roughly 1.5 million km from Earth, giving a stable thermal and viewing environment.
  6. Commission and calibrate. The 106° basic angle and the field distortion are measured to microarcsecond level using dense star fields, and the scan pattern is trimmed.
  7. Downlink and solve. Each day's transits are sent to the ground; a global iterative solution fits position, parallax, proper motion and orbital wobble simultaneously across all observations to date.
  8. Confirm any planets. A candidate wobble is only accepted after ground-based radial-velocity spectroscopy rules out a stellar companion and pins down the mass.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Large-format CCD mosaics and low-noise readout — a census needs billions of faint stars measured many times, so it needs huge detectors that work at high cadence.
  • EXISTS Nanometre-stable structures and thermal control — the instrument must be more stable than the signal it is trying to detect.
  • EXISTS Micro-thruster attitude control — the spacecraft must be pointed and spun without the jitter that reaction wheels or thruster firings would inject.
  • EXISTS Global iterative astrometric solution — positions and orbits cannot be solved star by star; every observation constrains every other, needing very large computing campaigns.
  • EXISTS Precise ground-based radial-velocity spectrographs — astrometry alone gives a mass times an unknown inclination, so an independent measurement is needed to confirm and weigh a planet.
  • EXISTS A quasar-defined celestial reference frame — parallaxes and proper motions are meaningless without a fixed, non-rotating frame to measure them against.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: final data releases from the current whole-sky astrometric mission, with improved proper motions and a vetted list of astrometric planet candidates — a common distance and mass scale for hundreds of millions of stars, plus the first population of planets found by wobble rather than by dimming or velocity.
  2. Next: combining astrometry with radial velocity, direct imaging and microlensing for the same systems — true masses and full orbits instead of minimum masses, especially for the wide-orbit giants that other methods miss.
  3. Next: a proposed dedicated astrometric exoplanet mission, or a near-infrared successor, aiming for tens of microarcseconds or better — sensitivity to planets a few times the mass of Jupiter at orbits of several years, and to the fainter, cooler stars where a distance scale is hardest to build.
  4. Longer term: a systematic census of the solar neighbourhood with consistent true masses — direct tests of planet-formation models, because the giant planets that shape a system's architecture are exactly the ones astrometry sees best.
