1Definition

Polarised light from an exoplanet carries information about cloud and haze particles that ordinary spectroscopy struggles to separate from the host star's signal and from instrumental systematics.

4How It Works

A high-contrast polarimeter measures the small polarised fraction of the combined star-planet light as the planet moves through its orbit; the polarisation phase curve constrains droplet size, particle shape and cloud altitude.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Telescope and adaptive optics — delivers a stable, diffraction-limited image so faint planet light is not buried in atmospheric blur. Key parts: 8–10 m class telescope, deformable mirror, wavefront sensor.
  • Coronagraph and starlight suppression — blocks the bulk of the host star's light before it reaches the polarimeter. Key parts: apodiser or vortex mask, Lyot stop, pointing and jitter control.
  • Polarimetry module — splits the beam into two orthogonal polarisation states and encodes the Stokes parameters. Key parts: achromatic half-wave plate on a precision rotation stage, Wollaston prism or polarising beam-splitter, calibration unit injecting known polarisation states.
  • Spectrograph — disperses the planet's light at a resolving power of a few hundred to a few thousand so cloud features are separable across wavelength. Key parts: grism or prism, slit or integral-field lenslets.
  • Detector — records both polarisation beams simultaneously with low noise and short exposure times. Key parts: near-infrared or visible detector array, fast readout electronics.
  • Data pipeline and scattering model — converts raw frames into polarisation spectra and fits them to cloud physics. Key parts: Stokes reduction software, instrumental-polarisation calibration tables, radiative-transfer scattering code.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the target and bandpass. Choose a giant planet on a wide, well-separated orbit so it clears the inner working angle, and a wavelength range where reflected starlight competes least with the star's own colour.
  2. Design the optical train. Lay out adaptive optics, coronagraph and polarimeter so the polarising optics sit downstream of the coronagraph, where scattered starlight is already reduced.
  3. Fabricate the polarimeter. Build an achromatic retarder stack that keeps a half-wave shift across the whole bandpass, pair it with a beam-splitter, and mount both on a stage that can index accurately to a fraction of a degree.
  4. Integrate with the high-contrast imager. Insert the module into an existing extreme-adaptive-optics instrument, keeping the two polarisation beams on the detector simultaneously so atmospheric and telescope fluctuations cancel.
  5. Calibrate instrumental polarisation. Observe unpolarised standard stars to map the polarisation the telescope mirrors impose, and polarised standards to fix the angle offset and efficiency; repeat regularly because mirror coatings age.
  6. Observe a polarisation sequence. Take rapid exposures with the waveplate stepped through several angles, nodding the star between the two beams, and repeat across the planet's orbital phase or across the transit.
  7. Reduce to Stokes spectra. Subtract the two beams to cancel unpolarised starlight, combine waveplate angles into Stokes Q and U, then de-rotate by the parallactic angle and bin into a spectrum.
  8. Fit cloud and haze parameters. Compare the polarisation spectrum and its phase dependence against radiative-transfer models that vary particle size, composition, shape and cloud-top pressure.
  9. Cross-check against intensity spectra. Combine the polarimetric result with ordinary spectroscopy, since a liquid-surface glint and a thin haze can look similar in intensity alone but differ strongly in polarisation.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Extreme adaptive optics and coronagraphy — without roughly 10⁻⁵ starlight suppression the planet's few-percent polarisation is swamped.
  • EXISTS Achromatic retarders and high-extinction polarisers — the polarisation state must stay accurately defined across the bandpass or the spectrum is corrupted.
  • PARTIAL Instrumental-polarisation calibration at the 10⁻⁵ level — mirror-induced polarisation currently limits how faint a polarised signal can be trusted.
  • PARTIAL Polarised radiative-transfer models of planetary atmospheres — Rayleigh and Mie scattering are well understood, but real haze particles are irregular and their scattering matrices are poorly constrained.
  • PARTIAL Large filled-aperture telescopes with polarimetric modes — collecting area sets how bright a target must be; only a handful of facilities offer the combination.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: polarimetric differential imaging routinely maps scattered light in circumstellar disks with current high-contrast instruments — proven hardware and reduction techniques that can be pointed at planets.
  2. Next: dedicated polarimetric modes with rigorous calibration reach the brightest hot Jupiters and young, widely separated giants — the first robust polarisation spectra of an exoplanet atmosphere.
  3. Next: 30 m class telescopes add collecting area and spectral resolution — polarisation phase curves for a small sample of planets, separating cloud decks from clear atmospheres.
  4. Longer term: space-based coronagraphs combine high-resolution spectroscopy with polarimetry on temperate planets — discriminating hazes, clouds and specular glints from liquid surfaces, which sharpens the interpretation of any biosignature.
