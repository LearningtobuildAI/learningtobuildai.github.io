PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Nanopore sequencers read informational polymers base by base as they thread a nanoscale pore, offering a compact, low-power route to sequence any genetic alphabet found off Earth rather than only the four bases used on Earth.

4How It Works

An applied voltage drives a strand through a protein or solid-state pore; each base produces a characteristic current blockade that is decoded in real time, and the same physical principle can in theory read non-terrestrial polymer chemistries.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Nanopore sensor — the transducer — a hole a few nanometres wide that a polymer threads through, one unit at a time. Key parts: protein pore (e.g. alpha-haemolysin, MspA) or solid-state pore in silicon nitride or graphene, insulating membrane or chip surface holding the pore, electrolyte on both sides.
  • Capture and ratcheting — gets a strand to the pore mouth and then moves it step by step rather than letting it shoot through. Key parts: processive motor enzyme, leader adaptor chemistry, electrokinetic capture electrodes.
  • Sample preparation — turns a soil, ice or brine sample into a clean polymer solution without assuming earthly biochemistry. Key parts: extraction and desalting, filtering of competing biopolymers, chemistry that binds adaptors to unknown backbone chemistries.
  • Readout electronics — applies the driving voltage and records the tiny current changes as each unit passes. Key parts: low-noise current amplifier, application-specific integrated circuit with one channel per pore, digitising and data compression.
  • Decoding and autonomy — turns raw current traces into sequences and decides what is worth keeping. Key parts: machine-learned signal models, adaptive sampling that rejects uninformative reads, onboard decision software.
  • Flight enclosure — keeps the chemistry alive through launch, vacuum, radiation and temperature swings. Key parts: sealed fluidic cartridge, reagent storage, thermal control and radiation shielding.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose or engineer the pore. Protein pores are tuned by directed evolution so that different chemical units produce distinguishable current blockades; solid-state pores are drilled by focused electron beams for robustness and for geometries a protein cannot provide.
  2. Fabricate the sensor array. Pores are inserted into lipid membranes or droplet interfaces formed directly on top of a chip, so that each microwell holds one pore connected to one electronic channel.
  3. Attach the motor and adaptors. A processive enzyme is coupled to the strand so that the polymer is pulled through at a controlled rate; because unknown polymers may not accept standard ligation chemistry, this step is the hardest to generalise.
  4. Prepare the sample. Polymers are extracted, salts and small molecules removed, and the solution buffered so that the pore stays open — critical when the starting material is a few grams of Martian regolith or an ice-melt brine.
  5. Assemble the instrument. Sensor chip, fluidics, reagent packs and readout board are integrated into a low-mass, low-power package comparable to a handheld sequencer.
  6. Train the decoder. Known polymers of many lengths and chemistries are run to build signal models; candidate non-terrestrial alphabets are handled as unknown classes rather than forced into A, C, G and T.
  7. Validate terrestrially. The instrument is tested on extreme environments — permafrost, hypersaline lakes, deep-sea sediments — and exposed to simulated Martian and icy-moon conditions.
  8. Fly and prove. A flight unit would first run on orbit or on a small lander, with results compared against ground labs to show that reading works outside Earth's biosphere.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Commercial nanopore sequencing — handheld devices already read DNA and RNA in the field and on the International Space Station, proving the sensing principle is spaceworthy.
  • PARTIAL Solid-state nanopore fabrication — inorganic pores survive conditions that destroy lipid bilayers, but making them with consistent sub-nanometre size is still laboratory work.
  • NOT YET Processive enzymes for non-DNA polymers — every motor protein in use evolved for earthly nucleic acids; without an equivalent for another backbone, strands cross the pore too fast to read.
  • PARTIAL Radiation-tolerant, low-noise electronics — spacecraft electronics exist, but combining very low current noise with radiation hardness in one small chip is not routine.
  • NOT YET Decoding of unknown alphabets — current basecallers are trained on four letters of one chemistry; a genuinely alien polymer would need unsupervised interpretation first.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: field sequencing on Earth and in orbit, plus instrument concepts such as MIT's nanopore-based life-detection programme — confidence that pore-based reading survives space conditions.
  2. Next: bench demonstration of single-unit discrimination in a non-canonical nucleic acid or synthetic sequence-defined polymer — a credible claim that the method is not restricted to Earth biochemistry.
  3. Next: a self-contained prototype tested in Mars and ocean-world analogues, with autonomous sample prep — readiness for a lander or plume-flyby payload.
  4. Longer term: an in-situ instrument on a planetary mission feeding sample-return triage — the ability to say whether a sample contains information-bearing polymers before deciding what to bring home.
