9Engineering Breakdown
  • Photometric data source — provides the ultra-precise, continuous light curves needed to see a moon or ring signal. Key parts: space telescope with stable pointing (e.g. Kepler, TESS), CCD detectors, raw pixel data pipeline.
  • Systematics correction — removes instrumental drift and astrophysical noise that would otherwise mimic a weak signal. Key parts: detrending algorithms (e.g. cotrending bases, Gaussian processes), auxiliary engineering and housekeeping data, starspot and variability models.
  • Transit modelling — establishes the planet's baseline transit shape and timing so anomalies can be measured. Key parts: transit shape models, limb-darkening coefficients, orbital parameter fitting.
  • Anomaly search — looks for timing shifts, depth changes, or out-of-transit dips caused by a moon or ring system. Key parts: transit timing variation (TTV) analysis, periodogram and wavelet tools, Bayesian model comparison.
  • Follow-up and false-positive rejection — checks that a candidate is not caused by a background eclipsing binary, starspot, or instrument glitch. Key parts: ground-based photometry, high-resolution imaging, spectroscopic vetting.
  • Theoretical forward models — predicts what a real exomoon or ring signal should look like for comparison with data. Key parts: N-body orbital dynamics, ring scattering and occultation models, instrument noise simulations.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a high-signal target. Select a transiting planet with deep, frequent transits and low stellar noise, ideally from Kepler or TESS data.
  2. Extract a clean light curve. Convert raw spacecraft pixels into a brightness time series, then correct for pointing drift and other systematic effects.
  3. Measure precise transit times. Fit each individual transit to get its mid-time, depth, and duration, building a timeline of the planet's passages.
  4. Search for timing variations. Look for periodic shifts in transit times that a moon's gravitational tug would impose on the planet.
  5. Search for out-of-transit features. Examine the light curve between transits for shallow, repeating dips from a ring system or a moon crossing the star.
  6. Fit competing models. Compare planet-only, planet-plus-moon, and planet-plus-ring models using Bayesian evidence to see which is favoured.
  7. Rule out false positives. Test alternative explanations such as background eclipsing binaries, starspots, or instrumental artefacts.
  8. Seek independent confirmation. Observe the target with other telescopes at predicted times to see if the signal repeats.
  9. Publish and await replication. Report the candidate with full data and models; the field requires independent replication before a detection is accepted.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Space-based ultra-precise photometry — Kepler and TESS provide the needed precision for planet transits, but the signal from a small exomoon sits at or below their noise floor.
  • PARTIAL Long, uninterrupted observation baselines — Kepler gave years of continuous data; TESS has gaps, and many TTV signals need many years to become clear.
  • EXISTS Bayesian inference and systematics modelling — the statistical tools exist and are widely used, but they must be applied very carefully to avoid false detections.
  • EXISTS Follow-up observatories — Hubble and JWST can confirm candidates, but available time is limited and scheduling is competitive.
  • PARTIAL Next-generation transit surveys — PLATO, planned for launch in 2026, will provide long, stable photometry of bright stars, improving the odds.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: continue searching Kepler and TESS data with improved systematics correction — a larger, cleaner sample of candidate signals and better limits on how common exomoons and rings are.
  2. Next: targeted JWST follow-up of the best candidates — independent confirmation or rejection of specific signals at higher precision.
  3. Next: PLATO and other long-baseline surveys begin operation — many more bright, quiet stars with years of uninterrupted photometry, where small moons are easier to spot.
  4. Longer term: dedicated exomoon and ring surveys, possibly with future large space telescopes — statistical understanding of how common moons and rings are around exoplanets, and their role in planetary system architecture.
