9Engineering Breakdown
  • Reactor pressure vessel — holds the simulated vent fluid at the pressure and temperature of the seafloor, tens of metres down. Key parts: pressure-rated steel or titanium cell, seals and fittings, temperature jacket.
  • Gradient chamber — brings an alkaline, hydrogen-rich fluid into contact with an acidic, CO2-rich fluid without letting them simply mix. Key parts: microfluidic channels or porous plugs, injection pumps, thermal gradient plates.
  • Mineral membrane — the catalytic barrier itself — iron-sulphide and nickel-iron-sulphide precipitates that form when the two fluids meet and separate them into compartments. Key parts: FeS / FeNiS precipitate layer, support grid or extruded cavity, aged or doped variants.
  • Feed and gas supply — delivers H2, CO2 and dissolved metals in controlled ratios. Key parts: gas mass-flow controllers, high-pressure syringe pumps, sulphide and metal salt reservoirs.
  • Analysis — finds out which organic molecules, and how many, the reactor made. Key parts: HPLC and mass spectrometry, NMR, Raman spectroscopy, pH and redox electrodes.
  • Sampling and quench — pulls fluid out of a hot, pressurised cell without letting the products degrade. Key parts: cooled exit capillary, pressure let-down valve.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the vent analogue. Set fluid chemistry from a real alkaline system such as the Lost City field, discovered in 2000: warm (roughly 40–90 °C), alkaline, hydrogen-rich water percolating through iron-bearing rock.
  2. Design the mixing geometry. Work out how two fluids at several pH units apart can meet across a thin precipitate without turbulent mixing washing the gradient away.
  3. Fabricate the cell. Machine the pressure vessel and cut the microfluidic or porous network that defines where the mineral barrier will sit.
  4. Establish the gradient. Pump the alkaline and acidic feeds at matched rates; the pH difference and the iron-sulphide precipitation do the rest, building the barrier in place.
  5. Add carbon and energy sources. Feed dissolved CO2 and H2 with nickel, iron and sulphur species so the fresh mineral surfaces can catalyse carbon fixation.
  6. Run and log. Hold the cell at vent conditions for hours to days, tracking pH, redox and temperature along its length.
  7. Quench and analyse. Sample the outflow under pressure, cool it and search for formate, acetate, methane and phosphorylated compounds.
  8. Test the membrane's electrical behaviour. Measure whether the mineral barrier sustains a proton gradient comparable to the one living cells maintain across their membranes.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Deep-sea vent chemistry and sampling — the reactor is only as good as the field measurements of real alkaline vent fluids it copies.
  • EXISTS High-pressure microfluidics — gradients across thin mineral layers exist only if flow can be controlled at small scale under pressure.
  • PARTIAL Prebiotic carbon-fixation catalysis — iron-sulphide and nickel-sulphide surfaces are known to make small organics, but yields and selectivity remain limited.
  • PARTIAL Prebiotic phosphorylation chemistry — energy-rich compounds such as acetyl phosphate can phosphorylate organics without enzymes, but the link to a working metabolism is unproven.
  • PARTIAL A testable theory of the first metabolism — the hypothesis that proton gradients across inorganic membranes drove the earliest carbon fixation is coherent but not yet demonstrated end to end.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: bench reactors demonstrably make organic molecules and sustain a proton gradient across a self-assembled iron-sulphide membrane — a physically plausible energy source for the first metabolism.
  2. Next: autocatalytic, self-sustaining carbon fixation in the reactor, producing its own catalysts and energy currency — chemistry that no longer needs a biologist feeding it.
  3. Next: coupling that chemistry to simple compartmentalised replicators — a bridge from protometabolism towards the earliest cells.
  4. Longer term: either a general account of how metabolism began, or a demonstration that it did not begin this way — a decisive answer to where life came from — and, incidentally, a toolkit for making chemicals under mild conditions without enzymes.
