PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Coronagraphy blocks a star's light inside the telescope to reveal faint companions, while a starshade flies tens of thousands of kilometres ahead as an external occulter. Together they enable direct imaging and spectroscopy of planets rather than indirect inference.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

An internal coronagraph uses deformable mirrors and shaped pupils to suppress diffracted starlight, and a starshade blocks the star before light enters the aperture, removing diffraction at source. Both demand wavefront stability at the picometre level and precise formation flying in the starshade case.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Coronagraph optics — Suppresses the star's light inside the telescope so a faint planet becomes visible. Key parts: deformable mirror, focal-plane mask or shaped pupil, Lyot stop, apodizer.
  • Wavefront control — Measures and corrects optical errors to picometre stability, because any ripple leaks starlight. Key parts: wavefront sensor, real-time control computer, deformable mirror actuators.
  • Starshade — Flies tens of thousands of kilometres ahead and blocks the star before its light enters the telescope. Key parts: petal-shaped occulter (tens of metres across), deployment structure, thrusters, laser metrology.
  • Formation flying — Keeps the starshade aligned with the telescope to within a metre over that distance. Key parts: relative navigation sensors, micro-thrusters, control algorithms.
  • Telescope and platform — Provides a stable, large aperture and points precisely at the target star. Key parts: primary mirror, pointing control, thermal control.
  • Spectrograph and data pipeline — Splits the planet's light into a spectrum and separates it from residual starlight. Key parts: integral field spectrograph, post-processing algorithms, calibration sources.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Model the diffraction. Use computer simulations to design masks and pupils that push starlight away from where the planet appears.
  2. Fabricate the deformable mirror. Build a mirror with hundreds or thousands of actuators that can bend its surface by fractions of a nanometre.
  3. Assemble the coronagraph instrument. Integrate the masks, mirrors, wavefront sensor and control electronics into a vacuum-sealed package.
  4. Test in a space-like environment. Run the instrument in a thermal-vacuum chamber to prove it holds alignment without air or gravity.
  5. Build the starshade. Manufacture a petal-shaped disc with edges accurate to tens of micrometres, so it blocks light without creating bright diffraction spikes.
  6. Launch and deploy. Fold the starshade for launch and unfold it in space once on the way to its observing position.
  7. Practice formation flying. Use thrusters and laser ranging to hold the starshade centred on the line between telescope and star, to within a metre at tens of thousands of kilometres.
  8. Observe and analyse. Point the telescope, block the star, and collect the planet's spectrum over hours to days.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Deformable mirrors with thousands of actuators — already used in ground-based adaptive optics to correct atmospheric blur.
  • PARTIAL Wavefront sensing and control at picometre stability — demonstrated in laboratory testbeds, but space-qualified systems are not yet flying.
  • NOT YET Starshade deployment and precision formation flying — no starshade has flown; it requires autonomous control at tens of thousands of kilometres.
  • EXISTS Large, stable space telescopes — Hubble and JWST show that large mirrors can hold shape in space.
  • EXISTS High-contrast post-processing algorithms — used on ground-based and space coronagraphs to dig fainter planets out of residual starlight.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: coronagraphs on Hubble, JWST and ground-based telescopes image young, massive planets — first direct spectra of a few exoplanets.
  2. Next: a dedicated coronagraph on the Roman Space Telescope (planned) demonstrates imaging of Jupiter-like planets — statistics on giant planets around nearby stars.
  3. Next: a starshade technology demonstration mission flies in formation with a space telescope — proof that starshades work in space.
  4. Longer term: a large space telescope with a starshade images Earth-sized planets and looks for biosignatures — direct study of potentially habitable worlds.
