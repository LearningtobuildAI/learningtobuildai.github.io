PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Extreme adaptive optics — corrects atmospheric turbulence thousands of times per second so the star image stays sharp. Key parts: deformable mirror with thousands of actuators, high-speed wavefront sensor (Shack-Hartmann or pyramid), real-time control computer.
  • Coronagraph — blocks the bright star so faint companions can be seen beside it. Key parts: apodizer or shaped pupil, focal-plane mask (vortex or Lyot), Lyot stop.
  • Integral field spectrograph — splits the remaining light into a spectrum at every pixel, giving the planet's colour and temperature. Key parts: lenslet array, prisms or grisms, low-noise infrared detector.
  • Wavefront calibration — measures and removes the optical errors that the wavefront sensor cannot see. Key parts: reference star or internal source, non-common-path aberration sensing, predictive control software.
  • Post-processing pipeline — subtracts the residual stellar halo in software. Key parts: angular differential imaging, spectral differential imaging, PSF subtraction algorithms.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design for a specific telescope. The coronagraph, adaptive optics and spectrograph are matched to the telescope's aperture, obstruction and site turbulence.
  2. Fabricate the deformable mirror. A thin reflective membrane is bonded to thousands of piezo or MEMS actuators and polished to optical quality.
  3. Make the coronagraph masks. Focal-plane masks and apodizers are etched or deposited with nanometre precision to suppress starlight at the target wavelength.
  4. Integrate the instrument. Optics, detectors and cryogenics are assembled in a clean room and aligned on a vibration-isolated bench.
  5. Commission on the telescope. The instrument is mounted at a focus, the AO loop is closed on real stars, and non-common-path aberrations are calibrated.
  6. Observe with the star blocked. Deep exposures are taken while the field rotates, so the planet moves relative to the residual starlight.
  7. Post-process the data. Reference images and the rotating field are combined to subtract the halo, revealing faint point sources.
  8. Extract spectra and orbits. The companion's spectrum gives its temperature and atmosphere, while repeated images give its orbit.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Large telescopes (8–10 m class) — They collect enough light to see a planet millions of times fainter than its star.
  • EXISTS Deformable mirrors with thousands of actuators — They must shape the wavefront finely enough to create a dark hole beside the star.
  • EXISTS Fast, low-noise infrared detectors — The planet signal is weak, so every stray electron matters.
  • EXISTS Coronagraph mask fabrication — Masks must be made to sub-wavelength precision to block starlight without dimming the planet.
  • EXISTS Real-time computing and post-processing algorithms — The AO loop runs at kilohertz rates, and halo subtraction needs large image archives.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: ground-based extreme AO instruments on 8-m telescopes image young, self-luminous giant planets and take their spectra — the first atmospheric studies of directly imaged exoplanets.
  2. Next: dedicated high-contrast instruments on 30-m class telescopes now under construction — imaging of older, fainter giant planets and possibly large rocky planets.
  3. Next: reflected-light spectroscopy of mature planetary systems — comparison of exoplanet atmospheres with those in our Solar System.
  4. Longer term: space-based coronagraphs on future observatories — direct imaging of Earth-like planets around nearby stars — if starlight suppression improves by another factor of a thousand.
