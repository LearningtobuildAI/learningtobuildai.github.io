PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

A multi-camera space photometer designed to detect and characterise planets around bright, nearby stars, with particular sensitivity to Earth-size worlds in the habitable zone. Its seismology channel simultaneously ages the host stars.

4How It Works

An array of off-axis refracting telescopes images the same field, combining photometric precision with a very wide dynamic range for bright hosts.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Camera array — 26 small refracting telescopes stare at the same patch of sky; 24 normal cameras plus 2 fast cameras that read out quickly so the brightest stars can be observed without saturating. Key parts: off-axis lens assemblies, about 120 mm aperture, lens barrel and baffles, mounting interface to the optical bench.
  • Focal planes — turn starlight into precise brightness measurements. Key parts: four large CCDs per camera — roughly a hundred in total, front-end electronics, readout at 25 s (normal cameras) and 2.5 s (fast cameras).
  • Optical bench and thermal control — holds all 26 cameras in rigid alignment and keeps them stable to fractions of a degree over years. Key parts: silicon-carbide bench structure, radiators and heaters, multi-layer insulation.
  • Onboard data handling — raw images from a hundred CCDs far exceed the downlink, so windows around target stars are extracted and compressed in orbit. Key parts: data processing units, star catalogue and window tables, mass memory.
  • Service module — powers, points and communicates. Key parts: solar arrays and batteries, attitude control thrusters and star trackers, X-band transmitter and antenna.
  • Ground segment — operates the spacecraft and turns telemetry into light curves. Key parts: mission operations centre, science operations centre, photometric pipeline, archive.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the optics for bright stars. A lens telescope with no central obstruction gives a clean point-spread function and little scattered light, so the very bright stars PLATO targets can be measured accurately. Several lens materials are stacked to control colour blur across the wide passband.
  2. Fabricate and coat the lenses. Each lens is ground, polished to a precise aspheric figure and anti-reflection coated; stray-light baffles are fitted around the barrel.
  3. Build the focal planes. Thinned, back-illuminated CCDs are packaged four to a camera with their readout electronics, giving a large sensitive area and fast, low-noise readout.
  4. Assemble the payload. The 26 cameras are bolted to a single stiff bench in a partly overlapping pattern, then aligned so their fields of view add up to one big patch of sky.
  5. Test on the ground. The payload and then the whole spacecraft go through thermal-vacuum, vibration and electromagnetic testing, and the photometric performance is verified against artificial stars.
  6. Launch to L2. A rocket lifts the spacecraft into space; it cruises about 1.5 million km away from Earth to a stable halo orbit around the second Lagrange point, where the thermal environment is steady and the Earth never blocks the view.
  7. Commission. Detectors are cooled and stabilised, focus and alignment are checked, and the pointing and data pipeline are calibrated on real stars.
  8. Run the survey. The spacecraft stares at one patch of sky for two years, then steps to a second patch, downlinking reduced data daily and building continuous brightness records for hundreds of thousands of stars.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Space photometry at micromagnitude-class precision — proved by earlier transit missions; PLATO must reach comparable precision on much brighter stars.
  • EXISTS Large-format CCDs with fast, low-noise readout — a hundred detectors must run simultaneously for years without drifting.
  • EXISTS Autonomous onboard data reduction — the raw image rate cannot be downlinked, so bright-star windows must be selected and compressed in orbit.
  • EXISTS A heavy-lift launcher and a deep-space ground network — the payload mass and the continuous data stream need both.
  • EXISTS Precise stellar catalogues and asteroseismology models — the same light curves are used to measure star sizes and ages, which is what makes planet radii and habitability meaningful.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: payload assembled and spacecraft in final test, awaiting its launch slot — a launch-ready observatory and a trained operations and science team.
  2. Next: launch and commissioning at L2 — verified photometric performance on real stars.
  3. Next: first long stare of two years — a catalogue of planets including small, temperate worlds around bright, well-characterised stars.
  4. Next: second field and extended mission — more sky, longer baselines and a broader sample of stars.
  5. Longer term: follow-up spectroscopy of the best targets by later observatories — masses and, for transiting planets, atmospheric measurements — turning candidates into characterised worlds.
