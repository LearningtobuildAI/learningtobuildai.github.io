PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Reflector and active surface — a 500 m dish that reshapes itself to keep a parabola focused as it tracks. Key parts: 4,450 triangular aluminium panels, steel cable net, motorised actuators at the net nodes, karst depression floor and drainage.
  • Feed cabin and positioning — suspends the receiver at the focus and steers it around the sky. Key parts: cables and six winches, Stewart platform for fine aiming, focus cabin with receivers and amplifiers.
  • Receivers — convert radio waves to electrical signals with the lowest possible noise. Key parts: 19-beam L-band receiver (roughly 1.05–1.45 GHz), ultra-wideband receiver covering a few hundred MHz to about 1.6 GHz, cryogenically cooled low-noise amplifiers.
  • Timing and signal chain — digitises wide bands while keeping absolute time, which is what reveals a drifting signal. Key parts: atomic-clock reference, wideband digitisers, optical fibre links to the control room.
  • Search computing — sifts terabytes for the rare narrow channel that behaves like a transmitter. Key parts: GPU clusters for coherent dedispersion, narrowband drift-search software, data storage and archive.
  • RFI monitoring and protection — keeps Earth-made signals from drowning the search. Key parts: monitoring antennas, spectrum monitoring, radio-quiet zone coordination.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the site. A natural karst bowl in Guizhou, deep enough to hold the dish and shielded from much of the surrounding radio traffic.
  2. Design the cable net. Structural modelling works out a net whose nodes can be pulled to form a small parabola inside the large sphere.
  3. Fabricate the panels. Thousands of aluminium triangles are made and mounted on the net, each tied to actuators below.
  4. Rig the feed cabin. Winches and cables are installed so the focus cabin can be held and moved above the dish.
  5. Install receivers and backends. Multibeam feeds, cryogenic amplifiers, digitisers and fibre links are fitted and calibrated.
  6. Calibrate the surface. The dish shape is measured and corrected until the focused beam is good enough for faint-signal work.
  7. Observe. Targets are tracked, or the sky drifts past, while the whole band is recorded for later search.
  8. Search and veto. Coherent dedispersion and drift searches pull out narrowband candidates, which are tested against interference by re-observing and by changing pointing.
  9. Confirm. Only a signal that repeats, moves with the sky and survives every interference test becomes a serious candidate.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Active reflector shape control — a fixed sphere cannot focus; the surface must deform continuously to track a source.
  • EXISTS Cryogenic multibeam receivers — system noise sets how faint a transmitter can be seen, and beams set how much sky each pointing covers.
  • EXISTS Real-time GPU signal processing — gigabits per second of raw voltage have to be dedispersed and searched faster than they arrive.
  • PARTIAL A radio-quiet site with interference mitigation — even in a protected valley, satellites, aircraft and ground transmitters produce narrowband signals that mimic the target.
  • PARTIAL Independent verification network — a single dish cannot prove a signal is not local; other telescopes in other countries must see it too.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: targeted searches of nearby stars and their exoplanets, plus commensal searching of data recorded for pulsar and fast-radio-burst surveys — the deepest narrowband limits available on any single dish, over the sky it can see
  2. Next: better interference rejection and automatic follow-up, so candidates can be checked within minutes — far fewer false alarms from satellites and phones
  3. Next: coordinated observing with interferometer arrays in the southern hemisphere — sky coverage and direction-finding that a single dish cannot provide
  4. Longer term: wide-field arrays with dish-class sensitivity — the ability to scan large areas of sky, not just point at chosen stars
  5. Longer term: an agreed international verification and announcement procedure if a signal survives every test — a credible, checkable claim rather than a headline
