PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Low-frequency radar penetrates kilometres of ice and reflects from the boundary between ice and liquid water, mapping the shape of subsurface oceans and detecting internal lakes. It is the primary tool for finding liquid water without landing.

4How It Works

Radar pulses at wavelengths of tens of metres pass through ice with little loss but reflect strongly at a water interface, and the delay and strength of the echo give depth and interface properties. Ionospheric distortion and surface clutter must be modelled out before the deep echo can be identified.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Antenna — radiates and receives low-frequency radio waves that can pass through kilometres of ice. Key parts: deployable booms 10–40 m long, wire dipole or array elements, feed and matching network.
  • Transmitter — generates short, high-power pulses at frequencies of a few megahertz. Key parts: power amplifier, pulse modulator, frequency synthesiser.
  • Receiver — detects the very weak echoes returning from deep interfaces. Key parts: low-noise amplifier, band-pass filters, analog-to-digital converter.
  • Digital processing unit — turns raw echoes into radargrams and corrects for ionospheric and surface clutter. Key parts: FPGA or radiation-hard processor, memory, pulse-compression and focusing software.
  • Timing and navigation — provides precise clock and orbit knowledge so echoes can be located on the surface. Key parts: ultra-stable oscillator, star trackers, inertial measurement unit.
  • Power and thermal — supplies electricity and keeps electronics within safe temperatures in space. Key parts: solar array or radioisotope power, batteries, radiators and heaters.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Set the science requirements. Choose the target depth, vertical resolution and ice properties; these fix the radar frequency, bandwidth and pulse length.
  2. Design the antenna. Model the radiation pattern and select boom length and element geometry for the chosen frequency band.
  3. Build the RF electronics. Assemble the transmitter, receiver and timing circuits, using space-qualified components and shielding against radiation.
  4. Write the processing chain. Develop algorithms for pulse compression, synthetic aperture focusing, ionospheric distortion correction and surface-clutter removal.
  5. Integrate and test on the ground. Test the full instrument in an anechoic chamber and, where possible, over known ice sheets or in a cold chamber to calibrate penetration.
  6. Launch and deploy. After launch, deploy the antenna booms and check that the radar returns clean echoes from the surface.
  7. Operate and map. Fly repeated passes, record radargrams, model the ionosphere for each pass and convert echo delays into depth profiles.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Low-frequency radar electronics — transmitters and receivers at 1–20 MHz are mature from terrestrial ice-penetrating radar.
  • EXISTS Large deployable space antennas — antennas tens of metres long have flown on Mars orbiters.
  • PARTIAL Ionospheric correction models — Mars' ionosphere is relatively weak, but Earth's and Jupiter's are more distorting, especially at low frequencies.
  • PARTIAL Radiation-hardened digital processors — the processing needed for real-time clutter removal demands high computing power that must survive space radiation.
  • EXISTS Precise orbit and timing knowledge — the depth measurement depends on knowing the spacecraft position and echo time to within metres and nanoseconds.
  • EXISTS Deep-space data downlink — raw radar data volumes are large, so onboard compression and selective downlink are needed.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: Orbital sounders map Mars' polar caps and buried glaciers; airborne radar charts Earth's ice sheets. — proven method for detecting liquid water under ice without landing.
  2. Next: Radars on Europa Clipper and JUICE sound the ice shells of Europa and Ganymede. — first direct measurements of the depth and structure of an extraterrestrial ocean.
  3. Next: Multi-frequency and higher-power sounders distinguish brine from fresh water and resolve thin internal layers. — better estimates of ocean habitability and ice-shell dynamics.
  4. Longer term: Dedicated radar sounders for Enceladus, Titan and other ocean worlds, possibly with real-time onboard processing. — systematic survey of subsurface oceans across the outer solar system.
