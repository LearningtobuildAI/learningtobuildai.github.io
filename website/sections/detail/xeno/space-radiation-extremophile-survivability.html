PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Exposure carrier — holds sample trays outside the vehicle, where nothing shields them from vacuum, ultraviolet and cosmic rays. Key parts: aluminium tray, individual sample cells, attachment interface for the host platform.
  • Sample windows — let ultraviolet through to the organisms while keeping them sealed and, in some cells, keep light out as a dark control. Key parts: quartz or magnesium-fluoride discs, seals, opaque covers.
  • Dosimetry — measures what the samples actually received, not what the orbit should have given them. Key parts: thermoluminescence detectors, plastic nuclear-track detectors, active radiation sensors, temperature loggers.
  • Recovery and return chain — brings the samples back without letting contamination or ordinary warmth change the result. Key parts: cold or ambient stowage, sample-return capsule or crewed return vehicle, sealed transfer containers.
  • Ground reference lab — runs the paired control experiment and analyses what came back. Key parts: space-simulation vacuum chamber with ultraviolet lamps, Mars-regolith and brine analogue mixes, viability assays, Raman and infrared spectroscopy.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the question. Panspermia, forward-contamination risk and life-detection limits each need different organisms, doses and controls, so the target is stated first.
  2. Prepare the samples. Spores, lichens, biofilms or dried cells are grown, counted, dried onto carriers and split into flight, ground-control and dark-control groups.
  3. Baseline characterisation. Every sample is fingerprinted before launch — cell counts, vitality, and spectroscopic signatures — so later change can be attributed to space.
  4. Package the hardware. Samples go into sealed cells behind ultraviolet-transparent windows, separated from each other so cross-contamination cannot occur.
  5. Install outside the vehicle. Trays are mounted on an external platform by spacewalk or robot arm, alongside dosimeters and temperature sensors.
  6. Expose. The samples sit in vacuum and full solar ultraviolet for months to years; the dark controls measure the contribution of radiation alone.
  7. Retrieve and return. The trays are pulled back inside and flown home, then opened in a laboratory under conditions that keep the surviving cells alive.
  8. Assay survival. Colony counts, germination and deoxyribonucleic-acid repair measurements give a survival fraction against the measured dose.
  9. Model and extrapolate. Survival data plus dosimetry and environmental records bound how long organisms could ride between planets and how hard spacecraft must be cleaned.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Routine access to external orbital platforms — the experiment only works if samples can be left outside a spacecraft and later reached again.
  • EXISTS Sample return to Earth — survivors must be counted in a full laboratory; instruments on orbit cannot measure viability well enough.
  • EXISTS Passive radiation dosimetry — without a dose record, survival results cannot be compared between flights or extrapolated to other orbits.
  • EXISTS Ground-based space simulators — separating vacuum, ultraviolet and radiation effects requires the same sample types tested one variable at a time on the ground.
  • EXISTS Cleanroom and planetary-protection practice — the experiment doubles as a contamination study, so the samples themselves must be known and controlled.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: external exposure platforms in low Earth orbit, with results from spore, lichen and radio-tolerant bacteria samples — hard numbers on how long a cell can survive in space, and how much shielding a spacecraft needs.
  2. Next: exposures outside the Earth's magnetic field, on free-flying or lunar-orbiting carriers — real galactic-cosmic-ray and solar-particle doses rather than the partly shielded low-orbit case.
  3. Next: multi-species biofilms and communities tested inside regolith and brine analogues — understanding of whether ecosystems, not just single hardy cells, can persist.
  4. Longer term: results feeding directly into planetary-protection rules and habitat shielding design for crewed deep-space missions — safer Mars sample return and life-detection missions that can still recognise a false positive.
