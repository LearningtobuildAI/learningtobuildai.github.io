PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Gravity storage involves lifting heavy masses using excess electrical energy during off-peak hours and allowing them to fall back down to generate electricity when needed. This method uses mechanical means instead of chemical reactions.

2Problem It Solves

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Excess power from renewable sources or other times is used to lift a large mass (like blocks or weights) into a higher position using cranes or shaft hoists. When energy demand increases, the masses are allowed to fall, driving generators and producing electricity.

5Materials Used
  • composite blocks
  • steel cables
  • cranes
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Requires specialized cranes, shafts, and composite blocks. The materials must be durable enough to withstand repeated lifting and lowering cycles over many years.

7Build Process

Construction involves setting up the storage tower or shaft, installing cranes or hoists, and preparing the composite blocks. Testing of the system follows before operation begins.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Mass blocks — store potential energy by being raised and lowered. Key parts: composite blocks, waste-material blocks, stacking and wear surfaces.
  • Lifting structure — supports and guides the blocks between low and high positions. Key parts: tower or shaft, guide rails, base foundation.
  • Hoisting mechanism — moves blocks up and down efficiently. Key parts: cables or chains, winches or cranes, sheaves and pulleys.
  • Motor-generator — converts electrical energy to lifting force and back to electricity. Key parts: motor-generator unit, power electronics, braking system.
  • Control system — orchestrates block movements, monitors load and optimises cycles. Key parts: sensors, software, safety interlocks.
  • Grid interface — connects the storage to the electrical grid. Key parts: transformer, switchgear, power conversion.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select site and prepare foundation. For a tower, pour a reinforced concrete base; for a shaft, survey and make safe an existing mine shaft or drill a new one.
  2. Fabricate the mass blocks. Cast blocks from concrete, composite materials or industrial waste, ensuring consistent weight and durability.
  3. Build the lifting structure. Erect a tall tower or install guide rails and hoisting frame in the shaft.
  4. Install hoisting machinery. Mount winches, cables, sheaves and the motor-generator unit at the top or base of the structure.
  5. Add power electronics and grid connection. Connect the motor-generator to a bidirectional converter, transformer and switchgear.
  6. Integrate control and safety systems. Install sensors for position, load and wind, and program software to manage multi-block lifting and lowering.
  7. Commission and cycle test. Test lifting and lowering sequences, measure round-trip efficiency, and verify safe operation over many cycles.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-torque electric motors and generators — they drive the hoist and recover electricity when lowering.
  • EXISTS High-strength, fatigue-resistant cables — they must lift heavy blocks thousands of times without failing.
  • PARTIAL Low-cost, durable block materials — blocks must be cheap enough and survive years of handling.
  • PARTIAL Precise multi-load control systems — coordinating many blocks or a single heavy mass safely is still being proven at scale.
  • EXISTS Suitable sites (shafts or land) — disused mine shafts and flat land near grid connections are available, though not everywhere.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: grid-connected pilot plants demonstrating round-trip efficiency and reliability — real performance data to guide design.
  2. Next: multi-megawatt, multi-hour systems with automated block handling — cost reduction through scale and manufacturing.
  3. Next: deployment in disused mine shafts and purpose-built towers — long-duration storage where pumped hydro is not possible.
  4. Longer term: standardised, mass-produced systems — wide use as a low-cost, chemistry-free storage option.
