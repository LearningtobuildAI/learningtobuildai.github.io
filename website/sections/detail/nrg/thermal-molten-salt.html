PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Thermal/Molten-Salt Storage is a method of energy storage where thermal energy is captured by heating molten salt and stored for later use. The heated salt can be used to generate power through steam turbines or provide process heat directly.

2Problem It Solves

It addresses the intermittency issues of solar power and other renewable sources by storing excess thermal energy during peak production times for use when generation is low. This makes solar power more dispatchable and reliable.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Nitrate salts, which are inexpensive and have high thermal capacity, are heated to very high temperatures (typically around 500°C) in a thermal storage system. This heated salt is then stored in insulated tanks until needed. When required, the hot salt is passed through a heat exchanger where it transfers its thermal energy to water, producing steam that drives a turbine to generate electricity or directly provides process heat.

5Materials Used
  • Nitrate salts
  • Insulation materials
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves sourcing and refining nitrate salts, constructing insulated storage tanks, and setting up heat exchangers and steam turbines or direct heating systems.

7Build Process

Construction begins with site preparation, followed by the installation of thermal storage tanks, heat transfer systems, and power generation components. The system is then commissioned for testing before full operation can commence.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Available today

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Hot and cold salt tanks — hold the same nitrate salt at two temperatures — roughly 290 °C and 565 °C — and so store the energy. Key parts: welded carbon-steel tank shells, mineral-wool insulation, ring or pile foundation, level and temperature instrumentation.
  • Solar or heat source — recharges the store. Key parts: heliostat field and central receiver, or parabolic-trough collectors, electric immersion heaters or heat pumps (power-to-heat variants), receiver heat-transfer fluid loop.
  • Charging loop — moves cold salt up to the hot tank. Key parts: circulation pumps, piping and valves, heat exchangers.
  • Discharging loop — taps hot salt to make steam or hot gas. Key parts: steam generator or process heat exchanger, feedwater pump, salt return piping.
  • Steam cycle and generator — turns the delivered heat into electricity where power is the product. Key parts: steam turbine, condenser, generator.
  • Freeze protection and controls — keeps salt above its freezing point, around 220 °C, everywhere in the loop. Key parts: electric trace heating, thermal insulation, control and safety system.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the duty. Decide the temperature range, hours of discharge and whether the output is electricity, steam or direct process heat — this sets the salt volume and tank size.
  2. Select the salt. Most plants use a 60/40 sodium-nitrate/potassium-nitrate mix, which is cheap and stable to about 565 °C; chloride or carbonate salts are studied for hotter operation.
  3. Build the tanks. Large insulated steel tanks are erected on engineered foundations, with internal distributors so incoming salt does not mix the hot and cold layers.
  4. Build the collector or heater. A heliostat field and tower, a trough field, or banks of electric heaters are installed to deliver heat into the loop.
  5. Install the molten-salt loop. Pumps, valves, expansion vessels and heat exchangers are fitted, and the whole circuit is wrapped in trace heating and insulation.
  6. Melt and fill. Salt arrives as solid pellets or flakes; it is melted with auxiliary heaters and circulated to fill the tanks, a slow first-time process.
  7. Charge. The heat source raises cold salt to the hot tank, storing energy for hours at a time.
  8. Discharge and deliver. Hot salt is pumped through a steam generator or process exchanger, returns to the cold tank, and the steam drives a turbine or feeds an industrial plant.
  9. Operate with freeze watch. Controls hold every pipe above the salt's freezing point, since a solid plug can shut down the plant.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Concentrating solar collectors or large electric heaters — they supply the heat that charges the store.
  • EXISTS Molten nitrate salt handling — decades of use in solar thermal plants have proven pumps, valves and materials.
  • EXISTS High-temperature pumps and valves — salt must circulate hundreds of times a day without leaking or seizing.
  • PARTIAL Cheap off-peak or surplus electricity — electric thermal storage only pays when power is very cheap for part of the day.
  • PARTIAL Industrial heat integration — many factories need continuous steam or heat that a store must match exactly.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: concentrating solar plants with 8–15 hours of molten-salt storage, such as Gemasolar and Noor Ouarzazate — solar power delivered into the evening and night.
  2. Next: electric thermal stores using salt, sand or brick feeding industrial steam and heat — decarbonising factories that cannot run on intermittent power directly.
  3. Next: retiring coal plants rebuilt around thermal stores and electric heaters — reuse of existing turbines, grid connections and sites.
  4. Longer term: multi-day thermal storage paired with very cheap wind and solar — firm, dispatchable heat and power over week-long lulls.

18Glossary
Thermal Storage
A method of storing heat for later use, often used in conjunction with solar thermal power plants or industrial processes.
CSP (Concentrated Solar Power)
A form of solar power generation that uses mirrors to concentrate sunlight onto a small area to produce high temperatures and generate electricity.
Dispatchable
The ability of an energy source to provide power on demand, as opposed to being dependent on the time of day or weather conditions.
