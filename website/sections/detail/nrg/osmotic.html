PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Osmotic or blue energy is a form of renewable energy that harnesses the natural salinity difference between freshwater and seawater, converting it into electrical energy through osmotic pressure.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Ion-selective membranes are used to separate ions from saltwater when it comes in contact with freshwater. This process creates an ion concentration gradient which drives a turbine or generates electricity via a membrane bi-layered cell setup.

5Materials Used
  • polymers
  • ion-selective membranes
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing of osmotic power plants involves creating and assembling ion-selective membranes, which are typically made from polymers. The assembly process includes integrating these membranes into a cell structure designed to optimize the flow of water and ions.

7Build Process

Construction involves setting up the plant in areas where freshwater meets seawater, such as estuaries or rivers flowing into the sea. Installation requires careful site selection and integration with existing infrastructure.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Intake and pretreatment — brings clean fresh water and seawater to the membranes without clogging them. Key parts: screens and settling basins, filtration, pumps.
  • Membranes — the heart of the process — they let water or ions pass selectively. Key parts: semi-permeable membranes (pressure-retarded osmosis), cation- and anion-exchange membranes (reverse electrodialysis).
  • Module or stack — packs large membrane area into a compact pressure vessel or plate stack. Key parts: spiral-wound or plate-and-frame elements, spacers and seals, electrodes for electrodialysis stacks.
  • Energy conversion — turns osmotic pressure into shaft power, or ionic flow into direct current. Key parts: pressure exchanger, water turbine and generator (PRO), stack electrodes and DC conversion (RED).
  • Grid connection and controls — conditions the output and manages the plant. Key parts: inverter or transformer, control system, fouling and performance monitoring.
  • Fouling and cleaning system — keeps membrane performance from collapsing. Key parts: backwash and chemical cleaning, biofouling control.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the site. A river mouth with high freshwater flow, strong seawater salinity and low silt load; the salinity gap sets the theoretical energy, roughly 0.7–0.8 kWh per cubic metre of freshwater mixed.
  2. Design the intake. River and sea intakes are sited and screened so that silt, debris and marine life are kept off the membranes, since pretreatment is a major share of plant cost.
  3. Make the membranes. Semi-permeable membranes are cast as thin polymer films on a porous support with high water permeability but low salt leakage; ion-exchange membranes are made by grafting charged chemical groups onto polymer sheets, one batch positive and one negative.
  4. Assemble modules and stacks. Membranes are wound into elements or stacked in alternating layers hundreds of pairs deep, with spacers between them and an anode and cathode at the ends.
  5. Install the hydraulics. In a pressure-retarded osmosis plant, seawater is fed at roughly half the osmotic pressure (the full seawater-versus-freshwater pressure is about 27 bar) and the pressurised flow drives a turbine; in reverse electrodialysis, the stack's voltage is drawn off directly.
  6. Connect and commission. Power conversion equipment is tied to the local grid, and flows, pressures and stack voltage are tuned for maximum net output.
  7. Operate and control fouling. Membranes are cleaned and monitored continuously, because fouling and low power density are what decide whether the plant ever pays for itself.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Ion-selective and semi-permeable membranes with high power density — today's membranes yield well under the several watts per square metre that would make the economics work.
  • PARTIAL Cheap, high-volume membrane manufacturing — membrane area needed per megawatt is very large, so unit cost dominates the whole plant.
  • EXISTS Pretreatment and antifouling know-how — desalination already provides the filtration and cleaning techniques this technology borrows.
  • EXISTS High-salinity brine or a strong salinity gradient — output scales with the salinity difference, so pairing with desalination brine or hypersaline lakes helps.
  • EXISTS Suitable river-mouth sites — the resource is natural and continuous, but only where large rivers meet salt water.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: kilowatt-scale pilot plants — a Norwegian pressure-retarded osmosis prototype built around 2009 and a Dutch reverse-electrodialysis pilot on a sea dike proved the physics but not the economics.
  2. Next: hybrid plants that mix desalination brine with treated wastewater — a far larger salinity gap and a co-located customer, improving power density and payback.
  3. Next: membrane power density of several watts per square metre at commercial prices — megawatt-scale plants at major river mouths.
  4. Longer term: arrays of modules at high-flow estuaries — genuinely firm, weather-independent renewable power, a rare thing in the renewable mix.

18Glossary
osmotic pressure
The pressure required to prevent the flow of solvent from a region of high solute concentration (freshwater) to a region of low solute concentration (saltwater)
ion-selective membranes
Membranes that allow certain ions to pass through while blocking others, facilitating the generation of electricity
