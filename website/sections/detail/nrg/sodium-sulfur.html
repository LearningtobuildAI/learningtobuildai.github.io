PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The sodium-sulfur (NAS) battery operates with molten sodium on one side of the solid electrolyte and molten sulfur on the other. During charging, sodium ions move from the negative electrode to the positive electrode through the electrolyte, while electrons flow externally. During discharging, this process is reversed, releasing electrical energy.

5Materials Used
  • Molten sodium
  • Molten sulfur
  • Solid beta-alumina electrolyte
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves creating the electrodes from molten sodium and sulfur, which are then sealed within a vacuum-insulated container. The solid beta-alumina electrolyte is carefully formed between these electrodes.

7Build Process

Components are assembled in an inert gas atmosphere to prevent oxidation of the reactive materials. The battery must be operated at high temperatures (around 300°C) to maintain the molten state of its components.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cell — stores energy electrochemically using molten sodium and sulfur separated by a solid ceramic electrolyte. Key parts: beta-alumina solid electrolyte tube, molten sodium anode, molten sulfur cathode, sealed cell casing.
  • Thermal management — keeps the battery at 300–350 °C so the electrodes stay molten and the electrolyte conducts sodium ions. Key parts: electric heaters, thermal insulation, temperature sensors, cooling for standby.
  • Module and string assembly — connects many cells in series and parallel to reach the required voltage and capacity. Key parts: cell interconnects, module housing, series/parallel busbars.
  • Power conversion system — converts the battery's DC output to grid-compatible AC and controls charge/discharge. Key parts: bidirectional inverter, transformer, switchgear.
  • Battery management and safety system — monitors cell health, temperature and faults, and handles the hazards of molten sodium and sulfur. Key parts: cell voltage and temperature monitoring, fault detection, fire suppression, enclosure ventilation.
  • Enclosure and installation — houses modules and thermal systems in a weatherproof, insulated container. Key parts: insulated container, structural supports, cable entry.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Make the beta-alumina electrolyte. Alumina powder is mixed with sodium oxide, formed into tubes, and sintered at high temperature to create a dense ceramic that conducts sodium ions.
  2. Assemble individual cells. Each electrolyte tube is placed in a cell casing, filled with molten sodium inside and molten sulfur outside (or vice versa), and hermetically sealed.
  3. Build modules. Cells are connected in series and parallel, wrapped in thermal insulation, and fitted with heaters and temperature sensors.
  4. Assemble battery strings and containers. Modules are stacked into insulated containers, wired into strings, and connected to the power conversion system.
  5. Install and commission. The container is delivered to site, connected to the grid, and slowly heated to operating temperature; capacity and safety systems are tested.
  6. Operate and maintain. The battery is kept hot continuously, with periodic checks of cell health, insulation and heaters; end-of-life cells are recycled.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Beta-alumina solid electrolyte manufacturing — the ceramic membrane must be dense, thin and highly conductive to sodium ions.
  • EXISTS High-temperature materials for molten sodium and sulfur — cell casings and seals must resist corrosion at 300–350 °C for years.
  • EXISTS Grid-scale power electronics — bidirectional inverters and transformers are needed to interface with the grid.
  • EXISTS Thermal insulation and heating systems — the battery must stay molten even when idle, which consumes energy.
  • EXISTS Safety systems for reactive materials — sodium and sulfur burn on contact with water or air, so containment and fire suppression are essential.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: MW-scale NAS battery installations for peak shaving and renewable integration — multi-hour storage on grids with high solar or wind share.
  2. Next: larger installations (tens to hundreds of MWh) with improved cycle life and lower standby losses — more cost-effective daily cycling and better competition with lithium-ion.
  3. Next: integration with grid-forming inverters and hybrid systems — support for weak grids and combination with fast-responding storage.
  4. Longer term: closed-loop recycling of sodium and sulfur and reduced material costs — sustainable, lower-cost long-duration storage.

18Glossary
Beta-alumina
A solid ceramic material used as an electrolyte in NAS batteries, allowing the passage of sodium ions at high temperatures.
Molten materials
Materials that are in a liquid state due to being heated above their melting point. In NAS batteries, these refer to sodium and sulfur.
