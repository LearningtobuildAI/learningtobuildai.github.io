4How It Works

These probes employ advanced quantum sensors capable of detecting minute changes in subatomic particles due to gravitational influences. By leveraging quantum mechanics principles, they can observe phenomena that classical gravity theories cannot explain.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Probe masses — the objects whose motion carries the signal — trapped atoms, ions or micrometre-scale particles. Key parts: levitated dielectric microspheres, cold atom clouds, trapped ions.
  • Trapping and cooling — hold the probe still enough that a minuscule gravitational effect is visible. Key parts: optical tweezers, ion traps, laser cooling, cryogenic environment.
  • Readout interferometry — measures displacement or coherence at the quantum limit. Key parts: frequency-stabilised lasers, optical cavities, squeezed-light source, single-photon detectors.
  • Isolation and shielding — removes every louder force that would swamp gravity. Key parts: ultra-high vacuum chamber, multi-stage vibration isolation, magnetic and electric shielding, thermal shielding.
  • Metrology and control — keeps the experiment stable and calibrated over long runs. Key parts: optical clocks and timing, feedback control, environmental sensors.
  • Theory and inference — turns a null or positive result into a statement about gravity. Key parts: predicted signatures from quantum-gravity models, statistical model comparison.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick a testable signature. Choose something a quantum theory of gravity predicts and classical physics does not — for example gravity-mediated entanglement between two masses, a Planck-scale blurring of distance, or loss of coherence in a massive object.
  2. Build the probe. Trap microspheres in optical tweezers or hold atoms in an interferometer, and cool the motion close to its quantum ground state.
  3. Isolate the experiment. Pump to ultra-high vacuum, suspend the apparatus on vibration isolators, and screen out stray electric, magnetic and thermal fields.
  4. Calibrate and subtract known forces. Measure Casimir, electrostatic, patch-potential and gas-collision effects so they can be excluded rather than mistaken for new physics.
  5. Interfere and read out. Run the probe through an interferometer or entangling sequence and record the phase, coherence or correlation with quantum-limited precision.
  6. Scale until gravity dominates. Increase mass and shorten separation until the gravitational coupling between probes is the strongest interaction left — the central unsolved engineering problem.
  7. Cross-check with long-baseline atom interferometers. Free-falling atom clouds separated by tens to hundreds of metres act as very sensitive low-frequency gravitational-wave and dark-matter antennas.
  8. Compare against theory. Use the result to constrain or rule out classes of quantum-gravity models, then design the next, more sensitive generation.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Quantum-limited interferometry — gravitational-wave observatories already operate at the quantum noise limit using squeezed light.
  • PARTIAL Ground-state cooling of mesoscopic objects — achieved for nanoscale particles in optical traps, but not yet for the larger masses these tests need.
  • NOT YET Entanglement between two massive objects — the proposed decisive test of whether gravity is quantum requires this, and no experiment has produced it.
  • EXISTS Ultra-high vacuum and vibration isolation — without them, gas collisions and seismic noise are orders of magnitude larger than the signal.
  • NOT YET Quantitative predictions from quantum-gravity theory — there is no agreed, testable prediction, so experiments are designed to bound whole families of models rather than confirm one.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: tabletop levitated-optomechanics experiments and cold-atom interferometers in laboratories; gravitational-wave observatories detecting waves from merging black holes and neutron stars — techniques for reaching and reading quantum states of motion.
  2. Next: long-baseline atom interferometers at the 100 m scale being built for gravitational-wave and dark-matter searches — a new frequency band no laser interferometer can reach, and a testbed for quantum-gravity signatures.
  3. Next: a positive entanglement witness between two mesoscopic masses — evidence that the gravitational field itself is quantum.
  4. Longer term: kilometre-scale, space-based or upgraded quantum sensors — probes of Planck-scale physics that no present experiment can touch — or firm upper bounds showing where quantum gravity does not show up.
