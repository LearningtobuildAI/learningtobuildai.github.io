PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Inertial confinement fusion (ICF) uses powerful lasers to compress a tiny spherical fuel pellet, causing the fuel to reach conditions where nuclear fusion can occur.

2Problem It Solves

ICF addresses the challenge of achieving sustained nuclear fusion reactions that produce more energy than is required to initiate them, which would be necessary for practical fusion power generation.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

High-intensity laser beams are precisely aimed at a small capsule containing fusion fuel. The lasers heat and compress the fuel, causing it to ignite in a process similar to a supernova explosion, leading to a short-lived but intense fusion reaction.

5Materials Used
  • laser glass
  • deuterium-tritium ice
  • lithium-deuteride
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves creating and testing laser systems capable of delivering precise, high-intensity pulses. Fuel capsules are also carefully engineered to ensure they can withstand the intense conditions without disintegrating prematurely.

7Build Process

ICF facilities require sophisticated laser systems, vacuum chambers, and highly controlled environments. The fuel pellets must be precisely manufactured from materials like deuterium-tritium ice or lithium-deuteride.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Laser driver — generates and amplifies the precisely shaped pulses that crush the fuel capsule. Key parts: neodymium-doped glass amplifier slabs, flashlamps, frequency converters to ultraviolet, 192 beamlines.
  • Beam transport and final optics — routes and focuses every beam onto the target with micrometre precision. Key parts: mirrors, lenses, spatial filters, debris shields.
  • Target assembly — the fuel capsule and the hohlraum that turns laser light into X-rays. Key parts: deuterium–tritium fuel layer, plastic or diamond ablator shell, gold or uranium hohlraum cylinder.
  • Target chamber and positioner — holds the target at the exact focus of all beams. Key parts: 10 m aluminium chamber, target positioner, cryogenic cooling.
  • Diagnostics and control — measures how well the implosion performed and keeps the laser aligned. Key parts: X-ray imagers, neutron time-of-flight detectors, laser alignment sensors.
  • Power conversion (future plant) — in a reactor, captures heat, breeds tritium and makes electricity. Key parts: liquid-metal first wall, tritium extraction, heat exchanger, turbine and generator.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Make the laser glass. Neodymium-doped phosphate glass is melted, cast into large slabs and polished to optical flatness.
  2. Build amplifier chains. Slabs are stacked between flashlamps in long beamlines that amplify a small seed pulse to megajoules.
  3. Fabricate the target. A hollow capsule is formed, filled with deuterium–tritium, and cooled until the fuel freezes into a smooth solid layer; the hohlraum is machined around it.
  4. Align the system. All 192 beams are aligned to hit the hohlraum symmetrically, and the target is positioned to within micrometres.
  5. Fire the shot. The lasers fire; inside the hohlraum they convert to X-rays that ablate the capsule, imploding the fuel until it ignites and releases fusion energy.
  6. Move to repetition (future). A power plant would inject new targets several times per second instead of firing a few shots per day.
  7. Capture heat and breed tritium. A liquid-metal blanket absorbs fusion neutrons, breeds tritium from lithium, and carries heat away.
  8. Generate electricity. The captured heat drives a steam turbine and generator, as in a conventional power station.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-energy, precisely shaped laser pulses — the driver must deliver megajoules in a carefully tailored pulse to compress the fuel evenly.
  • EXISTS Cryogenic target fabrication — the fuel layer must be near-perfect and kept at cryogenic temperature until the moment of firing.
  • PARTIAL Ignition physics at high gain — scientific net energy gain was reached at the National Ignition Facility in 2022, but only once and with modest gain.
  • NOT YET Repetition-rate lasers and target injection — a power plant needs many shots per second, not a few per day.
  • NOT YET Tritium breeding and neutron-resistant materials — the reaction consumes tritium and damages the chamber, so a plant must make its own fuel and survive neutron bombardment.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: laser facilities such as the National Ignition Facility study ignition and measure gain — confidence that inertial fusion can release more energy than the laser delivers.
  2. Next: higher gain and repeatable ignition with different target designs — the physics basis for an engineering test facility.
  3. Next: engineering test facilities with high-repetition lasers and target injection — proof that targets can be mass-produced and fired continuously.
  4. Longer term: pilot laser-fusion power plants — electricity from inertial fusion, if tritium breeding and chamber materials are solved.
  5. Longer term: commercial fleets — firm, low-carbon power alongside magnetic fusion and renewables.

18Glossary
net energy gain
The condition where the energy produced by a fusion reaction exceeds the energy required to initiate it.
inertial confinement
A method of achieving nuclear fusion by compressing a fuel target using an external source of energy, such as laser beams or particle beams.
