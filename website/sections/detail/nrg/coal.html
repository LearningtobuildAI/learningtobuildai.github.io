PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Coal is crushed into fine particles and burned in a furnace. The heat from the combustion raises water to high-pressure steam, which is directed through pipes to drive turbine blades, converting thermal energy into mechanical energy that generates electricity.

5Materials Used
  • coal
  • steam
  • turbine
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Coal mines extract and process coal for use in power plants. Power plant construction involves building boilers, turbines, and associated equipment.

7Build Process

Mining operations extract raw coal, which is transported to the power plant where it undergoes processing before being burned in a boiler.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Available today

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Fuel handling and preparation — stores, crushes and dries coal into a fine powder so it burns like a gas. Key parts: coal stockpile and reclaim system, conveyors, pulverising mills, primary air fans.
  • Boiler and furnace — burns pulverised coal to raise high-pressure, high-temperature steam. Key parts: furnace and water walls, superheater and reheater, economiser, air preheater.
  • Steam turbine and generator — expands steam through turbines to spin a generator and produce electricity. Key parts: high-pressure turbine, intermediate-pressure turbine, low-pressure turbine, generator, condenser.
  • Cooling and water system — condenses exhaust steam and rejects waste heat to the environment. Key parts: cooling tower or once-through cooling, circulating water pumps, make-up water treatment.
  • Emissions control — removes particulates, sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides from flue gas. Key parts: electrostatic precipitator or fabric filter, flue-gas desulphurisation scrubber, selective catalytic reduction.
  • Ash and byproduct handling — collects and disposes of or reuses bottom ash and fly ash. Key parts: bottom ash hopper, fly ash collection, ash silos and loadout.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Prepare the site. Clear and level land, build foundations for the boiler, turbine hall and cooling towers, and provide rail or road access for coal delivery.
  2. Erect the boiler. Assemble the tall steel frame and weld the water-wall tubes, superheater and economiser into the furnace structure.
  3. Install the turbine and generator. Place the multi-stage steam turbine and the generator on a common shaft, aligned to fractions of a millimetre.
  4. Build the cooling system. Construct cooling towers or intake and outfall channels for once-through cooling, and install circulating water pumps.
  5. Add emissions controls. Install electrostatic precipitators or fabric filters, flue-gas desulphurisation scrubbers and selective catalytic reduction units in the flue-gas path.
  6. Wire and instrument. Connect the generator to the grid via transformers and switchgear, and install the distributed control system and sensors.
  7. Commission and test. Fire the boiler on oil or gas, then coal; raise steam, roll the turbine, synchronise to the grid and tune combustion and emissions.
  8. Operate and maintain. Run as baseload or load-following plant, with regular inspection of boiler tubes, turbine blades and emissions equipment.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Pulverised coal combustion — grinding coal to talcum-powder fineness allows rapid, complete burning in a compact furnace.
  • EXISTS High-pressure steam turbine — converts steam at hundreds of degrees Celsius and pressures above 200 bar into rotary power.
  • EXISTS High-temperature alloys — superheater and turbine components must resist creep and oxidation at 600 °C and above.
  • EXISTS Flue-gas clean-up — modern air-quality rules require removal of over 99% of particulates and most sulphur and nitrogen oxides.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: existing plants run as legacy baseload, with retirements accelerating in many regions — short-term grid reliability while replacements are built.
  2. Next: co-firing biomass or ammonia in some boilers to cut net carbon emissions — a transitional use of existing coal assets with lower emissions.
  3. Next: carbon capture and storage retrofits on a limited number of plants — a possible lifeline for otherwise stranded coal capacity, if costs and transport networks allow.
  4. Longer term: phase-out or repurposing of coal sites for synchronous condensers, grid storage or renewable hubs — reuse of grid connections and land as coal-fired generation ends.
