PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Stellar fusion power plants are theoretical devices designed to replicate the nuclear fusion processes occurring in stars. These reactors aim to produce vast amounts of energy through controlled fusion reactions, potentially providing a sustainable and nearly limitless source of power.

2Problem It Solves

Current limitations in space exploration include reliance on heavy fuel supplies for long-duration missions and limited energy sources. Stellar fusion power plants could provide a solution by offering a compact, high-energy-density power source that does not require frequent resupply or refueling.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Fusion involves combining light atomic nuclei under extreme temperatures and pressures to form heavier nuclei, releasing significant amounts of energy. In stellar fusion power plants, this process is simulated in a contained environment using methods such as magnetic confinement or inertial confinement, with the goal of achieving and maintaining a self-sustaining plasma state.

5Materials Used
  • High-temperature superconductors
  • Heat-resistant ceramics
  • Tungsten alloys
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing of stellar fusion reactors faces significant challenges due to the extreme conditions required (temperatures in the millions of degrees Celsius). Materials must be able to withstand such environments and maintain structural integrity. Current prototypes are largely theoretical, with no large-scale manufacturing processes established.

7Build Process

Building a stellar fusion power plant involves developing advanced materials capable of withstanding high temperatures and pressures, designing and implementing confinement systems (such as tokamaks or laser-based inertial fusion), and achieving sustained plasma conditions that can initiate and maintain the fusion reaction. This process is highly experimental and requires substantial scientific breakthroughs.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Compact plasma core — a scaled-down confinement device that burns fusion fuel while weighing as little as possible. Key parts: field-reversed configuration or compact torus, superconducting coils, heating antennas.
  • Power conversion — turns fusion energy into electricity without a steam turbine, which is impractical in space. Key parts: direct converter (charged particles slowed by electric fields), or a closed Brayton cycle with a working gas, generator and power conditioning.
  • Waste-heat radiators — the only way to dump heat in a vacuum — a plant rejecting megawatts needs hundreds of square metres. Key parts: high-temperature panels, coolant loop, deployable structure.
  • Fuel storage and processing — stores and meters the fuel; a plant cannot refuel from a pipeline. Key parts: tanks for deuterium and helium-3, tritium recovery if the fuel cycle produces it, injectors.
  • Shielding and structure — protects crew and electronics from neutrons and X-rays while keeping mass low. Key parts: lithium-hydride and boron shielding, shadow shield geometry, truss and berthing interfaces.
  • Thermal and attitude control — keeps magnets cold, surfaces warm where needed, and the plant pointed correctly. Key parts: cryocoolers, multilayer insulation, reaction wheels or thrusters.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design for the launch mass budget. Unlike a grid plant, every component must survive launch loads and be lifted at a cost, so the reactor is designed small and modular.
  2. Build and test the plasma core on the ground. A prototype burns deuterium fuel in a vacuum chamber, measuring how long the plasma can be held and how much fusion energy it releases.
  3. Develop magnets and cryocoolers for space. Superconducting coils must run for years using radiators and mechanical coolers rather than a facility's liquid-helium supply.
  4. Demonstrate direct conversion. Charged fusion products are guided through electric fields or a magnetic nozzle, and the recovered current is measured.
  5. Qualify the radiators. Large deployable panels are tested in thermal vacuum to prove they reject heat at the rates the design requires.
  6. Assemble a ground prototype. Core, conversion, radiators, shielding and controls are integrated and run continuously in vacuum, aiming for net electricity out.
  7. Launch and assemble in orbit. If the plant is too large for one rocket, sections are launched separately and joined by robotic arms or crew; a lunar-surface version would be landed instead.
  8. Commission and operate. The plant starts on deuterium alone, then moves to the advanced fuel cycle once the core behaves predictably.
  9. Recycle or dispose. At end of life the activated core must be safely deorbited, buried on the Moon, or left in a stable graveyard orbit.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Net electricity from fusion on the ground — no fusion device has yet produced more energy than it consumes in a form that can be turned into power.
  • PARTIAL Compact, high-field superconducting magnets — high-temperature superconducting coils have been demonstrated at around 20 tesla, but not yet in a flight-qualified package.
  • NOT YET Low-neutron or aneutronic fuel cycles — deuterium–tritium fuel produces a heavy neutron flux needing thick shielding; deuterium–helium-3 reduces it but needs helium-3, which is scarce on Earth.
  • PARTIAL Lightweight megawatt-scale heat rejection — planned lunar fission reactors are driving radiator and Stirling/Brayton technology, but not yet at fusion-relevant temperatures and powers.
  • PARTIAL In-orbit assembly and servicing — the International Space Station and robotic servicing missions show it is possible, but not for a reactor.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: ground experiments on compact fusion configurations, plus NASA-funded studies of fusion-driven spacecraft — physics data and mass estimates for a space reactor.
  2. Next: a ground prototype in vacuum that sustains fusion and recovers electricity — confidence that a flight unit is worth building.
  3. Next: an uncrewed in-space or lunar-surface demonstrator at kilowatt to megawatt scale — real operating experience with radiators, cryocoolers and remote maintenance.
  4. Longer term: a megawatt-class plant supporting a lunar base or a deep-space transfer vehicle — power for missions too far from the Sun for solar arrays and too long for chemical fuel.
  5. Speculative: larger plants for settlements or outer-planet industry — energy independence from Earth, provided helium-3 or other advanced fuel can be sourced.

18Glossary
Plasma
A state of matter consisting of ions and free electrons, often used to describe the ionized gas in fusion reactors.
Magnetic Confinement
A method for containing plasma within a magnetic field, commonly used in tokamak designs.
Inertial Confinement
A technique that uses intense laser pulses or particle beams to compress and heat matter to fusion conditions, often employed in inertial confinement fusion (ICF) experiments.
