PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Fusion Energy is a technology that aims to harness the power of nuclear fusion, the process that powers stars like the sun. In this process, light nuclei (such as isotopes of hydrogen) are heated to extremely high temperatures to form plasma, which is then confined using magnetic or inertial methods until they fuse, releasing vast amounts of energy.

2Problem It Solves

Fusion Energy addresses the need for a sustainable and virtually limitless source of clean energy. It promises to provide baseload power with minimal environmental impact compared to fossil fuels or fission-based nuclear reactors, which could help mitigate climate change and reduce dependence on non-renewable resources.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Fusion involves heating hydrogen isotopes (deuterium and tritium) to millions of degrees Celsius to create a plasma state. This plasma is then contained using either magnetic confinement in devices like tokamaks or inertial confinement in lasers or z-pinch machines until the nuclei are close enough to fuse, releasing energy through the conversion of mass into energy according to Einstein's equation E=mc^2.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing fusion devices is complex due to the high temperatures and precise conditions required for plasma confinement and control. Materials must withstand extreme heat and radiation, making production challenging and expensive.

7Build Process

The build process involves designing, constructing, and testing large-scale fusion reactors such as tokamaks or stellarators over many years. This includes developing advanced materials, improving plasma confinement techniques, and optimizing energy conversion systems.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Confinement system — keeps the fusing plasma away from any solid surface, either with strong magnetic fields or by crushing tiny fuel pellets with beams. Key parts: magnet coils (magnetic route) or laser and beam drivers (inertial route), vacuum chamber, plasma diagnostics.
  • Fuel cycle — supplies deuterium and tritium and recovers unburned tritium from the exhaust so it can be reused. Key parts: deuterium supply, tritium processing and storage, isotope separation.
  • Blanket and first wall — absorbs the fusion neutrons, converts their energy into heat and breeds fresh tritium from lithium. Key parts: lithium-bearing blanket, neutron multiplier, coolant channels, armoured first wall.
  • Heat and power conversion — moves blanket heat to a turbine and generator, much like a conventional thermal power station. Key parts: primary coolant loop, heat exchanger, steam turbine, generator.
  • Exhaust and vacuum — removes helium ash and surplus heat and keeps the chamber at high vacuum. Key parts: divertor or exhaust target, cryopumps, tritium recovery from the exhaust stream.
  • Remote handling and containment — lets a plant be maintained without people entering, and keeps radioactive tritium from escaping. Key parts: robotic manipulators, gloveboxes and detritiation systems, storage for activated components.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a confinement route. Design the plant around either magnetic confinement (tokamak, stellarator) or inertial confinement driven by lasers or pulsed beams. The choice sets almost every other engineering decision.
  2. Fabricate the vacuum chamber. Weld or bolt the chamber together in sections to tight tolerances, with hundreds of ports for heating, fuelling and instruments.
  3. Install the confinement hardware. For magnetic designs, wind superconducting coils and enclose them in a cryostat cooled to a few degrees above absolute zero. For inertial designs, build and align the beam drivers and the target chamber.
  4. Line the interior. Fit the lithium-bearing breeding blanket and the exhaust surfaces, and place neutron shielding between the plasma and the magnets or sensitive equipment.
  5. Add fuel and vacuum plant. Install deuterium injection, tritium processing, cryopumps and the tritium accounting systems that track every gram.
  6. Condition and ignite the plasma. Start on hydrogen to learn control, move to deuterium, then introduce tritium. Heat to roughly 100–150 million °C so nuclei overcome their mutual repulsion and fuse.
  7. Sustain the burn and breed fuel. Run long enough for neutrons to heat the blanket and breed tritium, so the plant produces its own fuel rather than consuming a stockpile.
  8. Generate and export electricity. Carry blanket heat to heat exchangers, raise steam, spin a turbine and feed the grid — while proving remote maintenance and safe tritium containment.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Net energy gain in a confined plasma — laser fusion at the US National Ignition Facility released more energy than the laser delivered to the target in December 2022, but no magnetic device has yet produced more fusion energy than it consumes.
  • NOT YET Tritium breeding at scale — tritium barely exists in nature and decays with a 12.3-year half-life, so a power plant must manufacture its own from lithium; no integrated breeding blanket has yet produced net tritium.
  • NOT YET Neutron-tolerant structural materials — fusion neutrons displace atoms in steel and other structures far faster than fission neutrons, and no material has been qualified for years of plant-level exposure.
  • PARTIAL High-temperature superconducting magnets — coils wound from rare-earth barium copper oxide tape have reached around 20 tesla, enabling smaller and cheaper magnetic machines, but reliable industrial-scale production is still being proven.
  • PARTIAL Steady-state plasma control and disruption avoidance — a power plant must run for hours or days, while today's devices typically run for seconds to minutes, and uncontrolled disruptions can damage the machine.
  • PARTIAL Tritium handling and remote maintenance — tritium is radioactive and permeates materials, so plants must be serviced by robots and keep releases close to zero.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: ITER is being assembled in France, private ventures are building compact high-field tokamaks and alternative designs, and laser fusion has reached ignition — the physics and engineering base needed to attempt a burning plasma.
  2. Next: a magnetic device shows more fusion energy out than heating energy in, running repeatedly rather than as a one-off shot — confidence that net gain is achievable in a practical, repeatable machine.
  3. Next: pilot plants close the fuel cycle: breed tritium, tolerate neutron damage and produce hundreds of megawatts of heat — the parts still missing between a physics experiment and a power station.
  4. Longer term: grid-connected demonstration plants, then commercial fleets — firm, low-carbon baseload power with fuel drawn from seawater and lithium.

18Glossary
Plasma
A state of matter consisting of ions and free electrons, often created by heating gases to very high temperatures.
Tokamak
A toroidal-shaped device used for magnetic confinement of plasma in nuclear fusion research.
Inertial Confinement Fusion (ICF)
A method of achieving nuclear fusion by compressing and heating a target containing fuel using intense lasers or particle beams.
