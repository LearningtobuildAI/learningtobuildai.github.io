PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The tether's movement through the planet's magnetic field induces an electric current (electrodynamic effect), and its rotation can transfer angular momentum to payloads, propelling them to higher orbits without using fuel.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Tether conductor — a long conductive cable that interacts with a planet's magnetic field or carries momentum. Key parts: aluminium or copper wire, high-strength synthetic fibre reinforcement, insulation jacket.
  • Plasma contactor — emits or collects electrons to complete the electrical circuit with the ionosphere. Key parts: hollow-cathode plasma source, gas supply (xenon or argon), power supply.
  • Deployment and tension control — unreels the tether from a compact spool and keeps it taut. Key parts: motorised reel, brake, tension sensors, vibration dampers.
  • Power conversion and storage — rectifies the induced voltage and delivers stable electricity. Key parts: DC-DC converter, battery, control electronics.
  • Payload interface — for momentum-exchange use, attaches and releases a payload at the correct moment. Key parts: docking grapple, release mechanism, timing sensors.
  • Attitude and orbit control — points the tether along the local vertical and manages orbital changes. Key parts: star trackers, navigation receiver, small thrusters or magnetorquers.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design tether and orbit. Choose a length of kilometres to tens of kilometres, conductor gauge, and an orbit that gives useful magnetic flux or momentum exchange.
  2. Manufacture the conductor. Braid or weave the conductor together with high-strength fibres, coat it with insulation, and test tensile strength and conductivity.
  3. Wind and package. Spool the tether on a reel under controlled tension and protect it against launch vibration.
  4. Assemble the spacecraft bus. Integrate the reel, plasma contactor, power electronics, avionics and payload interface into a small satellite or upper stage.
  5. Launch and deploy. Release the spacecraft, then slowly unreel the tether while using thrusters to control attitude and avoid tangling.
  6. Establish electrical contact. Activate the plasma contactor to close the circuit with the ionosphere, and measure current, voltage and the resulting drag or thrust.
  7. Demonstrate energy harvest. Convert the induced current into a stable bus voltage and feed it to a battery or payload.
  8. Demonstrate momentum exchange. For transport, capture a payload, spin up the tether, and release at the correct phase to reach a higher orbit.
  9. Deorbit or retrieve. Cut or reel in the tether at end of life to limit space debris.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Long, strong, lightweight tether materials — fibres and conductors exist, but no tether of the required length and durability has flown reliably.
  • PARTIAL Space plasma contactors — hollow cathodes have flown, but long-duration operation in the ionosphere is unproven.
  • EXISTS High-voltage space power electronics — satellite converters and insulation handle kilovolt levels, but need adaptation for a moving tether.
  • PARTIAL Flexible-body dynamics and control — models exist, but controlling a kilometres-long flexible tether remains difficult.
  • PARTIAL In-orbit rendezvous and capture — docking is routine, but capturing and releasing a payload on a tether end is novel.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: subscale tether flights demonstrating current generation and thrust — data on plasma contact and tether dynamics for scaling up.
  2. Next: kilowatt-class electrodynamic tether for satellite deorbit — propellant-free end-of-life disposal and limited power generation.
  3. Next: momentum-exchange tether transferring payloads between low Earth orbit and higher orbits — low-cost orbital transport without rockets.
  4. Longer term: reusable tether infrastructure at a station or depot — routine power and payload transfer, cutting launch mass for deep-space missions.
