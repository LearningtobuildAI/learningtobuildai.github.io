PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC) is a renewable energy technology that converts thermal energy from the ocean into electricity by utilizing the temperature difference between warm surface water and cold deep ocean water.

2Problem It Solves

OTEC addresses the need for sustainable, low-carbon energy sources by harnessing the consistent temperature gradient in tropical oceans, providing a potentially continuous supply of electricity without emitting greenhouse gases during operation.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

OTEC systems typically consist of two main types: closed-cycle and open-cycle. In closed-cycle OTEC, a working fluid with a low boiling point is used to drive a turbine generator. The warm surface water boils the fluid, which then condenses in cold deep seawater, driving the turbine. Open-cycle OTEC uses the ocean water directly as the working fluid, vaporizing it and driving a turbine before returning it to the sea.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves designing and constructing OTEC plants, which require specialized equipment and materials. The design must account for the unique environmental conditions at sea, including pressure changes and corrosive effects from saltwater.

7Build Process

OTEC systems are built in stages: initial planning, detailed engineering, construction, testing, and commissioning. Each stage requires careful management to ensure that the system can operate efficiently and safely in marine environments.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cold-water pipe — draws near-freezing water from 700–1000 m down; the single hardest engineering problem. Key parts: large-diameter composite or polyethylene pipe, intake screen, suspension or mooring to the platform.
  • Warm-water intake — supplies surface water at 25–30 °C in tropical seas. Key parts: pumps, screens, pipework.
  • Heat exchangers — transfer heat between seawater and a working fluid with a low boiling point. Key parts: evaporator, condenser, titanium or corrosion-resistant tube bundles.
  • Power loop — expands vapour through a turbine to make electricity. Key parts: turbine, generator, working fluid such as ammonia (closed cycle), pumps and piping.
  • Platform or shore plant — holds everything together — a moored floating hull, or a land facility on a steep coast. Key parts: hull or concrete structure, mooring lines and anchors, station-keeping controls.
  • Export and co-product systems — delivers power and, often, fresh water or cold water for other uses. Key parts: subsea power cable or onshore substation, desalination stage (open cycle), seawater air-conditioning loop.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Find a site. Map where the sea surface is around 25 °C and water near 5 °C lies within a few kilometres of shore at 800–1000 m depth — mostly tropical coasts and islands.
  2. Design the cold-water pipe. Size a pipe several metres across for the enormous flow needed, and work out how it survives currents, waves and its own weight.
  3. Fabricate heat exchangers. Build large, biofouling-resistant exchangers that keep a tiny temperature difference useful — this is where most of the cost sits.
  4. Build the platform. Construct a floating hull or a shore-based plant, plus the mooring system to hold it over the cold-water intake.
  5. Install the cold-water pipe. Lower and connect the pipe in one piece or in sections, a delicate offshore operation that has sunk past projects.
  6. Assemble the power loop. Fit turbine, generator, pumps and working-fluid charge, and pressure-test the whole circuit.
  7. Commission. Start the warm and cold flows together and bring the turbine up to speed, tuning for the small temperature difference.
  8. Operate with co-products. Run continuously while making desalinated water, or supplying cold seawater to nearby buildings, to improve the economics.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Deep-water pipe engineering — pipes have been deployed, but long-lived, survivable large-diameter cold-water pipes at full scale are unproven.
  • PARTIAL Efficient low-temperature heat exchangers — thermal efficiency is only a few percent, so heat exchangers dominate cost and must resist fouling.
  • EXISTS Offshore mooring and installation — the oil and gas industry already moors and installs large structures in deep water.
  • EXISTS Subsea power cables — floating or shore plants need a reliable connection to the grid.
  • EXISTS Tropical deep-water sites — the resource is fixed by geography — a temperature difference of at least about 20 °C is needed.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: small pilot plants of tens to a few hundred kilowatts, mainly experimental — operating experience with cold-water pipes and exchangers.
  2. Next: 1–10 MW pilot plants combined with desalination or cooling — a revenue stream that partly pays for the very high capital cost.
  3. Next: 10–100 MW plants, likely shore-based on island coasts — firm power for tropical islands now running on diesel.
  4. Longer term: large floating plants producing power, fresh water, hydrogen or ammonia — steady baseload energy that does not depend on weather.

18Glossary
Working fluid
A substance used to transfer heat energy from warm water to a turbine in an OTEC system.
Closed-cycle OTEC
An OTEC process where the working fluid is recycled within the system, typically using a low-boiling-point liquid like ammonia or propane.
Open-cycle OTEC
An OTEC process that uses seawater directly as the working fluid, which is vaporized and then condensed back into water for return to the ocean.
Thermal gradient
The temperature difference between surface water and deep ocean water used in OTEC systems to generate electricity.
