PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Concentrating Solar Power (CSP) is a technology that uses mirrors to concentrate solar energy onto a small area, heating a fluid like molten salt. This heat can be stored and used later to generate electricity through a conventional steam turbine.

2Problem It Solves

CSP addresses the intermittent nature of solar power by providing a means to store thermal energy for dispatch during periods when sunlight is not available or insufficient, such as at night or during cloudy conditions. This allows for 24/7 baseload power generation in sunny regions.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Heliostats, which are mirrors that track the sun, direct sunlight towards a central receiver located on a tower or in a field. The receiver contains molten salt that absorbs the concentrated solar energy, heating it to high temperatures (typically around 565°C). This heated salt can be stored and used later to generate steam by passing through heat exchangers, which then drives a turbine to produce electricity.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves producing and assembling heliostats, receivers, heat exchangers, and associated components. Materials like steel, glass, and specialized alloys are used to construct these parts.

7Build Process

Construction of a CSP plant includes site preparation, installation of heliostat fields, the receiver tower or field, and the power block including turbines and storage tanks for molten salt. The entire system must be carefully calibrated to ensure efficient operation.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Available today

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Solar field — captures direct sunlight and concentrates it onto a receiver. Key parts: heliostats (mirrors on two-axis trackers) or parabolic troughs, support structures, tracking drives.
  • Receiver — absorbs concentrated light and transfers the heat to a fluid. Key parts: absorber tubes or panels, selective coatings, insulation.
  • Thermal energy storage — stores hot fluid so electricity can be made after sunset. Key parts: hot and cold molten-salt tanks, pumps, piping, insulation.
  • Power block — converts stored heat into electricity. Key parts: heat exchanger or steam generator, steam turbine, generator, condenser and cooling system.
  • Control system — aims the mirrors, manages heat flows and protects equipment. Key parts: direct-normal-irradiance sensors, temperature sensors, tracking controllers, plant software.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Assess the site. Measure direct normal irradiance (DNI) for at least a year and check terrain, water access and grid connection.
  2. Design the plant. Choose tower or trough technology, size the thermal storage, and lay out the mirror field.
  3. Manufacture components. Make mirrors, heliostats or trough modules, receivers, storage tanks and the turbine.
  4. Prepare the site. Build foundations, access roads, tank bases and the power-block buildings.
  5. Install the solar field. Mount heliostats or troughs on their trackers and connect their control wiring.
  6. Build the receiver. For a power tower, erect the tower and fit the receiver at the top; for troughs, assemble the loops and piping.
  7. Install storage and power block. Erect molten-salt tanks, heat exchangers, the steam turbine, generator and cooling system.
  8. Commission. Melt and circulate salt, test tracking and heat transfer, then synchronise the turbine to the grid.
  9. Operate and maintain. Clean mirrors, control salt chemistry, and service the turbine and tracking systems.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-temperature thermal storage — molten-salt tanks store heat for many hours, making the plant dispatchable.
  • EXISTS Concentrating solar receivers — they must withstand high light flux and repeated thermal cycling.
  • EXISTS Steam turbine and generator — a conventional power block turns the stored heat into electricity.
  • PARTIAL Dry cooling — it cuts water use in deserts but reduces efficiency, especially on hot days.
  • PARTIAL Dispatchable grid integration — the plant must ramp output to match demand and support grid stability.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: commercial plants with molten-salt storage in Spain, Morocco, South Africa, the UAE and the US — firm solar power that can run into the evening peak.
  2. Next: hybrid CSP–PV plants sharing a grid connection — cheaper daytime power from PV, with CSP storage firming the evening.
  3. Next: CSP heat for industry and green fuels — displacing fossil heat in mining, cement and chemical production.
  4. Longer term: advanced power cycles and higher-temperature storage — lower cost per kilowatt-hour and wider deployment.

18Glossary
Heliostat
A mirror that reflects sunlight onto a central receiver, tracking the sun's position throughout the day.
Receiver
A device where concentrated solar energy is absorbed and converted into heat, typically using molten salt as the heat transfer fluid.
Molten Salt
A mixture of sodium nitrate and potassium nitrate used in CSP systems to store thermal energy at high temperatures for later use.
