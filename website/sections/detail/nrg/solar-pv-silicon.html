PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Available today

Written by Claude (claude-opus-5-5) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Silicon cells — turn sunlight into electric current at a p–n junction. Key parts: monocrystalline wafer, doped emitter layer, surface passivation, anti-reflective coating, printed metal contacts.
  • Module package — protects the cells outdoors for 25–30+ years. Key parts: tempered glass, encapsulant film, backsheet or rear glass, aluminium frame, junction box with bypass diodes.
  • Mounting — holds modules at the right angle; single-axis trackers follow the sun for more energy. Key parts: piles or ballast, racking, tracker motors and controllers.
  • Inverters and power electronics — convert DC to grid-compatible AC and keep each string at its maximum power point. Key parts: string or central inverters, MPPT controllers, transformers.
  • Balance of system — wiring, protection and monitoring that tie it together. Key parts: DC and AC cabling, combiner boxes, breakers and surge protection, SCADA monitoring.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Purify the silicon. Quartz is reduced to metallurgical silicon in an arc furnace, then refined into polysilicon of extreme purity — mostly by the Siemens chemical-vapour process.
  2. Grow crystal ingots. Polysilicon is melted with a trace of dopant and a single crystal is slowly pulled from the melt (the Czochralski method).
  3. Slice wafers. Diamond-wire saws cut the ingot into wafers roughly 0.15 mm thick; thinner wafers save silicon but crack more easily.
  4. Make the cells. The surface is etched into tiny pyramids to trap light, dopants are diffused in to form the junction, passivation and anti-reflective layers are deposited, and silver contacts are screen-printed and fired.
  5. Test and sort. Every cell is flash-tested and binned by output so matched cells go into the same module.
  6. Assemble modules. Cells are soldered into strings, laminated between glass and encapsulant under heat and vacuum, then framed and fitted with a junction box.
  7. Quality-check modules. A flash test sets the power rating and electroluminescence imaging reveals micro-cracks invisible to the eye.
  8. Build the farm. Piles and racking go in, modules are mounted and wired into strings, strings feed inverters, and a substation connects the farm to the grid.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-purity polysilicon refining — cells need silicon far purer than any other industry except chips.
  • EXISTS Semiconductor doping and thin-film deposition — they create the junction and the coatings that make a wafer into a cell.
  • EXISTS Diamond-wire wafer sawing — it cut the silicon lost as sawdust and was a major reason module prices fell.
  • EXISTS Grid-tie inverters — without them the DC output cannot be fed into an AC grid safely.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: older PERC cells give way to TOPCon and heterojunction designs — higher efficiency from better surface passivation, on the same production lines.
  2. Next few years: back-contact cells and bifacial modules on trackers — more energy per hectare, which matters as good land gets scarcer.
  3. Next: perovskite-on-silicon tandem cells — efficiencies beyond the roughly 29% ceiling of a single silicon junction — once perovskite durability is proven outdoors.
  4. Longer term: less silver per cell and routine module recycling — growth without hitting a silver shortage or a waste problem at end of life.

18Glossary
p–n junction
A semiconductor interface where the p-type (positive) and n-type (negative) regions meet to facilitate the flow of current.
anti-reflective coating
A thin layer applied to the surface of solar cells to minimize light reflection, increasing absorption and efficiency.
