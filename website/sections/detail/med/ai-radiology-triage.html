PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

AI Radiology Triage systems utilize machine learning models to analyze medical images, such as X-rays, CT scans, and MRIs. These systems are designed to identify urgent findings that require immediate attention from radiologists or other healthcare professionals.

2Problem It Solves

AI Radiology Triage addresses the challenge of timely diagnosis by reducing the time-to-diagnosis for conditions like strokes and cancers. By automating the initial screening process, these systems can flag urgent cases that might otherwise be overlooked or delayed due to backlogs in radiology departments.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These AI systems process large volumes of imaging data using deep learning algorithms trained on extensive datasets with labeled examples of critical findings. The models generate scores for each image indicating the likelihood of containing urgent medical information, which helps prioritize studies and route them more efficiently to radiologists based on their urgency level.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves developing and training machine learning models on large datasets of medical images. This requires significant computational resources and expertise in both AI development and medical imaging. The hardware used includes GPUs for training, cloud services for storage and processing power, and specialized software tools for model development and deployment.

7Build Process

Building an AI Radiology Triage system involves several steps: data collection (including obtaining labeled datasets), model selection and training, validation through clinical trials, integration with existing radiology workflows, and ongoing monitoring to ensure performance and compliance with regulatory standards.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Image ingestion — gets every study from the scanner to the model without breaking the hospital's systems. Key parts: DICOM receiver and DICOMweb client, PACS and modality worklist interface, de-identification and protocol normalisation.
  • Model core — scores a scan for the target finding and outputs a probability plus a localisation. Key parts: convolutional or transformer network, segmentation head for lesion extent, calibration layer turning scores into usable probabilities.
  • Ground truth and annotation — produces the labels the model learns from and the reference it is judged against. Key parts: radiologist reads, pathology or follow-up outcome linkage, adjudication of disagreements.
  • Inference and integration — runs the model fast enough to matter and writes the result back into the clinical workflow. Key parts: on-premises GPU server or cloud inference, HL7 FHIR / worklist priority flag, notification to the stroke or cancer team.
  • Quality and governance — keeps the model honest after deployment. Key parts: drift and performance dashboards, audit logs per study, radiologist override capture, version control and change approval.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the clinical claim. Decide exactly what the tool detects, in which patients, and what action it should change — for example flagging a suspected large-vessel stroke for immediate review. A vague claim cannot be validated or regulated.
  2. Assemble the training set. Collect studies with linked reports, and where possible biopsy or follow-up outcomes, then de-identify and normalise them across scanners, doses and protocols.
  3. Label and adjudicate. Radiologists annotate each study; disagreements are resolved by a panel so the labels are reproducible rather than one person's opinion.
  4. Train and tune. Train the network, hold out a test set, and tune the operating threshold to the sensitivity the clinical use demands — usually high sensitivity with acceptable false positives.
  5. Validate internally, then externally. Test on unseen data from the same hospital, then on entirely separate sites with different scanners and populations, reporting performance by age, sex and ethnicity.
  6. Run a reader study. Compare radiologists alone against radiologists assisted by the model to show the tool actually improves the reading, not just matches it.
  7. Clear the regulator. Submit for clearance or approval with a locked model version, defined intended use and labelling, and a post-market surveillance plan.
  8. Deploy in silent mode. Run the model live but hide its output, so real-world performance and integration can be measured without affecting patients.
  9. Switch on and monitor. Activate priority flags, log every override, and watch for drift as scanners, protocols and patient mix change.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Large labelled medical imaging datasets — labels are expensive, rare for some findings, and often skewed to a few hospitals.
  • EXISTS Cheap parallel compute — training and low-latency inference both depend on GPUs.
  • EXISTS Imaging interoperability standards — DICOM and HL7 FHIR let a model plug into existing scanners and worklists instead of new ones.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory routes for clinical AI — clearance pathways exist, but rules for continuously learning models are still maturing.
  • PARTIAL Radiologist workflow acceptance — if the flag arrives in a different screen than the report, it is ignored.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: single-finding triage for emergencies such as intracranial haemorrhage and suspected large-vessel stroke — faster treatment times where minutes change outcomes.
  2. Next: multi-finding models and screening programmes, including mammography and chest CT — use where volume, not urgency, is the bottleneck.
  3. Next: prospective randomised evidence that AI-assisted reading improves patient outcomes — reimbursement and routine adoption rather than pilot funding.
  4. Longer term: autonomous reporting for narrow, low-risk tasks under radiologist oversight — relief for severe workforce shortages, with humans focused on hard cases.

18Glossary
Deep learning
A subset of machine learning that uses neural networks with multiple layers to model and solve complex problems, particularly in pattern recognition tasks such as image analysis.
Machine learning models
Statistical models that can learn patterns from data without being explicitly programmed. In the context of AI Radiology Triage, these models are trained on large datasets to recognize urgent findings in medical images.
Medical image datasets
Collections of digital images and their corresponding metadata used for training machine learning algorithms. These datasets often include a wide range of imaging modalities such as X-rays, CT scans, MRIs, etc., along with annotations indicating the presence or absence of specific findings.
Radiology workflow
The series of steps involved in the process of acquiring and interpreting medical images. This includes image acquisition, storage, distribution, interpretation by radiologists, and reporting to clinicians for patient care decisions.
