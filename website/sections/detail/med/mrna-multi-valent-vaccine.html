PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Antigen selection — decides which strains or pathogens the dose must cover. Key parts: global genomic surveillance, computational strain prediction, sequence optimisation (codon usage, untranslated regions, signal peptides).
  • mRNA drug substance — enzymatically copies a DNA template into messenger RNA. Key parts: linear DNA template, RNA polymerase and nucleotide mix (with modified nucleoside), capping and tailing enzymes, chromatography and filtration purification.
  • Lipid nanoparticle — packages and protects the mRNA and delivers it into cells. Key parts: ionizable lipid, helper phospholipid, cholesterol, PEG-lipid, microfluidic mixing.
  • Multi-antigen formulation — blends several distinct mRNAs into a single stable dose. Key parts: in-line mixers, ratio and particle-size control, lyophilisation or frozen storage.
  • Fill and cold chain — puts the dose into vials and keeps it potent to the point of use. Key parts: sterile filling lines, vials and stoppers, refrigerated or frozen distribution.
  • Release analytics — proves every antigen is present in the right amount and folds correctly. Key parts: encapsulation-efficiency assays, antigen expression tests, particle sizing.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the targets. Surveillance networks sequence circulating viruses; models estimate which strains are likely to dominate the coming season, or which pathogens to combine.
  2. Design each mRNA. For every chosen antigen a sequence is written and optimised so human cells translate it efficiently, and so the encoded protein keeps its shape.
  3. Make the DNA templates. Each sequence is synthesised and cloned into a plasmid, then cut or amplified to give a clean linear template.
  4. Transcribe the mRNAs. RNA polymerase copies each template in a separate reaction, using a modified nucleoside in place of uridine to reduce unwanted immune reactions; a cap and tail are added.
  5. Purify each mRNA. Chromatography and filtration strip out DNA, enzymes and truncated transcripts, because impurities scale with the number of antigens in the dose.
  6. Formulate the particles. Lipids dissolved in ethanol are mixed with mRNA in water through a microfluidic mixer, causing the RNA to be encapsulated as the lipids precipitate into nanoparticles.
  7. Blend and match ratios. Where several mRNAs are combined, longer and shorter sequences encapsulate at different rates, so the mix must be measured and corrected until each antigen is present in the intended proportion.
  8. Fill, freeze and release. The batch is sterile-filtered into vials, frozen or refrigerated, and tested before release for identity, potency and purity of every component.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Modified-nucleoside mRNA platform — without it, injected mRNA triggers strong innate immune reactions and is destroyed before it can be translated.
  • EXISTS Lipid nanoparticle delivery — naked mRNA cannot enter cells; the ionizable lipid is what carries it across the cell membrane.
  • EXISTS Rapid sequence-to-batch manufacturing — the whole point of the platform is producing a new combination in weeks rather than months.
  • PARTIAL Co-encapsulation of multiple mRNAs at controlled ratios — producing one mRNA per particle is routine; keeping several antigens in fixed proportion in one dose is harder.
  • PARTIAL Rapid regulatory pathways for strain changes — combination vaccines covering several pathogens need fresh clinical evidence even on a proven platform.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: strain-updated mRNA COVID boosters encoding two variants, and multivalent seasonal influenza candidates in late-stage trials — proof that more than one antigen can be delivered in a single mRNA dose.
  2. Next: combined vaccines against several unrelated pathogens in one injection, with positive phase 3 results reported in 2024 — fewer appointments, higher coverage, especially for older adults.
  3. Next: co-formulation of more than a handful of antigens without ratio drift — broad protection against whole virus families rather than single strains.
  4. Longer term: rapid-response libraries where a new pathogen is added to an existing multivalent dose within weeks — outbreak response that runs ahead of a virus rather than chasing it.
