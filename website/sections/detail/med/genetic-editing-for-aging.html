PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Target discovery — finds genes where a change causally shifts the rate of ageing, rather than merely correlating with it. Key parts: human genetics of long-lived families and rare variants, genome-wide CRISPR screens in cells and organoids, animal ageing models.
  • Editing machinery — makes the intended DNA change with as few unintended ones as possible. Key parts: Cas9 nickase or base editors that avoid double-strand breaks, prime editors for insertions and small rewrites, guide RNA design and optimisation.
  • Delivery — gets the editor into the right organ and cell type, and nowhere else. Key parts: lipid nanoparticles (well suited to liver), engineered AAV capsids, tissue-specific promoters and control elements.
  • Durability and control — editing is permanent, so the dose cannot be adjusted later. Key parts: transient editor expression, self-limiting or switchable constructs, dose-finding in animals.
  • Safety and immunology — detects off-target edits and manages immune reactions to the editing proteins. Key parts: whole-genome off-target sequencing, immunogenicity assays, long-term tumour surveillance.
  • Outcome measurement — replaces 'lifespan' with endpoints a trial can actually measure. Key parts: epigenetic clocks and other ageing biomarkers, frailty, organ-function and disease-free-survival measures.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Find a causal target. Search human genetics and CRISPR screens for variants that change disease risk or ageing pace, then confirm the effect by editing the gene in human cells and organoids.
  2. Design the editor. Choose the smallest effective change — often a single base — and pick an editor that introduces no double-strand break, since breaks invite rearrangements.
  3. Validate in cells. Measure on-target efficiency, then sequence the whole genome to count off-target edits before anything enters an animal.
  4. Choose a delivery route. Lipid nanoparticles suit the liver; other organs need engineered viral vectors or new chemistries, tested for where they actually end up.
  5. Test in animals. Premature-ageing and naturally ageing mice are dosed and followed for healthspan and lifespan, with tissues examined for tumours.
  6. Run safety studies. Long-term dosing studies check immune reactions, organ damage and any sign of cancer before a regulator will allow a human dose.
  7. Trial in a severe single-gene disease first. The first plausible use is a rare premature-ageing disorder such as Hutchinson–Gilford progeria syndrome, where the cause is one mutation and the benefit is clear.
  8. Extend to age-related disease. If safe, the same machinery is aimed at a common condition driven partly by one gene — for example, permanently lowering cholesterol by editing a liver gene.
  9. Only then attempt rate-of-ageing. Any attempt to slow ageing itself would combine several targets at once, guided by biomarkers rather than lifespan, which no trial can measure in reasonable time.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Precise editors with minimal off-target effects — base and prime editors avoid double-strand breaks, but off-target edits still occur and are hard to rule out completely.
  • PARTIAL Delivery to organs beyond the liver — lipid nanoparticles reach the liver well; heart, muscle, brain and fat — where ageing acts — remain difficult.
  • NOT YET Causal, druggable ageing targets — ageing is polygenic and no single edit is known to lengthen human life.
  • NOT YET Acceptable long-term safety record — a permanent edit given to healthy people must be near-zero-risk, and decades of follow-up do not yet exist.
  • NOT YET Regulatory and trial endpoints for ageing — regulators do not approve treatments for ageing as such, only for specific diseases.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: the first CRISPR therapy was approved in 2023 for sickle-cell disease and beta-thalassaemia — proof that a permanent edit can safely correct a human disease outside the body.
  2. Next: in-body editing trials for common conditions such as high cholesterol — evidence that editors can be delivered safely inside patients, not just in a laboratory dish.
  3. Next: editing in a premature-ageing disorder — the first safety and biomarker data linking a genetic edit to ageing biology in people.
  4. Longer term: combination edits guided by ageing biomarkers — a possible slowing of age-related decline — speculative, and dependent on solving delivery and safety first.
