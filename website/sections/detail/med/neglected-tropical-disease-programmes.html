PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD) elimination refers to the process of reducing the incidence and prevalence of NTDs, such as lymphatic filariasis, trachoma, and river blindness, to levels that are no longer considered public health problems. This is achieved through mass drug administration (MDA), vector control measures, and disease surveillance.

4How It Works

NTD elimination programs involve the widespread distribution of antiparasitic drugs to at-risk populations to prevent infection. Vector control measures such as insecticide-treated bed nets and indoor residual spraying are used to reduce transmission. Surveillance systems track disease prevalence and monitor program effectiveness.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves producing antiparasitic drugs on a large scale. Vector control devices like bed nets are mass-produced using textile manufacturing processes.

7Build Process

The build process includes drug formulation, packaging, and distribution logistics for MDA programs. For vector control, it involves fabricating and distributing bed nets and other equipment.

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. Surveillance systems require minimal power for data collection but can be battery-operated in remote areas.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Preventive chemotherapy drugs — single-dose tablets that clear or suppress parasites in whole populations without individual diagnosis. Key parts: ivermectin (onchocerciasis, lymphatic filariasis), albendazole (lymphatic filariasis, soil-transmitted helminths), diethylcarbamazine (lymphatic filariasis), azithromycin (trachoma), praziquantel (schistosomiasis).
  • Drug manufacture and supply — produce tablets at the millions-of-doses scale a national campaign needs. Key parts: active-ingredient synthesis, tablet compression and blistering, quality testing, donation programmes and pooled procurement.
  • Diagnostics and mapping — find where the disease still circulates and decide when to stop treating. Key parts: rapid antigen tests, microscopy and skin snips, WHO-graded clinical surveys, molecular tests, transmission assessment surveys.
  • Vector and intermediate-host control — block transmission by attacking the insects and snails that carry the parasite. Key parts: insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, tsetse traps, snail control.
  • Community delivery — get tablets to everyone at risk, often where there are few clinics. Key parts: trained community drug distributors, household registers, directly observed dosing, school-based delivery.
  • Surveillance and verification — prove elimination holds and detect any rebound. Key parts: case-based reporting, mosquito and snail testing for parasite DNA, WHO verification dossiers.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Map the burden. Surveys establish which districts are endemic and how intense transmission is, so treatment is targeted rather than blanket.
  2. Secure the drugs. National programmes draw on donated or low-cost tablets and forecast the number of doses needed district by district.
  3. Recruit and train distributors. Community health workers and volunteers are trained to dose by weight or height, record who was treated and manage side effects.
  4. Run mass treatment rounds. Whole at-risk populations are dosed annually or twice-yearly, aiming to treat at least 65% of them, until transmission falls below a threshold.
  5. Add vector control. Nets, spraying or snail control are layered on where drugs alone will not interrupt transmission.
  6. Monitor coverage and impact. Coverage surveys and repeated infection surveys show whether rounds are working.
  7. Assess whether to stop. Transmission assessment surveys in children test whether new infections have stopped; passing them allows treatment to be halted.
  8. Switch to surveillance. After treatment stops, the programme keeps testing people, mosquitoes or snails so any resurgence is caught early.
  9. Verify elimination. A national dossier is reviewed externally, and the country is certified free of transmission as a public health problem.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Drugs safe enough to give whole populations without diagnosis — mass treatment only works if the medicine is cheap, single-dose and has very low risk of harm.
  • EXISTS Field-ready rapid diagnostic tests — programmes need to measure infection in remote villages without laboratories.
  • EXISTS Insecticides, nets and spraying equipment — for vector-borne NTDs, drugs alone often cannot break transmission.
  • PARTIAL Drugs that kill adult worms — most current medicines kill larvae; adult worms survive and restart transmission, so many years of rounds are needed.
  • NOT YET Vaccines against NTDs — there is no licensed vaccine for most of these diseases, so control rests on drugs and vectors.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: national mass-treatment programmes reaching around a billion people a year in over 100 countries — sharp falls in infection in many districts, and in some countries elimination.
  2. Next: countries finishing the last treatment rounds and passing transmission assessment — verified elimination status, as achieved for trachoma in Ghana in 2018 and lymphatic filariasis in Sri Lanka in 2016, and a shift of budgets to surveillance.
  3. Next: new regimens and tools for the hardest settings, including triple-drug therapy for lymphatic filariasis and safer options where ivermectin cannot be used — faster interruption of transmission in conflict zones and co-endemic areas.
  4. Longer term: post-elimination surveillance sustained across whole regions — keeping diseases from returning and freeing health systems for other work.

18Glossary
Neglected Tropical Disease (NTD)
A group of tropical diseases that are primarily found in resource-poor settings and have a significant impact on public health.
Mass Drug Administration (MDA)
The large-scale distribution of antiparasitic drugs to at-risk populations to prevent infection with NTDs.
Vector Control
Strategies to reduce the population of disease-carrying insects, such as mosquitoes and flies, which transmit NTDs.
