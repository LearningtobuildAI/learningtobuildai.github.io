PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Organ-on-Chip technology involves creating microscale models of human organs that can be used for various biomedical applications, including drug discovery. These chips contain channels and chambers that mimic the structure and function of specific human organs.

Category
Medicine
Best use
Drug testing, toxicity screening
Stage
FAR
2Problem It Solves

Traditional drug discovery methods often rely on animal models or in vitro cell cultures, which can lead to inaccurate predictions of a drug's efficacy and safety in humans. Organ-on-Chip technology provides a more accurate representation of human physiology, improving the relevance and reliability of preclinical testing.

3Lifecycle / Journey Stage
early commercial
PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Organ-on-Chip devices use microfluidics to replicate the complex interactions within living tissues by incorporating multiple cell types into a microfabricated platform. Fluids containing nutrients, oxygen, and other necessary components are passed through these channels to simulate the physiological environment of the organ being modeled. This allows for the study of drug effects on individual organs without the need for animal testing or human trials.

5Materials Used
  • polymer
  • glass
  • biomaterials
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Organ-on-Chip devices are typically manufactured using microfabrication techniques such as photolithography and soft lithography to create microchannels and chambers within a polymer or glass substrate. These substrates can be coated with specific biomaterials to support cell growth and maintain the desired organ-specific microenvironment.

7Build Process

The process involves designing the chip layout, fabricating the microfluidic channels and chambers, coating them with appropriate materials, and integrating various cell types into the device. Cells are often seeded onto the surface of the chip and allowed to grow and differentiate before testing can begin.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Microfluidic chip — contains channels and chambers that mimic the microscopic architecture of an organ. Key parts: PDMS or thermoplastic layers, inlet and outlet ports, micropumps and valves, glass or plastic substrate.
  • Cell and tissue construct — provides the living human cells that form the functional organ model. Key parts: induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) or primary cells, organ-specific differentiated cells, supporting cells such as endothelial and fibroblast cells, extracellular matrix gel (e.g. collagen, Matrigel).
  • Perfusion system — supplies nutrients, removes waste, and applies mechanical forces like shear stress or stretch. Key parts: syringe or pressure pumps, tubing and connectors, bubble traps, medium reservoirs.
  • Sensing and readout — measures tissue function and response to drugs in real time. Key parts: embedded electrodes for transepithelial electrical resistance, optical sensors and microscopy, effluent sampling for mass spectrometry, gene expression and omics analysis.
  • Environmental control — maintains physiological conditions for long-term cell culture. Key parts: incubator housing, temperature control, gas mixing for CO2 and O2, humidity control.
  • Fluidic automation — enables reproducible, programmable operation of the chip. Key parts: software for flow control, robotic liquid handling, valve manifolds, data logging.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the chip. Use computer-aided design to lay out micrometre-scale channels, chambers and membranes that replicate the organ's key structures and fluid flow paths.
  2. Fabricate the chip. Create a master mould by photolithography, then cast polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) or injection-mould thermoplastics to form the chip layers. Bond the layers to a glass or plastic base to seal the channels.
  3. Prepare the cells. Differentiate induced pluripotent stem cells into the desired organ-specific cell types, or isolate primary cells. Expand and quality-check them for viability and markers.
  4. Seed and form tissue. Introduce cells into the chip together with an extracellular matrix gel. Allow them to self-organise into 3D structures such as tubules, villi or spheroids, often with endothelial cells lining a parallel channel.
  5. Culture and mature. Perfuse culture medium through the chip for days to weeks under controlled temperature and gas. Apply mechanical cues (e.g. fluid shear or cyclic stretch) to promote maturation.
  6. Integrate sensors and automation. Connect pumps, sensors and environmental controls. Program the perfusion and sampling schedule so the model operates reproducibly without manual intervention.
  7. Validate the organ model. Compare the chip's behaviour against known human organ function: barrier integrity, enzyme activity, gene expression, and responses to reference drugs.
  8. Test drug candidates. Expose the model to drug compounds at various doses and time courses. Measure efficacy, toxicity and metabolic changes using the integrated readouts.
  9. Analyse and interpret data. Combine imaging, sensor and omics data to assess the drug's effect. Use the results to prioritise compounds or flag safety risks before animal or human trials.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) — provide a renewable, patient-specific source of human cells that can be differentiated into many organ cell types.
  • EXISTS Microfluidic fabrication — soft lithography, injection moulding and bonding techniques create the precise microchannels and chambers needed for organ models.
  • PARTIAL Biomaterials and extracellular matrices — mimicking the stiffness, porosity and biochemical cues of different organs remains challenging and is not fully solved for all tissues.
  • PARTIAL Organ-specific differentiation protocols — for many organs, we cannot yet produce fully mature and functional cells in vitro, limiting how predictive the models are.
  • PARTIAL Integrated sensors and analytics — continuous, non-invasive monitoring of tissue health and drug response requires better miniaturised sensors and automated sampling.
  • NOT YET Regulatory acceptance — drug regulators have not yet qualified organ-on-chip models as validated replacements for animal testing, so they are used mainly as supportive evidence.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: single-organ chips (e.g. liver, kidney, heart) used in drug discovery research — earlier, human-relevant prediction of drug toxicity and efficacy, reducing late-stage failures.
  2. Next: multi-organ 'body-on-a-chip' systems linking several organ models — study of drug metabolism and systemic effects, giving a more realistic picture of how a drug moves through the body.
  3. Next: patient-derived cells integrated into chips for personalised testing — selection of the most effective drug for an individual patient before treatment begins.
  4. Longer term: regulatory qualification of organ-on-chip models as alternatives to animal testing — faster, cheaper and more ethical drug development with fewer animal experiments.
  5. Longer term: standard integration into the pharmaceutical pipeline from discovery to preclinical safety — lower attrition rates and safer medicines reaching patients.
