9Engineering Breakdown
  • Brain mapping (connectomics) — produces a wiring diagram of every neuron and synapse — the 'parts list' of a mind. Key parts: electron microscopes, tissue slicing and staining, automated image segmentation, synapse-level reconstruction software.
  • Functional recording — measures what those neurons are actually doing, not just how they are wired. Key parts: implanted electrode arrays, calcium and voltage imaging, functional MRI and MEG (coarse, external).
  • Implant and data link — would carry a very large number of channels in and out of living tissue. Key parts: electrode arrays, amplifiers, wireless telemetry, power supply.
  • Computational substrate — a machine able to run a full brain model in real time. Key parts: high-performance computers, neuron and synapse models, acceleration hardware.
  • Embodiment and interface — gives the simulated mind senses and a way to act. Key parts: sensor feeds, motor outputs, virtual or robotic body.
  • Verification — tests whether the copy behaves like the original — the open scientific problem. Key parts: behavioural tests, memory and personality measures.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Map a whole brain at synapse resolution. Slice tissue into nanometre-thin sections, image each one, and stitch the images into a 3D wiring diagram. A fruit-fly brain has been done; a mouse is in progress; a human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons and is thousands of times larger again.
  2. Record a living brain's activity. Place electrodes or optical sensors across the whole brain and log the firing of every neuron over time. Today's arrays reach thousands of channels in animals; a human brain has billions of neurons.
  3. Build biophysically detailed neuron models. Turn each cell type into equations describing how it integrates inputs and fires — the same approach used in simulation projects for cortical columns and small nervous systems.
  4. Connect the model to the map. Wire the simulated neurons together exactly as the connectome says, then tune the model until its activity patterns resemble the recorded ones.
  5. Run it in real time. Simulate the whole network faster than biological time on a large computer cluster — today this is possible only for tiny nervous systems or small brain regions.
  6. Validate the copy. Compare behaviour, memory and responses with the original. If they diverge, the model is wrong; no one yet knows what level of agreement would count as success.
  7. Establish a read-out path. Only then could a high-bandwidth implant in a living person feed the model continuously, so the simulation reflects a particular individual rather than a generic brain.
  8. Provide a body and a life. Give the simulation sensors and outputs so it can interact with the world, and decide — a legal and ethical question, not an engineering one — what status a copy has.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Whole-human-brain connectomics — mapping one human brain at synaptic resolution is far beyond current microscope, storage and compute capacity.
  • NOT YET Non-destructive recording of all neurons — there is no way to read every neuron in a living human brain without damaging it.
  • NOT YET Real-time whole-brain simulation — no computer comes close to running 86 billion detailed neurons at biological speed.
  • PARTIAL High-bandwidth neural implants — research systems record from hundreds to thousands of neurons in people; this is many orders of magnitude short.
  • NOT YET A testable account of consciousness — without one, there is no way to tell whether a simulation is a mind or just a very good imitation of one.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: small-brain connectomes and implants that restore limited function, such as movement or speech prostheses — tools and methods that larger mapping and recording efforts depend on.
  2. Next: a complete mouse brain map plus large-scale activity recording — a test bed for comparing a simulation against the real animal.
  3. Next: real-time simulation of a small mammalian brain that behaves recognisably like the animal — evidence that the modelling approach scales at all.
  4. Longer term: scanning and simulating a human brain — the technical precondition for any claim of uploading — and, separately, hard questions about whether the copy is the person.
