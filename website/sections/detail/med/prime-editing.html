PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Targeting module — finds the exact DNA address and nicks just one strand. Key parts: Cas9 nickase (H840A), nuclear localisation signals.
  • Writing module — copies a new sequence from an RNA template into the DNA. Key parts: engineered reverse transcriptase, linker peptide joining the two proteins.
  • pegRNA — the guide that both addresses the site and carries the replacement text. Key parts: spacer complementary to target DNA, scaffold that binds Cas9, primer binding site, reverse transcriptase template.
  • Nick guidance and repair tuning — a second cut or a repair-suppressing protein biases the cell toward keeping the edit. Key parts: nicking guide RNA (PE3), mismatch repair inhibitor (PE4/PE5).
  • Delivery vehicle — gets the editor into the right cells and no others. Key parts: lipid nanoparticle, engineered virus capsid, electroporation for cells removed from the body, ribonucleoprotein or mRNA payload.
  • Cell repair machinery — the host's own flap and ligation enzymes actually finish the edit. Key parts: flap endonucleases, DNA ligase, mismatch repair pathway.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the edit. Decide the exact bases to change — a point mutation, a small insertion or a small deletion — and check what else in the genome looks similar.
  2. Design the pegRNA. A software model picks the spacer, the primer binding site that will grip the nicked strand, and the template that spells out the new sequence.
  3. Make the editor. The nickase and reverse transcriptase genes are fused into one construct, produced as protein or as mRNA in a manufacturing facility.
  4. Package and deliver. For cells taken from a patient the payload goes in by electroporation; for treatment inside the body it is wrapped in lipid nanoparticles or a viral capsid that targets a chosen tissue.
  5. Nick and prime. Inside the nucleus the complex binds its target and cuts only the strand carrying the PAM; the freed 3' end pairs with the pegRNA's primer binding site.
  6. Write the new sequence. The reverse transcriptase extends that 3' end using the pegRNA template, building a flap of DNA that contains the desired edit.
  7. Resolve the flap. The cell's own enzymes equilibrate the new and old flaps, remove the unedited one and ligate the edited strand into place.
  8. Bias the outcome. A second nick on the opposite strand, or temporary suppression of mismatch repair, makes the cell keep the edited version rather than reverting it.
  9. Verify. Targeted and whole-genome sequencing confirms the intended edit and checks for unintended changes before any clinical use.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS CRISPR-Cas9 programmable targeting — prime editing reuses the same address-finding machinery, adapted so it nicks rather than cuts both strands.
  • EXISTS Reverse transcriptase enzymes — the writing step is an RNA-to-DNA copy, an enzyme class borrowed from retroviruses and engineered for accuracy.
  • EXISTS Synthetic guide RNA manufacturing — a pegRNA is a longer, more structured guide than standard CRISPR guide RNA and must be made to high purity.
  • PARTIAL Safe delivery to specific tissues — editing the right cells inside the body, especially outside the liver, is the main obstacle to treating most diseases.
  • PARTIAL Control of cellular DNA repair — the cell decides whether to keep the edit, so efficiency depends on understanding flap and mismatch repair.
  • PARTIAL Long-term safety data on genome editors — off-target edits and large rearrangements must be ruled out over years, not months.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: prime editing used widely as a research tool in cells, organoids and animals; base editing and first-generation CRISPR therapies already reaching patients — proof that precise, break-free editing works, and a regulatory path for edited cell therapies.
  2. Next: first human trials of prime-edited cells for blood and immune disorders — real safety and durability data in people, using ex vivo editing where delivery is easiest.
  3. Next: in vivo prime editing of liver and other organs, and larger edits via twin prime editing or integrase-assisted insertion — one-off treatments for metabolic and genetic liver disease.
  4. Longer term: multi-edit and organ-targeted treatments at scale — correcting several mutations at once, and reaching tissues such as muscle, lung or nerve where delivery is still unsolved.
