Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Robot body and effector — performs the therapeutic action at cellular scale. Key parts: DNA origami or polymer scaffold, magnetic or catalytic core, drug reservoir, cutting or heating element.
  • Propulsion and steering — moves robots through tissue and blood using external fields or chemical gradients. Key parts: magnetic field coils, ultrasound transducers, asymmetric catalytic surface.
  • Swarm control and localisation — coordinates many units and tracks them inside the body. Key parts: real-time imaging (MRI, ultrasound, photoacoustic), control algorithms, feedback loop.
  • Payload and targeting — releases drugs or acts only on diseased cells. Key parts: targeting ligands, pH- or enzyme-triggered release, stealth coating.
  • Power and communication — supplies energy without onboard batteries; no wires at this scale. Key parts: external magnetic or acoustic energy, chemical fuel from body fluids, passive signalling.
  • Safety and clearance — ensures robots degrade or leave the body after the procedure. Key parts: biodegradable materials, renal clearance size range, retrieval or deactivation mechanism.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the robot and swarm behaviour. Choose size (nanometres to microns), material and task; define how units cooperate and how they will be removed.
  2. Fabricate bodies. Top-down lithography for micro-scale units, or bottom-up self-assembly (DNA origami, block copolymers, magnetic nanoparticle clusters) for nanoscale units.
  3. Functionalise surfaces. Attach drugs, targeting molecules and antifouling coatings; load payloads.
  4. Add propulsion. Embed magnetic material or shape the body asymmetrically so external fields or chemical gradients drive motion.
  5. Test in microfluidic models. Measure speed, steering, drug release and cell-level effects in channels that mimic blood vessels.
  6. Test in animals. Use small animals to study navigation, imaging, immune response and clearance.
  7. Build closed-loop control. Combine real-time imaging with external field generators to steer swarms to a target and stop them.
  8. Prepare for clinical use. Develop sterile manufacturing, dosing, safety and regulatory pathways; none exists for autonomous surgical nanobots.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Nanoscale fabrication and self-assembly — DNA origami and nanoparticle assembly work in the lab, but not at millions of identical surgical units.
  • NOT YET Deep-tissue imaging with nanoscale resolution — Existing MRI and ultrasound cannot track individual nanobots in real time inside the body.
  • PARTIAL Biocompatible propulsion and power — Magnetic and acoustic propulsion have been demonstrated in vitro and in small animals, but efficiency and depth are limited.
  • PARTIAL Swarm control algorithms — Simple swarms of magnetic particles have been steered in the lab, but not with cell-level precision.
  • PARTIAL Biodegradation and clearance — Some nanomaterials clear from the body, but ensuring no toxic residue after surgery is unsolved.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: Individual magnetic microrobots and acoustic particle swarms demonstrated in lab and small animals for targeted drug delivery — basic propulsion, imaging and biocompatibility data.
  2. Next: Closed-loop systems that guide simple particle swarms to a tumour or clot in humans — targeted therapy without open surgery.
  3. Next: Biodegradable nanobots that perform local drug release or mechanical action and then clear safely — repeatable, regulated nanomedicine.
  4. Longer term: Coordinated nanoscale swarms capable of cellular-level cutting, repair or ablation — internal surgery without incisions, if imaging, power and control are solved.
