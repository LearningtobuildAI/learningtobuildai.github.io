PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Precision oncology is an approach that uses molecular profiling to identify the specific genetic alterations driving a patient's cancer, enabling targeted therapies tailored to the individual patient.

Category
Medicine
Best use
Improved efficacy, reduced side effects
Stage

2Problem It Solves

Traditional biopsy methods often require invasive procedures and may not capture the full genetic profile of a patient's cancer. Liquid biopsies offer a non-invasive alternative, providing real-time monitoring of disease progression and response to therapy without the need for repeated invasive procedures.

3Lifecycle / Journey Stage
early commercial
PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Liquid biopsies involve collecting blood samples from patients, which are then analyzed using advanced sequencing technologies. These techniques can detect circulating tumor cells and fragments of DNA (ctDNA) that provide information about the genomic landscape of the cancer. This data is used to tailor treatment plans for each patient.

5Materials Used
  • Blood samples
  • Sequencing reagents
  • Circulating tumor cells (CTCs)
  • ctDNA
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves developing and validating liquid biopsy assays, which include sample collection devices, extraction kits, and sequencing platforms. These components are typically produced in specialized laboratories with stringent quality control measures.

7Build Process

The build process includes designing the assay to target specific genetic markers, optimizing the extraction of ctDNA from blood samples, and integrating this with high-throughput sequencing technologies. Validation studies are conducted to ensure accuracy and reliability before clinical use.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Tumour profiling pipeline — finds the specific mutations that make one person's cancer vulnerable. Key parts: tumour biopsy or blood draw, DNA and RNA sequencing, bioinformatics pipeline, pathologist review.
  • Gene-editing toolkit — cuts or rewrites DNA at a chosen site in a cell. Key parts: Cas9 nuclease, base editor or prime editor protein, guide RNA, repair template.
  • Delivery system — gets the editing machinery into the right cells and nowhere else. Key parts: electroporation, lipid nanoparticles, viral vectors.
  • Engineered cell therapy — turns the patient's own immune cells into cancer killers. Key parts: T cells collected by apheresis, chimeric antigen receptor or T-cell receptor construct, culture and expansion.
  • Targeted drugs — block the protein a mutation drives, or deliver a toxin to cells carrying a marker. Key parts: kinase inhibitors, monoclonal antibodies, antibody–drug conjugates.
  • Manufacturing and quality control — produces a safe, patient-specific dose. Key parts: cleanroom suite, sterility and identity testing, potency assay, chain of custody.
  • Clinical decision and monitoring layer — decides which therapy fits and tracks whether it is working. Key parts: molecular tumour board, circulating tumour DNA monitoring, imaging.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Sample the tumour. A needle biopsy of a few millimetres, or a blood draw of a few millilitres for circulating tumour DNA when a biopsy is risky.
  2. Sequence and interpret. Hundreds of cancer genes, or the whole exome, are read and compared with the patient's normal DNA to separate driver mutations from harmless variation.
  3. Choose the target and the edit. The team picks either a mutated protein to drug, or an immune gene to edit out — for example a checkpoint gene that would otherwise switch the engineered cell off.
  4. Design and build the payload. Guide RNA sequences are chosen by software, the editing protein is synthesised as mRNA, and off-target sites are predicted and then measured in donor cells.
  5. Manufacture the therapy. For a cell product, T cells are collected by apheresis, edited outside the body by electroporation, grown to billions of cells over roughly one to two weeks, then frozen.
  6. Run quality control. Each batch is checked for sterility, correct edit, unwanted edits, and the ability to actually kill target cells before it is released.
  7. Treat the patient. Short chemotherapy clears space in the immune system, then the edited cells are infused — or, for an in-body approach, the editing particles are dosed directly.
  8. Monitor response and toxicity. Falling circulating tumour DNA and shrinking scans mean it is working; cytokine release and immune attack on normal tissue are watched for closely.
  9. Re-profile on relapse. Tumours evolve, so a new sample is sequenced and the next therapy chosen against the resistance mutation that appeared.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Affordable, accurate genome sequencing — profiling a tumour costs a fraction of what it did a decade ago and is routine in many hospitals.
  • EXISTS CRISPR-Cas9 and newer editors — first used to edit human cells in 2012; base and prime editors now allow single-letter changes without a double-strand break. Clinical use is still early.
  • PARTIAL Safe, targeted delivery in the body — lipid nanoparticles reach the liver well but other organs poorly, which limits which cancers can be edited directly rather than in a lab dish.
  • PARTIAL Cell manufacturing capacity — patient-specific cell therapies need cleanroom time and specialist staff that most hospitals do not have.
  • PARTIAL Knowledge of which mutations are actionable — only a minority of mutations found have a drug or edit that changes the outcome; the rest are unresolved.
  • NOT YET Long-term safety data on editing — decades of follow-up are needed to rule out edits landing in the wrong place and causing a new cancer.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: sequencing panels and biomarker-matched drugs are standard care in major cancer centres; engineered cell therapies are approved for some blood cancers and melanoma — real, if narrow, survival gains for patients whose tumour carries the right marker.
  2. Next: CRISPR-edited cell therapies move from small trials to approved products — treatments for patients who relapse after current cell therapies, because edited cells resist exhaustion.
  3. Next: editing delivered directly in the body by lipid nanoparticles — avoids the cost and delay of extracting, editing and re-infusing a patient's own cells.
  4. Longer term: off-the-shelf donor cells with multiple edits to evade rejection — treatment that can start within days rather than weeks, and at far lower cost.
  5. Longer term: editing used alongside standard therapy and against resistance mutations — durable remissions rather than a few months of extra disease control.

18Glossary
Liquid biopsy
A method for detecting cancer by analyzing circulating tumor cells or DNA in a blood sample, providing non-invasive monitoring of disease.
Circulating tumor cells (CTCs)
Cells that break away from the primary tumor and enter the bloodstream, potentially contributing to metastasis.
ctDNA
Fragments of DNA released by dying cancer cells into the bloodstream, which can be sequenced to identify genetic mutations associated with cancer.
