PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Brain preservation — stabilise the brain's structure so it can be imaged without decay. Key parts: perfusion fixation, cryoprotection, vitrification.
  • Nanoscale imaging — capture every neuron and synapse in three dimensions. Key parts: serial-section electron microscopy, focused ion beam SEM, expansion microscopy.
  • Connectome reconstruction — trace neural wiring from stacks of images. Key parts: automated segmentation, synapse detection, human proofreading.
  • Functional state capture — record synaptic strengths, receptor types and ongoing activity patterns. Key parts: multi-electrode arrays, calcium imaging, molecular markers.
  • Computational simulation — run a model of the reconstructed brain. Key parts: neuron models, synaptic plasticity rules, large-scale simulation software.
  • Validation and interface — test whether the simulation behaves like the person and give it senses or a body. Key parts: behavioural tests, neural correlates of consciousness, sensorimotor virtual embodiment.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Preserve the brain. After death, the brain is chemically fixed or vitrified to lock its structure in place before decay destroys it.
  2. Slice and image. The tissue is cut into thousands of nanometre-thin sections and imaged with electron microscopes to see every membrane and synapse.
  3. Reconstruct the connectome. Machine-learning algorithms trace each neuron and its connections from the images, with human proofreading to correct errors.
  4. Capture functional properties. Synaptic strengths, receptor types and neuromodulator levels must be measured or inferred; no current method can do this for a whole human brain.
  5. Build a computational model. The wiring diagram and functional data are converted into a simulation of neurons, synapses and their dynamics.
  6. Run the simulation. A supercomputer executes the model, requiring memory and processing far beyond today's largest machines to run in real time.
  7. Validate and interact. The simulated mind is tested for memory, personality and consciousness, and given sensors or a virtual body to interact with the world.
  8. Transfer or store. If successful, the mind could be copied, stored, or run in a different substrate — raising unresolved questions about identity.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Whole-brain connectomics at synaptic resolution — complete wiring diagrams exist for a fruit fly and parts of mouse cortex, but not for a human brain.
  • NOT YET Functional mapping of synapses — current methods cannot measure the strength and type of every synapse in a human brain.
  • PARTIAL Exascale computing — the largest supercomputers are approaching this scale, but simulating a human brain in real time is far beyond them.
  • NOT YET Understanding of consciousness — there is no scientific consensus on what consciousness is or how to detect it in a simulation.
  • PARTIAL Brain preservation without information loss — vitrification can preserve structure, but whether it retains functional information is unproven.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: complete connectomes of small animals such as the fruit fly and parts of mouse cortex — automated reconstruction methods and data-handling tools.
  2. Next: functional connectomics that links wiring to activity in mammals — models that reproduce simple behaviours.
  3. Next: simulation of a whole mouse brain — proof that a detailed model can produce mind-like behaviour.
  4. Longer term: human connectome and functional map — a substrate for uploading, if consciousness can be validated.
  5. Speculative: mind uploading and digital afterlife — continuation of personal identity in a digital form — entirely unproven.
