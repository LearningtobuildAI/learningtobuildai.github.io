PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

In-Vivo Base Editing (iVBE) is a genetic medicine technology that allows for precise editing of genes within living cells in the body without cutting both strands of DNA, enabling one-time therapies to correct or switch off disease-causing genes.

Category
Genetic Medicine
Best use
Inherited cardiovascular and metabolic disease (clinical trials)
Stage
TRIAL
2Problem It Solves

iVBE addresses the need for targeted and efficient genetic modifications in living organisms to treat inherited diseases such as cardiovascular and metabolic disorders where traditional CRISPR-Cas9 methods might cause unintended edits due to off-target effects or require multiple treatments.

3Lifecycle / Journey Stage
early commercial
PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Lipid nanoparticles encapsulate a base-editor enzyme and guide RNA, which are then delivered directly into the target tissue via an injection. The base-editor converts a single DNA letter (nucleotide) at the precise location specified by the guide RNA, altering the gene's output without making double-strand breaks.

5Materials Used
  • lipid nanoparticles
  • base-editor enzyme
  • guide RNA
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves the synthesis of guide RNA, production of lipid nanoparticles encapsulating base-editors and RNA, and formulation into a stable injectable solution. Quality control measures ensure purity and efficacy of the components before clinical use.

7Build Process

Components are synthesized or produced separately, then combined in a cleanroom environment under sterile conditions. The mixture is loaded into delivery devices for testing and eventual clinical trials.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Delivery vehicle — carries the editing payload through the bloodstream and into target cells, chiefly liver cells. Key parts: ionizable lipid, helper phospholipid, cholesterol, PEG-lipid.
  • Payload — provides the instructions and the editing machinery inside the cell. Key parts: messenger RNA encoding the base editor, guide RNA.
  • Base editor protein — chemically converts one DNA letter to another without cutting both strands. Key parts: deaminase enzyme (cytosine or adenine), Cas9 nickase, nuclear localisation signals.
  • Guide RNA — directs the editor to the exact spot in the genome. Key parts: targeting spacer sequence, scaffold that binds the Cas9 nickase.
  • Manufacturing and quality control — produces clinical-grade mRNA, guide RNA and lipid nanoparticles, and verifies their purity and safety. Key parts: in vitro transcription and purification, microfluidic mixing and formulation, analytical assays for size, potency and off-target editing.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the target letter. Identify a disease-causing gene and the precise single DNA letter that should be changed to alter the protein it makes.
  2. Design the editor. Select a cytosine or adenine base editor and design a guide RNA that positions the deaminase over the target letter.
  3. Make the RNA payload. Produce the editor mRNA and guide RNA by in vitro transcription, add caps and tails, and purify them.
  4. Formulate lipid nanoparticles. Mix the RNA with ionizable lipids, helper lipids, cholesterol and PEG-lipids so the RNA is packaged into nanometre-scale particles.
  5. Test in cells and animals. Measure editing efficiency at the intended site and check for unintended edits elsewhere in the genome, then confirm the effect in animal models.
  6. Scale up under clean conditions. Produce clinical-grade batches in a good-manufacturing-practice facility and release them only after passing purity and potency tests.
  7. Treat patients and monitor. Infuse the formulation intravenously in a clinical trial and track editing in liver biopsies, blood biomarkers and any side effects over time.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Base editing enzymes — demonstrated in cells and animals, but no in-vivo base editor has yet been approved for clinical use.
  • EXISTS Lipid nanoparticle delivery to the liver — clinically validated by approved siRNA and mRNA medicines, which naturally accumulate in liver cells.
  • NOT YET Targeted delivery to other organs — most inherited diseases affect tissues beyond the liver, and no routine delivery system exists for them yet.
  • EXISTS Large-scale mRNA manufacturing — the COVID-19 vaccines proved that messenger RNA can be made at very large scale with consistent quality.
  • PARTIAL Off-target editing assays — methods to detect unintended edits exist, but standards for what counts as safe are still being defined.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: early clinical trials for liver targets, such as a programme for inherited high cholesterol that began dosing in 2022 — first proof that a single infusion can durably change a disease gene in people.
  2. Next: regulatory approval of the first in-vivo base editing therapy — a one-time treatment option for a common inherited condition, replacing daily medication.
  3. Next: new delivery systems that reach muscle, the central nervous system and the eye — base editing for a much wider range of inherited diseases.
  4. Longer term: editing for widespread conditions such as heart disease risk factors — a shift from chronic management to preventive, one-time genetic medicine.

18Glossary
Base-Editor
An enzyme that chemically converts a single DNA letter (nucleotide) to another, enabling precise genetic modifications without cutting both strands of DNA.
Guide RNA
A sequence-specific RNA molecule that directs the base-editor to the correct location in the genome for editing.
Lipid Nanoparticles
Small lipid-based carriers used to deliver genetic material, such as base-editors and guide RNAs, into cells.
