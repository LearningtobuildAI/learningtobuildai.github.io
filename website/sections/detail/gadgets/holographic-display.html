PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Light engine — provides coherent red, green and blue light to form the hologram. Key parts: laser diodes, beam combiners, collimating optics, thermoelectric coolers.
  • Spatial light modulator — encodes the holographic interference pattern onto the light wavefront. Key parts: liquid-crystal-on-silicon backplane, phase-modulating liquid crystal layer, driver electronics, polarizers.
  • Hologram computation unit — calculates the light pattern needed for each frame in real time. Key parts: high-end GPU or FPGA, hologram generation algorithms, data compression, latency buffers.
  • Optical relay and projection — forms the wavefront in free space at the intended viewing zone. Key parts: lenses, mirrors, diffractive optical elements, beam splitters.
  • Eye tracking and view adaptation — detects viewer positions and adjusts the hologram so each eye sees correct depth. Key parts: stereo cameras, infrared illuminators, real-time tracking software, calibration memory.
  • Volumetric medium (optional) — provides a physical volume where light scatters to form a visible 3D image. Key parts: aerosol or mist generator, rotating diffuser, nonlinear optical medium.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Specify the viewing geometry. Decide image size, depth range, viewing angle and number of simultaneous viewers. This sets the required modulator resolution and optical aperture.
  2. Fabricate high-resolution phase modulators. In a cleanroom, build a CMOS backplane with millions of pixel electrodes, then apply a liquid-crystal layer and alignment layers.
  3. Implement the hologram generation software. Write algorithms that compute phase patterns from 3D scene data, using GPU acceleration and techniques such as Fresnel diffraction or point-cloud methods.
  4. Assemble the optical train. Mount lasers, modulators and lenses on a rigid bench, align them to sub-micron precision, and calibrate wavefront aberrations.
  5. Integrate real-time tracking. Add cameras and infrared illumination, calibrate them to the optical system, and link tracking data to the hologram computation.
  6. Test and iterate. Measure resolution, depth accuracy, viewing angle, brightness and colour fidelity; reduce speckle and crosstalk by optimising algorithms and optics.
  7. Package and control. Enclose the system, manage heat from lasers and electronics, and build a user interface that converts standard 3D models into holographic frames.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-resolution spatial light modulators — current modulators have millions of pixels, but true holographic displays need hundreds of millions for wide viewing angles and large sizes.
  • PARTIAL Real-time hologram computation — demonstrated for simple scenes, but complex dynamic scenes at video rate require far more computing power.
  • EXISTS Coherent or partially coherent light sources — laser diodes and LEDs are widely available and compact.
  • PARTIAL Eye tracking and view-dependent rendering — used in AR/VR, but integrating with holographic displays requires very low latency and high accuracy.
  • PARTIAL Volumetric display media — fog, plasma and rotating screens have been demonstrated in labs, but they are bulky, dim or slow.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: research prototypes show small, limited-colour floating images with narrow viewing angles — understanding of optical and computational limits.
  2. Next: higher-resolution modulators and faster algorithms enable larger, full-colour images with wider viewing angles — practical use in design review and medical imaging.
  3. Next: integration of eye tracking and multi-user support — displays usable by several people at once without headsets.
  4. Longer term: consumer or professional displays with video-rate, full-colour, wide-angle 3D images — everyday holographic communication and visualisation.
