PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sensor array — picks up the tiny signals the brain produces during inner speech. Key parts: dry EEG electrodes, fNIRS light emitters and detectors, elastic headband with fixed montage.
  • Analog front end — amplifies microvolt-scale signals without drowning them in noise. Key parts: low-noise amplifiers, filters, shielded wiring, analog-to-digital converter.
  • Artifact handling — removes blinks, jaw clenching, swallowing and movement, which are far larger than the signal of interest. Key parts: accelerometer/IMU, reference channels, regression or blind-source-separation software.
  • Decoder — turns patterns of brain activity into words. Key parts: feature extractor (spectral and spatial features), neural network classifier, language model to correct and predict.
  • Compute and output — runs inference and delivers text on the device. Key parts: low-power processor or paired phone, text interface, on-device storage.
  • Calibration and personalisation — every head and every brain is different, so the model must be tuned per user. Key parts: guided training session, per-user model weights.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the modality and montage. EEG is fast but spatially blurry; fNIRS is slower but better localised. Combining them trades speed against information.
  2. Build the sensors. Dry or semi-dry electrodes avoid conductive gel but need spring-loaded contact through hair; fNIRS needs emitters and detectors placed close enough to reach cortex.
  3. Design the front end. Amplifiers must resolve signals of a few microvolts next to mains hum and muscle noise, so shielding and active electrodes matter.
  4. Collect training data. A user performs prompted inner speech for hundreds of trials per word or phoneme while their signals are recorded.
  5. Clean the data. Eye blinks, jaw tension and motion are regressed out or rejected before the classifier sees anything.
  6. Train the decoder. A model learns to map features to words or characters; a language model then turns noisy output into fluent text.
  7. Close the loop with the user. The user sees predictions and corrects them, feeding back into per-user fine-tuning.
  8. Harden and miniaturise. The lab rig is shrunk into a wearable that survives daily wear, sweat and hair, and keeps raw neural data private on the device.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Dry high-density EEG electrodes — gel-free contact through hair is what makes a headband usable outside a lab.
  • EXISTS Portable fNIRS — adds blood-oxygen information that EEG alone cannot provide.
  • EXISTS Low-power on-device machine learning — a headband cannot stream raw neural data to the cloud for every keystroke.
  • NOT YET Large labelled inner-speech datasets — imagined speech varies hugely between people, and there is no large shared corpus to train on.
  • PARTIAL Clinically validated speech neuroprostheses — invasive implants in trials have restored speech at near-conversational rates, setting the accuracy bar non-invasive devices must approach.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: lab systems decoding small sets of imagined words or syllables with subject-specific training — proof that inner speech leaves a readable trace on the scalp.
  2. Next: headbands that need only minutes of calibration for a few dozen commands — hands-free control for users with severe motor impairment.
  3. Next: generalised decoders that transfer between users with little retraining — a product that works out of the box rather than per subject.
  4. Longer term: continuous free-text inner speech at conversational speed — silent text entry for anyone — and a serious privacy problem needing on-device processing and consent design.
