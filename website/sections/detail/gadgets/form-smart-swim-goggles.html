PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Display module — a tiny transparent screen inside one lens that floats numbers in front of the swimmer's eye without blocking the view. Key parts: micro-display panel, driver electronics, flex cable, waveguide optics.
  • Goggle lens and frame — keeps water out and sight clear while holding the embedded optics. Key parts: polycarbonate or silicone-skirted frame, lens with laminated waveguide film, seals.
  • Electronics pod — the brains and battery, clamped to the goggle strap behind the head. Key parts: microcontroller or system-on-chip, rechargeable battery, Bluetooth radio, buttons and haptics.
  • Motion sensing — works out strokes, laps and turns without any external equipment. Key parts: accelerometer, gyroscope, magnetometer.
  • Heart-rate sensing — measures pulse during the swim and feeds it to the display. Key parts: optical sensor (light source plus photodiode), strap or armband, wireless link.
  • Firmware and companion app — turns raw motion into pace, splits and stroke counts, and lets workouts be set up on a phone. Key parts: lap and turn-detection algorithms, activity sync, poolside configuration app.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the optics. Ray-tracing software is used to work out how a virtual image can be projected through a swimmer's goggle lens and appear focused a couple of metres ahead, while remaining legible against bright water.
  2. Make the waveguide. Fine diffraction gratings are patterned onto a master and replicated onto a thin glass or polymer sheet, which is laminated into the lens so the image travels along it and exits at the pupil.
  3. Build the micro-display. A chip-scale panel — a miniature organic LED array or a lit liquid-crystal panel — is mounted with its driver circuit and connected by a flexible cable.
  4. Seal the electronics. Chip, radio, battery and controls are potted into a housing that survives repeated immersion and chlorine or salt water.
  5. Calibrate the sensors. Each inertial unit is calibrated for offset and drift, and the magnetometer is corrected for the metal-rich environment of an indoor pool.
  6. Write the swimming algorithms. Firmware learns to count strokes, spot tumble turns and open turns, and mark manual laps from a button press.
  7. Test in water. Pressure testing, drop and leak testing, optical legibility checks underwater, and pool trials against a stopwatch to validate distance and pace.
  8. Pair, update and ship. Over-the-air firmware updates and phone pairing are validated, then units are assembled and shipped with the app.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Transparent near-eye waveguide displays — the same optics used in augmented-reality glasses, but engineered to stay readable in bright, moving water.
  • EXISTS Micro-scale low-power displays — the screen must be tiny, bright and efficient enough to run from a small goggle battery.
  • EXISTS Micro-electromechanical inertial sensors — solid-state accelerometers and gyroscopes are what make stroke and lap detection possible without touching the water.
  • EXISTS Optical heart-rate sensing — light-based pulse measurement in a strap gives the display a heart-rate figure without a chest belt of electrodes.
  • EXISTS Low-power wireless and depth-rated sealing — Bluetooth links to phone and strap, and seals must hold at repeated swim depth.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: goggles that show live pace, splits and stroke data, with heart rate from a paired strap — pool swimmers can train to a target pace without looking at a clock.
  2. Next: heart rate and more sensing built directly into the goggles — one device instead of two, and fewer pairing failures.
  3. Next: open-water modes with audio or haptic coaching cues — usable guidance in lakes and sea, where the swimmer cannot read a watch or clock face.
  4. Longer term: richer augmented-reality overlays and multi-swimmer training features — virtual pacemakers, live lane data and remote coaching.
