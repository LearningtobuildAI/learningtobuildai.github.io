9Engineering Breakdown
  • Particle sensor — counts and sizes airborne particles, giving PM1, PM2.5 and PM10 concentrations. Key parts: laser diode, photodiode, optical chamber, small fan.
  • Gas sensors — measure carbon dioxide and total volatile organic compounds. Key parts: NDIR CO2 sensor, metal oxide VOC sensor, signal conditioning.
  • Climate sensors — measure temperature and relative humidity, which affect comfort, mould risk and sensor readings. Key parts: thermistor or digital temperature sensor, capacitive humidity sensor.
  • Processing and firmware — converts raw signals into calibrated readings, applies corrections and manages sampling. Key parts: microcontroller, firmware with calibration coefficients, data logging.
  • User interface and connectivity — shows readings and sends data to apps or smart home systems. Key parts: display or LED indicator, Wi-Fi or Bluetooth module, mobile app or cloud service.
  • Power and enclosure — houses and powers the device while allowing air to flow past the sensors. Key parts: USB or battery power, vented enclosure, mounting bracket.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the airflow and optical chamber. Arrange the fan, laser and photodiode so that sampled air crosses the beam without dead spots, and separate the gas sensors from the particle sensor to avoid contamination.
  2. Assemble the particle counter. Mount the laser diode and photodiode at a fixed angle, align the optics, and fit the fan; test with known test dust to check counting.
  3. Integrate the gas sensors. Place the NDIR CO2 sensor with its infrared source and detector, and the metal oxide VOC sensor, on the board; allow for warm-up and baseline calibration.
  4. Build the circuit board. Solder the microcontroller, sensors, power management, display and wireless module onto a printed circuit board; program initial firmware.
  5. Write the firmware. Implement sampling schedules, convert raw signals to PM, CO2, TVOC, temperature and humidity, apply temperature and humidity corrections, and set thresholds for alerts.
  6. Assemble the enclosure. Fit the board into a vented case, add light guides for the display or LEDs, and ensure the airflow path matches the design.
  7. Calibrate and test. Compare particle readings against a gravimetric reference or a calibrated optical counter, and CO2 against a reference NDIR instrument; adjust coefficients.
  8. Final quality control. Run each unit for several hours, check sensor drift, verify connectivity and accuracy, then package.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Low-cost optical particle counters — they make it practical to measure PM2.5 continuously in a small device.
  • EXISTS NDIR CO2 sensors — they give a reliable proxy for ventilation rate, which is the key metric for airborne transmission risk.
  • EXISTS Metal oxide VOC sensors — they respond to many volatile organic compounds, though they need baseline correction and cannot identify specific gases.
  • EXISTS Low-power microcontrollers and wireless — they process sensor data and connect to phones or home networks without draining power.
  • EXISTS Calibration standards and reference instruments — without them, readings drift and cannot be trusted for decisions like opening windows.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: consumer monitors with PM2.5, CO2, TVOC, temperature and humidity widely available — people can see ventilation and pollution levels in real time.
  2. Next: better calibration and sensor fusion, plus standardised indices for indoor air quality — more reliable comparisons and actionable advice.
  3. Next: integration with HVAC, air purifiers and smart home systems to automate ventilation — automatic response to poor air, reducing exposure without user effort.
  4. Longer term: dense networks of monitors in buildings and wearable personal exposure trackers — fine-scale mapping of indoor air quality and personalised health insights.
