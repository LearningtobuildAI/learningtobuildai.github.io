PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Light-field panel — shows a dense set of views at once so several people can see 3D without glasses. Key parts: high-resolution LCD or OLED, lenslet array or diffractive backlight, per-pixel view steering.
  • Aerial voxel engine — draws the floating part of the image — bright points in empty air. Key parts: infrared or visible laser, scanning mirrors or spatial light modulator, focusing optics.
  • Eye and hand tracking — finds where each eye is and what the hands are doing, many times a second. Key parts: infrared cameras, depth sensor, gaze-estimation software.
  • Haptic layer — lets the user feel invisible controls. Key parts: ultrasonic phased array, modulation controller.
  • Render and warp pipeline — draws the right view for the right eye fast enough that it feels solid. Key parts: GPU, low-latency reprojection, scene graph.
  • Safety envelope — keeps laser and ultrasound exposure inside legal limits. Key parts: wavelength choice and beam enclosure, power interlocks, shielding and exposure monitoring.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the image method. Decide how much of the picture is a light-field panel and how much is drawn as points in air. Aerial voxels are the hard, bright, risky part; the panel can carry the rest.
  2. Build the panel. A high-resolution screen is covered with a lenslet array or given a directional backlight, so each pixel emits many narrow beams and a viewer sees a different image from each position.
  3. Add eye tracking. Infrared cameras watch the face and eyes; software estimates gaze in a few milliseconds. This steers extra view resolution toward each pupil instead of spreading it over all directions.
  4. Assemble the aerial voxel engine. A laser is focused to a tiny point; scanning mirrors sweep that point through a volume. Air plasma glows briefly at the focus, or a trapped light-absorbing particle is moved and lit. Brighter, larger volumes need faster scanning and more laser power.
  5. Design the safety envelope. Choose wavelengths that are strongly absorbed by water in the eye, keep scattered exposure under laser-safety limits, enclose stray beams, and interlock the laser so it stops if anything enters the volume. Ultrasound haptics must stay inside accepted exposure limits too.
  6. Fuse tracking, haptics and interaction. Hand and finger positions drive objects; a phased array of ultrasound emitters focuses pressure at the fingertips; clicks, drags and gestures get visual and tactile feedback.
  7. Tune the render loop. The GPU renders per-eye views at high frame rate and reprojects them at the last instant using fresh eye data, so head movement does not break the illusion of a solid object.
  8. Calibrate everything. Eye tracker, panel and laser volume are aligned into one coordinate frame and re-checked as the user moves around the desk.
  9. Validate with users and regulators. Depth-accuracy, comfort, fatigue and multi-user tests, plus laser and ultrasound certification, before anything can be sold.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Light-field and multi-view displays — desktop 3D panels exist, but resolution per view is far below what a full workspace needs.
  • PARTIAL Aerial voxel generation — plasma and trapped-particle displays have been shown in labs; they are small, dim and slow.
  • PARTIAL Ultrasonic mid-air haptics — demonstrated in prototypes; limited force and small working volume.
  • EXISTS Low-latency eye and hand tracking — shipped in headsets and phones; provides the gaze data the display needs.
  • EXISTS Real-time rendering hardware — GPUs can render many views per frame, which was impossible a decade ago.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: eye-tracked light-field desktops and stereo AR/VR headsets — glasses-free 3D at a desk for one or two viewers, without the floating image.
  2. Next: brighter, larger eye-safe aerial voxel volumes plus fused hand tracking and haptics — reaching into a 3D scene and feeling it respond — the core of the concept.
  3. Next: hybrid workstations that combine a light-field panel with sparse aerial highlights — practical desks where only the objects you touch are drawn in mid-air.
  4. Longer term: certified, full-parallax, multi-user volumetric workspaces at usable resolution and colour — shared 3D design and control rooms replacing walls of 2D screens.
