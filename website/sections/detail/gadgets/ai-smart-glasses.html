PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Optical display engine — projects a focused image into the wearer's eye so it appears overlaid on the real world. Key parts: micro-display (OLED or microLED), waveguide combiner, collimating and projection optics.
  • Sensing suite — captures the scene and the wearer's motion and voice for the AI to interpret. Key parts: camera(s), inertial measurement unit, depth sensor, microphone array.
  • Compute and AI runtime — runs vision, translation and language models, either on the glasses or on a paired phone or cloud service. Key parts: low-power system-on-chip, neural accelerator, memory and storage, wireless link to phone/cloud.
  • Audio interface — delivers spoken translations, navigation cues and assistant replies without blocking the ears. Key parts: bone-conduction or open-ear speakers, beamforming microphones.
  • Power system — supplies enough energy for a day of mixed use while staying light. Key parts: lithium-polymer battery, power management integrated circuit, charging contacts or case.
  • Frame and thermal management — houses all components in a comfortable, glasses-like form and spreads heat away from the head. Key parts: lightweight frame and temples, heat spreaders or graphite sheets, nose pads and hinges.
  • Connectivity — links the glasses to a phone, the internet and other devices. Key parts: Bluetooth, Wi-Fi, optional cellular modem on a paired device.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the optical stack. Choose a micro-display and a waveguide geometry, then simulate how light will travel from the display to the eye without ghosting or colour smearing.
  2. Fabricate waveguides. High-index glass wafers are patterned with surface-relief gratings using nano-imprint or etching, then diced and polished.
  3. Manufacture micro-displays. OLED or microLED arrays are built on silicon backplanes, with pixel sizes measured in a few micrometres.
  4. Assemble the optical module. The display, collimating lenses and waveguide are aligned to within a micrometre and fixed in place.
  5. Build the electronics. The system-on-chip, sensors, battery and antennas are placed into the temple arms, often on flexible printed circuits.
  6. Train and optimise AI models. Translation, object recognition and navigation models are trained, then compressed and quantised so they run on low power or a nearby phone.
  7. Integrate and calibrate. Cameras are aligned with the display, the inertial unit is calibrated, and software is tuned so virtual objects stay anchored to the world.
  8. Test and certify. Units undergo drop, sweat, battery, electromagnetic and eye-safety testing before they can be sold.
  9. Scale manufacturing. Assembly and optical alignment are automated, and test lines are built to keep yields high.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Micro-display technology (OLED or microLED) — microLED offers brightness and efficiency but is not yet cheap or high-resolution enough for mass-market glasses.
  • PARTIAL Waveguide optics — mass-producing waveguides with uniform colour and low light loss remains difficult and costly.
  • EXISTS Low-power AI chips — dedicated neural accelerators can run vision and speech models within a few watts.
  • EXISTS Computer vision and speech models — translation, object detection and navigation are mature enough for real-time use.
  • EXISTS Battery energy density — current lithium-polymer cells limit all-day use in a lightweight frame.
  • EXISTS Wireless connectivity — Bluetooth and Wi-Fi reliably link glasses to a phone or cloud service.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: audio-only AI glasses and monocular display glasses for notifications and basic translation — hands-free access to an AI assistant and simple text prompts.
  2. Next: full-colour, high-brightness microLED displays with waveguides and on-device AI — clear, contextual overlays for navigation and object recognition without a phone.
  3. Next: seamless augmented reality with SLAM, 3D mapping and persistent virtual objects — navigation arrows that stick to the street and labels that stay on real objects.
  4. Longer term: always-on, prescription-ready glasses with all-day battery and optional cellular — a true everyday wearable that replaces the phone for many tasks.
