PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Bidirectional power stage — converts the battery's DC into grid-shaped AC and back again, the core difference from a one-way charger. Key parts: silicon-carbide MOSFET or IGBT bridge, isolated DC/DC converter, filters and magnetics.
  • Vehicle interface — the physical and electronic link to the car's pack, including what the car will allow. Key parts: CCS / NACS / CHAdeMO connector, pilot and control lines, battery management system limits.
  • Communication and control — negotiates power direction, current limits and safety with car and grid. Key parts: controller board, ISO 15118 / CHAdeMO signalling stack, energy meter.
  • Grid interface and protection — keeps the house and the grid safe when power flows the other way. Key parts: transfer switch or contactor, anti-islanding detection, residual-current and overcurrent protection.
  • Thermal management — removes heat during sustained multi-kilowatt export. Key parts: heatsink, fan or liquid loop, temperature sensors.
  • Energy management — decides when to charge, when to back up the house and when to sell to the grid. Key parts: tariff and solar inputs, user app, scheduling logic.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the topology. A bidirectional design needs an active rectifier plus an isolated DC/DC stage; engineers pick a topology that keeps losses and cost low at 7–22 kW.
  2. Build the power board. Silicon-carbide devices, gate drivers and current sensors are soldered onto a thick-copper board, and transformers and inductors are wound to spec.
  3. Write the control firmware. Fast loops keep current sinusoidal and synchronised to the grid; slower logic follows commands from the car and the home energy system.
  4. Implement the protocol stack. The charger must speak the vehicle's digital handshake exactly, and pass the standardised safety and insulation tests it demands.
  5. Assemble and seal the unit. Power stage, control board and cooling go into an outdoor-rated enclosure with the connector, cabling and a lockable holster.
  6. Certify. The unit is type-tested for electrical safety, electromagnetic compatibility, anti-islanding and the grid code of each country it will be sold in.
  7. Install at the property. An electrician fits it on a dedicated circuit, wires the transfer switch or backup panel, and proves it disconnects from the grid before running islanded.
  8. Pair with a vehicle. Only cars whose firmware and warranty permit reverse power can be used, so commissioning ends with a compatibility check.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Vehicles that allow power out — reverse power needs the car's own charger, contactors and warranty to support it; only some models do.
  • PARTIAL Bidirectional charging standard (ISO 15118-20 and CHAdeMO) — a published standard is what lets any charger talk to any car instead of each pairing being bespoke.
  • EXISTS Wide-bandgap power semiconductors — silicon carbide and gallium nitride make a compact, efficient bidirectional converter practical.
  • PARTIAL Grid connection rules for export from vehicles — utilities and regulators must allow, meter and pay for power pushed back through a household connection.
  • PARTIAL Home wiring able to island safely — backup operation requires a transfer switch or critical-loads panel so the house is never fed while the grid is live.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: vehicle-to-load sockets and first vehicle-to-home systems in a few markets — running tools or a fridge from a car, and limited whole-home backup during outages.
  2. Next: standardised bidirectional chargers on sale across major markets — a car becomes a home battery without proprietary hardware.
  3. Next: tariff and aggregator schemes paying owners for grid export — fleets of parked cars providing peak capacity and frequency support.
  4. Longer term: millions of vehicles coordinated as distributed storage — cheap firming for wind- and solar-heavy grids, provided owners keep enough charge.
