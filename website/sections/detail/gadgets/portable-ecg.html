9Engineering Breakdown
  • Electrodes — pick up the heart's electrical signal from the skin without gels or adhesives. Key parts: dry metal electrodes (e.g., stainless steel), contact pads, shielded internal wiring.
  • Analog front end — amplify and filter the microvolt-to-millivolt signal before digitisation. Key parts: instrumentation amplifier, anti-aliasing filter, analog-to-digital converter.
  • Digital processing and AI — detect heartbeats and classify the rhythm for anomalies. Key parts: low-power microcontroller, real-time QRS detection algorithm, machine-learning classifier.
  • Power and enclosure — run the device and protect it during everyday use. Key parts: rechargeable battery, charging circuit, sealed housing, display or indicator LEDs.
  • Connectivity — send recordings to a phone or cloud for review and storage. Key parts: Bluetooth radio, mobile app, cloud storage.
  • User interface — guide the user and show immediate results. Key parts: buttons or touch surface, screen or LEDs, haptic feedback.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the electrode-skin interface. Choose biocompatible metals and optimise contact area and pressure to get stable signals without conductive gel.
  2. Lay out the analog circuit. Place the amplifier close to the electrodes, separate analog and digital grounds, and add filters to reject noise and motion artefact.
  3. Write embedded firmware. Sample at 250–500 Hz, apply digital filters, detect R peaks in real time, and buffer the trace for storage.
  4. Develop the AI classifier. Train on large sets of labelled ECGs, then validate sensitivity and specificity for atrial fibrillation against cardiologist interpretation.
  5. Build the mobile app and cloud service. Pair the device, display a live trace, store recordings, and generate reports that a clinician can review.
  6. Prototype and test. Compare recordings with a standard 12-lead ECG in a clinical study and check for motion artefact and skin irritation.
  7. Certify to medical device standards. Run electrical safety, electromagnetic compatibility and accuracy tests (e.g., IEC 60601 series) and obtain regulatory clearance.
  8. Manufacture and calibrate. Use automated assembly, then calibrate each unit with a signal generator and seal it to the required ingress-protection rating.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Low-power microcontrollers and analog-to-digital converters — must run for days on a small battery while sampling at hundreds of hertz.
  • EXISTS Dry electrode materials — allow use without gels or adhesives, though signal quality can be lower than with wet electrodes.
  • PARTIAL AI algorithms for arrhythmia detection — some are cleared for atrial fibrillation, but broader arrhythmia detection is still maturing.
  • EXISTS Smartphone connectivity and cloud services — enable remote review, storage and sharing of recordings.
  • EXISTS Regulatory standards for consumer ECG — provide a pathway to market for devices that claim to detect rhythm disorders.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: single-lead devices for opportunistic atrial fibrillation screening, cleared as medical devices in some regions — early detection of silent rhythm problems during everyday life.
  2. Next: multi-lead or continuous patch monitors for longer-term rhythm surveillance — better capture of intermittent arrhythmias that a 30-second reading misses.
  3. Next: deep integration with electronic health records and remote cardiology services — alerts that trigger timely clinical review and treatment.
  4. Longer term: AI that detects ischaemia or heart attack from a pocket device, with robust clinical validation — pre-hospital triage and faster access to emergency care.
