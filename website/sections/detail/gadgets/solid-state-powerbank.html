PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Anode — stores lithium and sets much of the cell's capacity; a solid electrolyte allows lithium metal here instead of graphite. Key parts: lithium-metal foil or silicon-based composite, copper current collector.
  • Solid electrolyte — conducts lithium ions between the electrodes while physically blocking the dendrites that cause fires in liquid cells. Key parts: sulfide or oxide ceramic (e.g. garnet-type) powder, polymer binder or composite layer, thin sintered membrane.
  • Cathode — the lithium source on first charge; must be mixed with solid electrolyte rather than soaked in liquid. Key parts: nickel-rich layered oxide, conductive carbon, aluminium current collector.
  • Cell stack and interfaces — intimate contact between rigid layers, kept under pressure so ions can cross every boundary. Key parts: calendered electrode sheets, interface coatings, stack pressure hardware.
  • Battery management and conversion — a single cell outputs roughly 3–4 V, so electronics must regulate charging and boost to the 5 V USB output. Key parts: charge controller and protection IC, DC-DC boost converter, USB-C power-delivery controller, temperature sensors.
  • Pack and enclosure — holds the cells, spreads heat and provides the pocket-sized case and ports. Key parts: aluminium or plastic shell, insulating and thermal pad, USB-C ports and status indicator.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Synthesise the electrolyte. Sulfide glass-ceramic is milled from raw powders and heat-treated; oxide versions are sintered at over 1000 °C. Both are processed in dry rooms or inert gas, because sulfides react with moisture.
  2. Form the membrane. The electrolyte is pressed or cast into a dense sheet tens of micrometres thick — thin enough for low resistance, thick enough to stop dendrites.
  3. Make the cathode composite. Cathode powder is mixed with electrolyte particles and carbon, then coated onto aluminium foil and rolled flat.
  4. Stack and densify. Cathode, electrolyte and anode are laminated and pressed, sometimes warm-pressed or sintered, to close the microscopic gaps between layers.
  5. Package the cells. Cells are cut and sealed into laminated pouches or ceramic packages with welded tabs; sealing quality decides life and safety.
  6. Form and test. The first slow charge forms the interfaces; cells are then tested for capacity, resistance and self-discharge, and aged at elevated temperature.
  7. Build the pack. Cells are connected in series-parallel, bonded to a circuit board with the charge controller, boost converter and USB-C port.
  8. Enclose and certify. The pack goes into the shell with thermal padding and is drop-tested and safety-tested, including the UN 38.3 transport standard for lithium batteries.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Solid electrolytes with high room-temperature ionic conductivity — sulfide ceramics can reach ionic conductivities comparable to liquid electrolytes in the lab; keeping that performance in a manufactured sheet is harder.
  • PARTIAL Stable lithium-metal interfaces — without a liquid to wet the surfaces, contact is poor and resistance grows, shortening life.
  • PARTIAL Dry-room or inert-atmosphere manufacturing — many promising electrolytes degrade in ordinary humid air, so existing lithium-ion factories cannot simply be reused.
  • NOT YET Low-cost thin ceramic membranes — sintering large, defect-free ceramic sheets cheaply is not yet a proven mass-production process.
  • PARTIAL Stack-pressure management in a pocket device — cells need sustained mechanical pressure; doing that in a thin, drop-tested consumer product is an engineering compromise.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: tiny ceramic and thin-film solid-state cells shipping in wearables and sensors — real-world data on solid-state lifetime in small formats.
  2. Next: pilot production lines for larger sulfide-based cells, initially for electric vehicles — falling cell costs and evidence that the chemistry scales beyond button size.
  3. Next: solid-state power banks and phones using the same cells — thinner, faster-charging gadgets that are far less likely to catch fire.
  4. Longer term: solid-state cells become the default in premium devices — higher energy density per kilogram and per litre across portable electronics — if ceramic manufacturing and cost targets are met.
