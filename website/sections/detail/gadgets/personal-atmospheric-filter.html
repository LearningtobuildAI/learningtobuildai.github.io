9Engineering Breakdown
  • Air mover — pulls ambient air in and pushes cleaned air toward the nose and mouth. Key parts: brushless micro-fans, ducts and nozzles, vibration damping.
  • Filter media — removes particles from the airstream. Key parts: pleated HEPA-grade electret polypropylene, pre-filter screen, activated carbon layer for gases and odours.
  • Electrostatic stage — gives particles a charge so they stick to plates instead of passing through. Key parts: corona wire or needle electrodes, collection plates, high-voltage supply (a few kilovolts).
  • Power and control — runs the fans, electrodes and sensors from a battery. Key parts: lithium-ion cell, charging port, fan-speed and ion-output controller.
  • Sensing — measures how dirty the surrounding air is and adjusts output. Key parts: optical particle counter, humidity and temperature sensor.
  • Delivery interface — the part that decides how much cleaned air actually reaches the user's airway. Key parts: visor or air-curtain nozzle, neck or ear mounting, soft seals.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Set a performance target. Decide how much particle reduction is needed in the breathing zone and the airflow to achieve it, in litres per minute.
  2. Make the filter media. Polypropylene fibres are melt-blown to sub-micrometre diameters, given an electrostatic charge so they capture particles by attraction as well as by sieving, then pleated to add area.
  3. Build the electrostatic stage. Electrodes and collection plates are stamped and assembled, and a small high-voltage circuit is added; ozone production must be held near zero.
  4. Assemble the air path. Fans, filter, electrodes and ducts are fitted into a housing designed to keep airflow smooth and noise low.
  5. Add electronics. Battery, charging circuit, particle sensor and control firmware are installed and calibrated against reference aerosols.
  6. Shape the wearable. The shell is moulded in lightweight plastic to sit comfortably on the neck or head, with the outlet aimed at the nose and mouth.
  7. Test in a chamber. A person or breathing mannequin sits in a chamber filled with a known aerosol while particle counters compare inhaled air with room air; leakage and comfort are also measured.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Melt-blown electret filter media — the same material class used in respirators and HVAC filters, but at a small, low-power scale.
  • EXISTS Miniature brushless fans and lithium batteries — quiet fans and small cells make a wearable air path possible at all.
  • EXISTS Low-power optical particle sensors — they let the device react to real pollution instead of running at fixed output.
  • NOT YET A test standard for personal clean-air delivery — there is no agreed method to certify how much a wearable actually reduces what a person inhales.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: neck- and head-worn purifiers sold mainly in Asian markets, some with a visor feeding filtered air to the face — a personal option where outdoor air is heavily polluted.
  2. Next: independent chamber testing and a comparable performance rating — buyers can tell a real reduction in inhaled particles from a marketing claim.
  3. Next: quieter, lighter designs integrated with headphones or a light mask — devices people actually wear all day.
  4. Longer term: adaptive systems that sense the wearer's breathing and the local pollution and deliver air only when needed — longer battery life and better protection during commutes and wildfire smoke.
