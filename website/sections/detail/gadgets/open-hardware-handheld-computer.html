9Engineering Breakdown
  • Compute module — the sealed heart of the device — processor, memory and storage on one small board. Key parts: ARM or RISC-V system-on-chip, LPDDR RAM, eMMC or UFS storage, power-management IC.
  • Mainboard — carries the module and everything else, and its full schematic and board layout are published. Key parts: openly licensed PCB design, connector headers, sensor set, expansion pads.
  • Radio modules — connect to mobile and local networks, deliberately as replaceable parts. Key parts: cellular modem on a socketed module, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth chip, GNSS receiver, antennas.
  • Display and input — the human interface. Key parts: LCD or OLED panel, touch digitizer, buttons and switches, optional keyboard shell.
  • Power system — keeps it running and lets the user swap the cell. Key parts: replaceable lithium-ion cell, USB-C charging and power-delivery circuit, fuel gauge.
  • Enclosure and mechanicals — holds the parts together in a way that can be opened with ordinary tools. Key parts: injection-moulded or machined case, standard screws rather than glue, mounting points for accessories.
  • Software stack — makes the hardware usable and keeps it working after the maker moves on. Key parts: bootloader, mainline Linux kernel with device drivers, touch-friendly desktop environment, over-the-air update service.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a chip that can be documented. Everything depends on picking a processor whose datasheet and registers can be published without a non-disclosure agreement, and whose support can be merged into the mainline Linux kernel. Most mobile chips fail this test, so the choice is narrow.
  2. Design the mainboard. Engineers draw the schematic and lay out the printed circuit board with free design tools, putting the cellular modem on a socketed module so a single component can be replaced or upgraded later.
  3. Publish the design. Schematic, board files, bill of materials and mechanical drawings are released under an open licence before manufacturing, so others can inspect, correct and re-spin them.
  4. Bring up prototypes. A handful of boards are assembled by a quick-turn service; developers then write or fix drivers for the display, touch, modem and power management until the device boots and runs a standard kernel.
  5. Design the case and tooling. The case is 3D-printed through several iterations, then a steel mould is cut for injection moulding, or the design is kept small enough for machining and 3D printing by users.
  6. Certify the device. The finished design must pass radio-emissions, electrical-safety and cellular-network approval — an expensive step that must be budgeted before any production run.
  7. Run a small production batch. A contract manufacturer populates and tests the boards, the units are assembled with battery and screen, and each one is flashed and functionally tested before shipping.
  8. Maintain and supply spares. The hard part after launch: keep kernel support current, sell screens, batteries and boards individually for years, and hand the design to a community that can keep producing it.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Processors with openly published documentation — without register-level documentation, no one outside the chip vendor can write or maintain a driver.
  • PARTIAL Mainline kernel support for mobile chips — several phone chips now boot a stock kernel, but camera, modem and power management often lag for years.
  • NOT YET Open cellular modem firmware — baseband firmware remains proprietary, so even a fully open phone relies on a closed black box.
  • EXISTS Small-batch printed-circuit assembly — contract manufacturers can build a few thousand boards cheaply, which is what makes niche devices possible at all.
  • PARTIAL Open touch-friendly Linux interfaces — projects such as Phosh and Plasma Mobile provide usable shells, but app polish and battery life still trail mainstream phones.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: niche open handhelds like the PinePhone and Librem 5 ship in thousands, running community distributions such as postmarketOS — proof that a repairable, openly documented pocket computer can be bought and used daily, with real compromises.
  2. Next: better-documented SoCs and faster mainline bring-up — porting a new device drops from years to months, letting more small makers enter the market.
  3. Next: modular designs with a published parts list and guaranteed spares — a device that can be upgraded and repaired for a decade instead of replaced every three years.
  4. Longer term: repairability rules and interoperable modules spread to mainstream handsets — open-hardware practice moves from hobby scale into ordinary consumer devices.
