PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Optical engine — draws a virtual image onto the user's palm instead of using a screen. Key parts: red, green and blue laser diodes (or a single green diode), beam-combining optics, MEMS scanning mirror, exit window.
  • Depth and camera sensing — finds the palm, focuses the projection and reads the environment. Key parts: time-of-flight or stereo depth sensor, wide-angle camera, proximity and eye-safety detectors.
  • Compute and connectivity — runs the assistant locally and offloads heavy models to the cloud. Key parts: low-power system-on-chip, eSIM cellular modem, Wi-Fi and Bluetooth, microphone array.
  • Power and thermal — fits enough energy into a badge-sized body without burning the wearer. Key parts: small lithium-polymer cell, magnetic clip-on battery booster, graphite or vapour-chamber heat spreading.
  • Input and interaction — replaces touch and typing. Key parts: voice wake word and speech-to-text, hand gestures and taps, capacitive touch surface.
  • Agent software — turns camera, microphone and location into useful answers. Key parts: multimodal model running in the cloud, speech synthesis, phone and web app for settings.
  • Enclosure and mounting — keeps the device on clothing and aimed at the hand. Key parts: machined aluminium shell, magnetic backplate, clip and lanyard options.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the optical path. Choose laser wavelengths and scan angles so that a readable image lands on a palm 20–40 cm away, then model distortion and speckle in software.
  2. Fabricate the MEMS mirror. A silicon-on-insulator wafer is etched to leave a tiny suspended mirror on torsion springs, coated with a reflective layer, and diced and packaged in a vacuum or inert atmosphere.
  3. Assemble the optical engine. Laser diodes, collimators and the scanning mirror are aligned to within a few micrometres in a clean room, then sealed against dust.
  4. Build the electronics. The system-on-chip, depth sensor, camera, cellular modem and microphones are mounted on a dense circuit board small enough to sit in the housing.
  5. Solve thermal and power. Heat paths are designed so skin-contact surfaces stay below roughly 43 °C, and the battery is sized against the measured power draw of camera, modem and projector together.
  6. Calibrate each unit. Because every laser and mirror differs slightly, each device is measured on a test rig and given its own correction table so the projected image is the right shape and colour.
  7. Write the agent stack. Camera frames, speech and location are streamed to a cloud model that returns text and actions; latency budgets, wake-word detection and privacy handling are tuned on real users.
  8. Certify and support. The projector must pass laser-safety rules such as IEC 60825-1 as a Class 1 product, plus radio and battery approvals, before shipping — and cloud services must be funded for the life of the device.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Micro laser beam-scanning displays — tiny MEMS mirrors scanned by laser diodes are already built at scale for head-up and pico projectors.
  • PARTIAL Low-latency multimodal AI models — the assistant is only usable if vision, speech and answer return in about a second over a mobile network.
  • EXISTS Always-on, low-power cellular modules — the device has no screen and no phone tether, so it needs its own cheap data connection.
  • PARTIAL Skin-safe thermal design in sealed wearables — a fanless device against the body must shed several watts while staying comfortable to wear.
  • EXISTS Eye-safe proximity sensing — the projector must cut out instantly if a face enters the beam path to remain legally Class 1.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: the first commercial screenless projector wearable shipped in 2024 and its cloud service was shut down in early 2025 — hard evidence that latency, battery life, projection brightness in daylight and a subscription price all matter more than novelty.
  2. Next: AI glasses that pair cameras, speakers and near-eye displays with a phone — ambient assistance that people actually wear daily, with the display moved from the palm to the lens.
  3. Next: small on-device models handling speech and vision locally — sub-second responses and offline use, which removes the biggest complaint about cloud-only wearables.
  4. Longer term: assistants spread across earbuds, glasses and a projector that shares one identity and context — genuinely ambient computing, where the interface is chosen by situation rather than fixed to one gadget.
