PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Through CRISPR technology, the chloroplasts of plants are modified to utilize different light spectra and atmospheric gases that might be present in extraterrestrial environments. This allows for the growth of crops where traditional agriculture would not be feasible.

5Materials Used
  • CRISPR enzymes
  • genetic vectors
  • growth media
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The process involves genetic engineering and biotechnology to create seed stocks that can withstand extreme environmental conditions. This includes laboratory research, gene editing, and field trials.

7Build Process

Initial stages involve identifying target genes for modification using CRISPR technology. Subsequent steps include testing the modified plants in controlled environments before scaling up to larger scale testing and eventually commercialization.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking and other specialized processes required for genetic modification.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Photosynthetic chassis — the plant or microbe whose light-harvesting and carbon-fixing machinery is rewritten for an alien environment. Key parts: chloroplast and photosystem complexes, antenna pigments tuned to the local light spectrum, carbon-fixing enzymes.
  • Genome design toolkit — writes and installs the new genetic instructions. Key parts: CRISPR base and prime editing, synthetic gene circuits, directed-evolution and screening pipelines.
  • Sealed growth environment — recreates the pressure, temperature, humidity and gas mix the redesigned crop needs. Key parts: pressurised transparent enclosure, tuned LED arrays, gas mixing and scrubbing, thermal control, radiation shielding.
  • Substrate and nutrient loop — grows the crop without imported soil and recycles every atom. Key parts: regolith or hydroponic bed, perchlorate and salt removal, nitrogen fixation, waste and biomass recycling.
  • Stress and repair systems — keeps DNA and proteins intact under radiation and low gravity. Key parts: enhanced DNA-repair pathways, antioxidant networks, shielding pigments.
  • Sensing and autonomy — watches the crop and adjusts conditions without a crew. Key parts: spectral and fluorescence phenotyping, environmental sensors, closed-loop control software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick and characterise a chassis. Start from fast-growing, edible, robust species — lettuce, potato, duckweed or a cyanobacterium — and map how each responds to low pressure, altered gas mixes and radiation.
  2. Rewrite light capture. Engineer antenna complexes to absorb the light actually available, including far-red wavelengths, and to shed excess energy instead of being damaged by it.
  3. Rework carbon and gas handling. Tune carbon-fixing enzymes and carbon-concentrating mechanisms, and select for tolerance of high carbon dioxide, low oxygen and reduced atmospheric pressure.
  4. Add stress tolerance. Insert or upregulate DNA-repair and antioxidant systems, and test whether shielding pigments reduce radiation damage across a full life cycle.
  5. Build the enclosure. Assemble a sealed, shielded chamber with spectrally tuned lighting, pressure and humidity control, and sensors, sized so a crop can complete seed-to-seed growth.
  6. Close the nutrient loop. Feed the plants from processed regolith or hydroponic solution, strip toxic salts such as perchlorates, and return crop waste to the loop as nutrients.
  7. Test in orbit and in analog habitats. Run multi-generation trials on orbital platforms and in closed-loop habitat experiments on Earth before trusting the design off-world.
  8. Test under partial gravity. Use centrifuges, parabolic flights and, eventually, a lunar surface greenhouse to see how roots, stems and seed set behave when weight is a fraction of Earth's.
  9. Scale into a food loop. Grow the system from a few trays to a farm that supplies a meaningful share of a crew's calories, and keep it genetically stable for many generations.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Multi-gene synthetic biology and genome editing — single traits are routine; coordinating whole new metabolic pathways reliably is not.
  • PARTIAL Closed-loop life support — experiments such as ESA's MELiSSA programme and closed habitat studies have recycled air, water and some nutrients, but not food at scale.
  • PARTIAL Low-gravity and low-pressure plant biology — orbital plant-growth experiments and reduced-pressure chamber studies exist, but data across full life cycles are thin.
  • NOT YET Radiation-tolerant plant engineering — no crop has been shown to survive years of unshielded cosmic and solar radiation.
  • PARTIAL In-situ resource use for soils — regolith simulants have grown plants, but real soils carry reactive salts and perchlorates that must be removed.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: engineered crops in sealed controlled-environment agriculture, plus orbital and closed-habitat plant experiments — data on how plants behave without normal gravity, pressure and sunlight.
  2. Next: shielded surface greenhouses on the Moon or Mars growing food from local substrate — a partial reduction in the mass a mission must launch from Earth.
  3. Next: multi-generation, fully recycled food loops with autonomously controlled environments — a settlement that can feed itself rather than resupply itself.
  4. Longer term: habitat-scale agriculture engineered for a specific non-Earth atmosphere and gravity — self-sufficiency on another world — though agriculture on an actual exoplanet remains beyond any credible engineering route, since no propulsion or habitat technology can reach or support one.
