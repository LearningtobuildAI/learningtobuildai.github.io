PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Growing structure — stacks plants in layers to multiply the growing area per square metre of floor. Key parts: multi-tier racks, trays or troughs, seismic and load-bearing frame.
  • Lighting — replaces sunlight with tuned spectra, usually pink or magenta blends of red and blue. Key parts: LED arrays, dimmable drivers, reflectors.
  • Root-zone delivery — feeds water and nutrients directly to the roots. Key parts: hydroponic channels or aeroponic misters, pumps, nutrient dosing units, filtration and sterilisation.
  • Climate control — holds temperature, humidity, CO₂ and airflow in the narrow band crops tolerate. Key parts: heat pumps, dehumidifiers, fans and ducting, CO₂ injection.
  • Sensing and control — keeps every tray on the same recipe and flags problems early. Key parts: EC and pH probes, temperature and humidity sensors, camera crop monitoring, software.
  • Facade and building interface — where the idea meets architecture: glazing, insulation and heat exchange between the farm and the occupied floors. Key parts: double-skin facade, shading, heat recovery, fire separation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the site. A building with high floors, strong floor loadings, spare electrical capacity and cheap low-carbon power suits this better than a light industrial shed.
  2. Design the layers. Rack heights and aisle widths are set so plants get even light and workers or robots can reach every tray.
  3. Install the structure. Galvanised steel racks are bolted in and levelled; drainage and cable routes run underneath.
  4. Fit lighting. LED bars are mounted above each tier and tuned per crop — more red for leaf growth, more blue to shape plant form.
  5. Plumb the nutrient loop. Pumps, tanks and dosing units are connected into a closed loop; water is filtered and sterilised so it can be recirculated for weeks rather than dumped.
  6. Close the climate. The room is sealed and insulated, then heat pumps, dehumidifiers and fans are commissioned to hold the set points.
  7. Automate seeding and harvest. Seed is sown into trays or plugs, moved by conveyor or robot, and cut and packed on site.
  8. Integrate with the building. Waste heat from the LEDs is recovered for hot water or space heating; the facade is detailed for daylight, shading and fire safety.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Efficient LED lighting — lighting is the single largest electricity cost, so efficiency decides whether a crop is viable.
  • EXISTS Hydroponic and aeroponic growing — soil-free root zones cut water use and allow tight control of nutrients.
  • EXISTS Sealed climate control with heat pumps — consistent conditions are what make yields predictable indoors.
  • PARTIAL Cheap low-carbon electricity — without it, indoor staples cannot compete with field-grown grain on cost.
  • PARTIAL Automation and robotics — labour is the second biggest cost after energy; handling trays by machine changes the economics.
  • PARTIAL Crop varieties bred for indoor life — short, high-density, low-light varieties are still mostly selected rather than purpose-bred.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: warehouse-scale vertical farms supplying leafy greens and herbs to nearby supermarkets — year-round local salad with very low water use and no pesticide residue.
  2. Next: larger, more automated farms and a wider crop list such as berries and soft fruit — better use of capital and a bigger share of the fresh-produce market.
  3. Next: farms genuinely built into building skins and rooftops, sharing heat and power with occupants — food grown where it is eaten, with waste heat doing double duty.
  4. Longer term: staple crops such as wheat and rice grown economically indoors — meaningful substitution for field agriculture — only if energy becomes very cheap and clean.
