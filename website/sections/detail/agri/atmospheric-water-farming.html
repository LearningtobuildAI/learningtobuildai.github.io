PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Moisture capture unit — absorbs water vapour from air using a porous material such as a metal-organic framework (MOF) or desiccant. Key parts: MOF-coated heat exchanger, fans and ducting, air filters.
  • Desorption and condensation — releases the captured water by heating the material, then condenses the vapour into liquid. Key parts: solar thermal collector or electric heater, condenser coil, collection tray.
  • Water storage and treatment — stores water and ensures it is clean enough for irrigation. Key parts: storage tanks, filters, pumps.
  • Irrigation system — delivers water to crops with minimal loss. Key parts: drip lines, emitters, soil moisture sensors, controllers.
  • Power supply — runs fans, heaters, pumps and controls, often off-grid. Key parts: solar photovoltaic panels, batteries, inverter.
  • Control and monitoring — optimises capture and release cycles based on humidity, temperature and solar input. Key parts: humidity and temperature sensors, microcontroller, software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Assess the site. Measure humidity, temperature and solar resource over at least a year to size the system and estimate water yield.
  2. Select or synthesise the sorbent. Choose a MOF or desiccant with high water uptake at the local humidity, or procure MOF-coated heat exchangers.
  3. Assemble the capture unit. Build ducting and fans that draw air over the sorbent-coated surfaces.
  4. Install heating and condensation. Fit solar thermal collectors or electric heaters to release water, and condenser coils to collect it.
  5. Connect storage and irrigation. Plumb tanks, pumps and drip lines to deliver water to crops.
  6. Set up power and controls. Install solar panels, batteries and a controller that schedules capture cycles.
  7. Commission and test. Run repeated cycles, measure water output and quality, and tune the control parameters.
  8. Operate and maintain. Monitor performance, clean filters, and replace or regenerate the sorbent as needed.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) with high water uptake — demonstrated in laboratory and small pilot devices, but not yet mass-produced for agriculture.
  • EXISTS Solar thermal or photovoltaic power — provides the heat to release water and electricity to run fans and pumps.
  • EXISTS Drip irrigation and water storage — delivers water efficiently to crops and buffers supply.
  • NOT YET Low-cost, scalable MOF manufacturing — current synthesis is expensive and limited in volume, which keeps costs too high for staple crops.
  • EXISTS Humidity and climate data for sizing — needed to predict yield and design the system for a given location.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: commercial atmospheric water generators for drinking water and greenhouse irrigation — off-grid water in arid regions for communities and high-value crops.
  2. Next: pilot farms using MOF-based harvesters for field irrigation — proof of concept at agricultural scale.
  3. Next: scaled-up, lower-cost MOF production — broader deployment for staple crops.
  4. Longer term: integrated solar-powered water farms in deserts — agriculture in water-scarce regions without depleting aquifers.
