9Engineering Breakdown
  • Growth environment — provides stacked, controlled conditions for plant growth without soil. Key parts: vertical racks, hydroponic nutrient delivery, LED lighting, climate control (temperature, humidity, CO2).
  • Plant chassis — the engineered plant that acts as a living factory for the target protein. Key parts: fast-growing species (e.g., tobacco, lettuce, duckweed), genetic modifications for protein expression, selection markers.
  • Genetic engineering toolkit — introduces and controls the genes that make the desired protein. Key parts: CRISPR/Cas or other gene-editing tools, promoters and regulatory elements, transformation methods (Agrobacterium, biolistics).
  • Bioprocessing — harvests plant material and extracts and purifies the protein. Key parts: harvesting and homogenisation, clarification and filtration, chromatography, formulation.
  • Automation and monitoring — keeps the vertical farm running reliably and at low labour cost. Key parts: sensors for pH, nutrients, light, robotic seeding and harvesting, software for climate and fertigation control.
  • Containment — prevents engineered plants or their genes from escaping into the environment. Key parts: physical barriers, sterilisation of waste and water, biological containment (e.g., non-flowering strains).
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select and engineer the plant chassis. Choose a fast-growing, easily transformed species. Insert genes for the target protein using CRISPR or other methods, with promoters that allow controlled expression.
  2. Establish sterile cultures. Regenerate transformed plantlets or seeds under sterile conditions to ensure a uniform, contamination-free starting material.
  3. Scale up in vertical hydroponic racks. Move plantlets into stacked trays with recirculating nutrient solution, LED lighting tuned to the plant’s needs, and controlled temperature, humidity and CO2.
  4. Induce protein production. Trigger the promoter (e.g., with a chemical or a change in light) so the plants begin making the target protein. Monitor expression levels.
  5. Harvest biomass. Collect leaves or whole plants at peak expression, using automated cutting and transport to keep the material fresh.
  6. Extract and purify. Homogenise the biomass, clarify the liquid, and use chromatography or other separation steps to isolate the protein to the required purity.
  7. Quality control. Test for identity, potency, and absence of toxins, pesticides or microbial contamination. For pharmaceuticals, follow good manufacturing practice.
  8. Package and distribute. Formulate the protein for its final use—as a drug, a food ingredient, or an input for cultivated meat—and ship under appropriate conditions.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Plant genetic engineering — decades of research allow reliable transformation and expression of foreign proteins in plants.
  • EXISTS Vertical farming (hydroponics and LED lighting) — commercial vertical farms already grow leafy greens indoors at scale, proving the growth environment.
  • EXISTS Downstream protein purification — methods to extract and purify proteins from plant material are well established in the biotech industry.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory pathways for plant-made products — some plant-made pharmaceuticals are approved, but rules for vertical-grown food proteins and novel ingredients are still developing.
  • PARTIAL Cost-effective scale-up — vertical farming is energy-intensive and currently cheaper for high-value pharmaceuticals than for commodity food proteins.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: research and clinical-scale production of high-value proteins in contained vertical systems — proof that plants can make complex proteins with the right quality attributes.
  2. Next: pilot plants producing a small number of approved pharmaceuticals or food ingredients — real cost and regulatory data for vertical molecular farming.
  3. Next: on-site production of growth factors and other inputs for cultivated meat — a cheaper, more resilient supply chain for the cultivated-meat industry.
  4. Longer term: decentralised vertical molecular farms making multiple products — local production of medicines and food ingredients, reducing dependence on long supply chains.
