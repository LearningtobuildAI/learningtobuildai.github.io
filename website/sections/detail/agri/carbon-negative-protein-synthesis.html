9Engineering Breakdown
  • Gas fermentation bioreactor — house the microbes and dissolve CO2 and hydrogen into the liquid they live in. Key parts: loop or stirred-tank vessel, gas spargers and recirculation pump, pH, temperature and dissolved-gas sensors.
  • Gas supply — deliver the three feeds the microbes need: carbon dioxide, hydrogen and oxygen. Key parts: electrolyser making H2 and O2 from water, CO2 capture unit or tanker delivery, gas mixing and pressure control.
  • Microbial culture — the living catalyst — hydrogen-oxidising bacteria that build protein from CO2. Key parts: seed fermenter for culture growth, culture bank, contamination control and sterilisation.
  • Nutrient dosing — supply nitrogen, minerals and vitamins the microbes cannot make themselves. Key parts: media preparation tanks, metering pumps.
  • Harvest and drying — separate the biomass from the water and turn it into a stable powder. Key parts: centrifuge or membrane filter, spray or drum dryer.
  • Protein extraction and texturing — turn the dried biomass into ingredients that behave like meat or dairy. Key parts: cell disruption, protein purification, extrusion or blending equipment.
  • Cooling and utilities — remove the heat the exothermic fermentation releases. Key parts: heat exchangers, chillers, water treatment.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose and grow the strain. Select a hydrogen-oxidising bacterium that grows fast on CO2 and is safe to eat, then expand it from frozen vials into a seed culture.
  2. Make the hydrogen. Use renewable electricity to split water in an electrolyser. Hydrogen is the energy source; the oxygen by-product can partly feed the reactor.
  3. Capture carbon dioxide. Take CO2 from a brewery, cement plant or other concentrated waste stream — or, in principle, from the air — and clean it to food-grade purity.
  4. Run the fermentation. Feed CO2, H2, O2 and nutrients into the bioreactor at carefully controlled ratios so gas dissolves efficiently; the culture grows continuously and is drawn off.
  5. Harvest the biomass. Centrifuge or filter the broth to collect the microbial cells, then wash and pasteurise them.
  6. Dry and mill. Dry the paste into a powder that is roughly half protein by mass, with fibre, fats and vitamins alongside.
  7. Extract and formulate. Separate specific proteins where needed, then bind, extrude or blend the powder into patties, nuggets, drinks or dairy-type products.
  8. Clear regulation and scale up. Obtain novel-food approval in each market, then move from pilot vessels to larger continuous reactors with better gas transfer.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Cheap renewable electricity — hydrogen from electrolysis is the main cost, so the price of wind and solar power sets the price of the protein.
  • EXISTS Water electrolysers — they supply the hydrogen and oxygen the microbes metabolise.
  • PARTIAL Industrial gas fermentation — fermenters of tens of cubic metres run today; food-grade plants at thousands of tonnes per year are still being built.
  • PARTIAL Carbon capture from point sources or air — a clean, affordable CO2 stream is the raw material — and capturing it is what makes the process potentially carbon-negative.
  • PARTIAL Novel-food regulation — approval has been granted in a few jurisdictions, so each new market needs its own safety assessment.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot and first commercial plants producing microbial protein, with early novel-food approval — real products, real safety data and a working cost curve.
  2. Next: plants in the tens of thousands of tonnes per year using hydrogen from dedicated renewables — ingredient-scale supply for food manufacturers rather than tasters and niche products.
  3. Next: cost falling toward that of conventional protein — use as a bulk ingredient in everyday foods, not just premium alternatives.
  4. Longer term: plants sited on captured CO2 and curtailed renewable power — food production that stores carbon and does not compete for farmland — the carbon-negative case.
