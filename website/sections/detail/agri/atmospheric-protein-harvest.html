PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Gas supply — provides the hydrogen, oxygen and carbon dioxide the microbes consume. Key parts: water electrolyser, CO2 capture unit (direct air or industrial flue gas), gas mixing and compression.
  • Bioreactor — a controlled vessel where hydrogen-oxidizing bacteria grow and multiply. Key parts: stirred-tank or loop reactor, gas spargers, pH, temperature and dissolved-gas sensors.
  • Nutrient delivery — supplies minerals, nitrogen and vitamins needed for growth. Key parts: media preparation tanks, sterile filtration, dosing pumps.
  • Harvesting and dewatering — separates the bacterial biomass from the liquid broth. Key parts: centrifuge, filtration or membrane system.
  • Drying and protein processing — converts wet biomass into a stable, edible protein powder. Key parts: spray or drum dryer, cell disruption (if needed), extraction and purification.
  • Utilities and control — provides electricity, heat, cooling and automated process control. Key parts: power supply, heat exchangers, PLC/SCADA system.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a site. Co-locate with cheap renewable electricity and a source of carbon dioxide, such as a cement plant or direct air capture unit.
  2. Install an electrolyser. Use renewable electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen.
  3. Secure CO2 supply. Capture carbon dioxide from air or industrial exhaust, then purify and compress it.
  4. Build the bioreactor. Fabricate a food-grade stainless-steel vessel with gas spargers, cooling coils and sensors for pH, temperature and dissolved gases.
  5. Prepare nutrient media. Mix water with minerals, nitrogen and vitamins, then sterilise the solution.
  6. Start the culture. Inoculate the reactor with a pure strain of hydrogen-oxidizing bacteria and establish growth conditions.
  7. Run continuous fermentation. Feed hydrogen, oxygen, CO2 and nutrients while continuously removing broth containing new biomass.
  8. Harvest and dry. Centrifuge or filter the broth to collect cells, then dry them into a powder.
  9. Purify and package. Extract and refine the protein, test for safety and quality, and package for food use.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Water electrolysis — provides the hydrogen that powers microbial growth.
  • PARTIAL Direct air capture of CO2 — still energy-intensive and costly, limiting where plants can be sited.
  • PARTIAL Gas fermentation bioreactors — large-scale gas fermentation exists for fuels, but food-grade hygiene and scale need further development.
  • PARTIAL Novel food regulatory approval — some jurisdictions have approved single-cell proteins, but global clearance is incomplete.
  • EXISTS Renewable electricity — the process is energy-hungry; low-carbon power is needed for climate benefit.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot and demonstration plants producing tonne-scale protein — proof of concept and initial food applications.
  2. Next: commercial plants at 1,000+ tonnes per year with wider regulatory approvals — cost-competitive protein for mainstream foods.
  3. Next: integration with renewable energy and carbon capture, using waste CO2 — carbon-negative protein production.
  4. Longer term: global scale, contributing to food security — reduced pressure on land, water and conventional agriculture.
