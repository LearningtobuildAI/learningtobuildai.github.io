9Engineering Breakdown
  • Expert networks — many small feed-forward networks, each expected to specialise in different patterns; only a few run for any given token. Key parts: two or three linear layers per expert, activation function.
  • Router (gating network) — scores every expert for each token and picks the top few to run. Key parts: small linear gate, top-k selection, score normalisation.
  • Shared experts — a small set of experts that always run, absorbing common knowledge so the routed experts can specialise. Key parts: always-active feed-forward blocks.
  • Load-balancing mechanism — stops the router sending everything to a handful of experts, which would waste the rest. Key parts: expert-usage statistics, per-expert bias adjustments or an auxiliary loss.
  • Attention and representation layers — understand context; in this family the key/value cache is compressed into a low-rank latent to cut memory. Key parts: multi-head attention, low-rank key/value compression, normalisation layers.
  • Distributed training and serving stack — spreads experts across many accelerators and shuttles tokens to whichever expert needs them. Key parts: expert-parallel partitioning, all-to-all interconnect, low-precision arithmetic, inference batching and caching.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the sparsity budget. Choose layer count, how many experts per layer, how finely they are split, and how many are active per token — this sets the cost/quality trade-off.
  2. Write the expert and router code. Implement the expert feed-forward blocks, the top-k gate, and the shared-expert path in a training framework such as PyTorch.
  3. Add balancing and stability tricks. Bias terms nudged up or down according to each expert's load, plus careful initialisation, keep training from collapsing onto few experts.
  4. Partition across accelerators. Experts are placed on different devices; tokens are dispatched to their chosen experts and results returned, so fast interconnects matter more than raw compute.
  5. Pre-train on a large text corpus. The model learns to predict the next token at scale, with the router gradually sorting itself into specialisms.
  6. Post-train and align. Supervised fine-tuning and preference-based reinforcement learning shape the base model into a helpful assistant.
  7. Quantise and serve. Weights are stored at low precision and an inference engine batches requests so that only the needed experts are loaded and run per token.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Transformer architecture — experts replace the feed-forward blocks of an otherwise standard transformer.
  • EXISTS Sparse mixture-of-experts training — the core idea of routing tokens to a subset of parameters is well established and now widely reproduced.
  • EXISTS High-bandwidth accelerator interconnect — every token must be shipped to its experts and back; slow links make the savings disappear.
  • EXISTS Low-precision training and inference — eight-bit formats cut memory and bandwidth enough to make very large sparse models economic.
  • PARTIAL Routing and load-balancing research — getting experts to specialise usefully rather than collapse is still an active, partly empirical problem.
  • EXISTS Large-scale distributed training infrastructure — thousands of accelerators must run for weeks without a failure ending the run.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: open-weight sparse models served cheaply by many providers — frontier-class capability at a fraction of dense-model inference cost.
  2. Next: finer expert granularity and learned, hardware-aware routing — better quality per unit of compute and less wasted communication.
  3. Next: sparse models extended to images, audio and long agentic tasks — one economical model handling many modalities.
  4. Longer term: experts that can be swapped or updated independently after training — cheap specialisation and patching without retraining the whole network.
