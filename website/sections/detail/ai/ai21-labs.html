PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Data pipeline — assembles the trillions of words of text the model learns from. Key parts: web crawl, deduplication and near-duplicate filtering, quality and safety classifiers, licence and provenance tracking.
  • Tokeniser and embeddings — converts text into the integer tokens and vectors the network can process. Key parts: byte-pair or similar subword tokeniser, vocabulary, embedding matrix, positional encoding.
  • Transformer network — the core stack of layers that predicts the next token. Key parts: multi-head self-attention, feed-forward blocks, normalisation layers, residual connections.
  • Training cluster — provides the compute to train a model that may take months. Key parts: tens of thousands of accelerators, high-bandwidth interconnect between them, parallelism software (data, tensor and pipeline), checkpointing and failure recovery.
  • Post-training and alignment — turns a raw next-token predictor into something that follows instructions safely. Key parts: instruction-tuning datasets, human preference comparisons, reward model or direct preference optimisation, safety filters and refusal behaviour.
  • Serving stack — runs the trained model for many users at once. Key parts: inference engine, key-value cache, request batching, quantisation, rate limits and guardrails.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Assemble and clean the corpus. Text is crawled or licensed, filtered for quality, deduplicated at scale and stripped of personal data before training.
  2. Train the tokeniser. A subword vocabulary is learned statistically from the corpus so common words become single tokens and rare ones are split.
  3. Fix the architecture and scale. Engineers choose parameter count, depth, attention variant and context length — trade-offs between capability, cost and latency.
  4. Pre-train. The model repeatedly predicts the next token across trillions of tokens, adjusting billions of weights over weeks or months on a large accelerator cluster.
  5. Supervised fine-tuning. Curated question-and-answer pairs teach the model to respond to instructions rather than merely continue text.
  6. Preference tuning. People rank pairs of answers; a reward model or direct optimisation method then nudges the model toward the preferred style and content.
  7. Evaluate and red-team. Benchmarks and adversarial testing probe reasoning, factuality, bias and misuse before release.
  8. Serve and monitor. The model is quantised and deployed behind an API with batching, caching and content filters, then monitored for drift and abuse.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Transformer architecture — attention lets one model learn from vastly more data than earlier recurrent networks.
  • EXISTS Dense accelerator clusters — training frontier models needs thousands of chips wired together with very fast links.
  • EXISTS Large-scale distributed training software — without parallelism and checkpointing, runs of weeks would fail before finishing.
  • EXISTS Human preference and annotation data — alignment and evaluation depend on large, well-managed pools of human judgements.
  • PARTIAL Efficient long-context attention — attention cost grows quickly with length, limiting how much a model can read at once.
  • NOT YET Verifiable reasoning and factuality — models still generate confident errors, which limits use in high-stakes work.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: API-served frontier models with billions of parameters; vendors such as AI21 Labs ship both dense and hybrid state-space/attention models — wide commercial use of text generation, summarisation and coding assistance.
  2. Next: cheaper long-context and mixture-of-experts designs, plus small distilled models running on devices — lower cost per query and offline or privacy-preserving use.
  3. Next: tool-using agents with memory and retrieval over private data — automation of multi-step knowledge work under human review.
  4. Longer term: training methods that reward verifiable reasoning rather than fluent text — reliable use in science, engineering and regulated industries — the open problem.
