9Engineering Breakdown
  • Corpus ingestion — builds the raw record of what is being researched, patented, funded and hired for. Key parts: patent and preprint databases, grant and funding registries, job postings and code repositories, news and product-launch feeds.
  • Entity resolution and taxonomy — turns messy names into stable things — one organisation, one technology, one concept. Key parts: author and assignee disambiguation, concept tagging, embedding index, versioned technology taxonomy.
  • Signal detection — finds weak, early traces before they become obvious. Key parts: citation-graph velocity, burst and novelty detection, topic and community clustering, cross-domain co-occurrence analysis.
  • Forecasting models — extrapolates each signal into a dated expectation with uncertainty. Key parts: time-series and sequence models, diffusion/adoption curve fitting, survival models for time-to-maturity, ensemble and calibration layer.
  • Evaluation harness — the part that keeps the system honest. Key parts: historical backtesting ('hindcasting'), scoring against later-confirmed outcomes, bias and survivorship audits.
  • Analyst interface — presents a forecast with its evidence, not just a number. Key parts: retrieval and citation tools, conflict and contradiction flags, confidence display, feedback capture.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Assemble the corpora. Pull decades of patents, papers, grants, job ads and product announcements, with licence terms recorded per source so the training data can legally be used.
  2. Normalise and deduplicate. Resolve 'who' and 'what' consistently: merge spelling variants, split namesakes, and map free text onto a controlled taxonomy of technologies.
  3. Build the signals. Convert the corpus into time series — new entrants per field, citation velocity, co-authorship between previously separate fields, hiring shifts — each timestamped so nothing seen later leaks into earlier predictions.
  4. Detect emergence. Flag clusters that are growing faster than their field's baseline, and, more importantly, clusters forming at the boundary between two fields, which is where genuinely new capabilities usually appear.
  5. Fit forecasting models. Train models to map an early signal pattern to a later outcome — shipped product, commercial deployment, or fade-out — using only information available at the time of the prediction.
  6. Backtest honestly. Re-run the system as if standing in past years and score its forecasts against what actually happened, including the predictions it never made and the ones that quietly failed.
  7. Calibrate uncertainty. Adjust stated confidence so that '70% likely' events really do occur about 70% of the time, and refuse to output a point estimate without a range and a date.
  8. Wrap in an agent loop. Give the model tools to retrieve evidence, follow citations and check its own claims, so every forecast can be traced back to the records that produced it.
  9. Deploy with human review. Analysts triage outputs, record where the system was wrong, and those judgements feed back into the taxonomy and the calibration layer.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Large machine-readable corpora of research and patents — forecasting needs decades of dated records, and much historical text is still paywalled or un-digitised.
  • EXISTS Transformer language models — they supply the embeddings and concept extraction that make cross-domain similarity searchable.
  • EXISTS Citation and collaboration graph infrastructure — the structure of who-cites-whom and who-works-with-whom carries much of the early signal.
  • NOT YET Labelled datasets of technology emergence and failure — no large, unbiased record exists of which early signals actually led to deployed technology; without it, models are tuned on anecdotes.
  • NOT YET Methods to separate substance from hype — publication and funding counts rise with fashion as well as with real progress, and current models cannot reliably tell the two apart.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: retrieval-and-summarise assistants over patent and literature databases — faster expert scanning, with the model as a search tool rather than an oracle.
  2. Next: calibrated short-horizon forecasts (roughly 1–3 years) that are backtested and published with their error rates — usable early warning for research funders and engineering teams.
  3. Next: shared benchmark suites and open hindcasting for technology forecasting — comparable claims, and honest measurement of what machine forecasting can and cannot do.
  4. Longer term: combining text signals with cost, materials and manufacturing constraints — forecasts that account for whether something can actually be built and shipped, not merely published.
  5. Longer term: audited decision-support systems in research and industrial strategy — portfolio choices informed by evidence — provided the limits of prediction are stated up front.
