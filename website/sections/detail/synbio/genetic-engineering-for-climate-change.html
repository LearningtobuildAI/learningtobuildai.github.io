PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Host organism (chassis) — the cell that is rewritten to live on carbon dioxide or to make a fuel molecule. Key parts: engineered bacteria or yeast, photosynthetic cyanobacteria, acetogens that already eat waste gases.
  • Carbon-fixing pathway — the enzyme chemistry that pulls CO2 into the cell's metabolism. Key parts: RuBisCO and carboxysome compartments, synthetic CO2-fixing cycles built from many enzymes, energy and electron supply (ATP, NADPH or hydrogen).
  • Genetic toolkit — the instruments used to design, insert and tune new genes. Key parts: DNA synthesis, CRISPR genome editing, promoters and regulatory parts, automated screening and lab evolution.
  • Bioreactor and gas delivery — feeds CO2 to cells fast enough for them to grow. Key parts: gas fermenters or photobioreactors, mixing and mass-transfer systems, light or hydrogen supply, temperature and pH control.
  • Product recovery — separates the fuel or chemical from water and cells — often the most energy-hungry step. Key parts: cell separation, distillation or membrane separation, drying.
  • Containment and measurement — keeps modified organisms out of the environment and proves how much carbon was really captured. Key parts: biocontainment and kill switches, carbon accounting and life-cycle analysis.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the target molecule. Decide whether the goal is a fuel, a chemical feedstock or durable stored carbon, because that sets the whole pathway.
  2. Design the metabolic route. Map which enzymes are needed to convert CO2 plus an energy source into the product, and which existing genes to delete.
  3. Build the DNA. Genes are synthesised chemically and assembled into constructs, then inserted into the host with CRISPR or similar tools.
  4. Screen and evolve. Thousands of strains are grown in small wells; the best are repeatedly selected and mutated to improve growth and yield.
  5. Prove it in a bioreactor. The winning strain runs in a litre-scale reactor where gas supply, pH and temperature are controlled.
  6. Close the carbon balance. Measure CO2 in, product out and energy used, to check that the process stores more carbon than it emits.
  7. Scale up. Move to cubic-metre reactors, where mixing, gas transfer and contamination become the hard problems.
  8. Recover and use the product. Separate the fuel or chemical, then burn it, sell it, or store it permanently depending on the goal.
  9. Contain and monitor. Deploy biocontainment, waste treatment and environmental monitoring before any outdoor or large-scale use.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Cheap DNA synthesis and genome editing — without fast, low-cost gene construction and CRISPR editing, each strain takes years instead of weeks.
  • PARTIAL Fast, accurate carbon-fixing enzymes — RuBisCO, nature's main CO2-fixing enzyme, is slow; synthetic alternatives have been shown only in test tubes.
  • PARTIAL Organisms that grow on CO2 alone — bacteria engineered to use CO2 as their sole carbon source were reported in 2019, but they grow slowly and need extra energy.
  • PARTIAL Low-carbon energy or hydrogen input — capturing CO2 takes energy; if that energy comes from fossil fuels the climate benefit shrinks or disappears.
  • NOT YET Biocontainment and regulation — releasing engineered organisms at industrial scale needs containment rules that do not yet widely exist.
  • PARTIAL Cheap product separation — recovering dilute fuel from water can consume more energy than the fuel contains.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: engineered enzymes and yeasts already make ethanol and other fuels from crops and from steel-mill waste gases — a working industrial base of fermentation, reactors and product recovery to build on.
  2. Next: engineered autotrophs producing chemicals and fuels at pilot scale from concentrated CO2 streams — the first realistic pairing of a CO2 source with a living factory.
  3. Next: full life-cycle proof that a plant captures more carbon than the process emits — credible carbon accounting, without which the technology cannot claim a climate benefit.
  4. Longer term: products that lock carbon away for decades, or fuels that displace fossil ones, at commercial scale — a supplement to geological CO2 storage and to electrification, mainly where waste gases and cheap low-carbon energy are both available.
