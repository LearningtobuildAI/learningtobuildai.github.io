1Definition

Synthetic Carbon Capture Organisms are engineered microorganisms designed to hyper-efficiently fix carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, converting it directly into stable biopolymers. These organisms are optimized for the Calvin cycle and incorporate new synthetic metabolic pathways.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These organisms are genetically modified to enhance their ability to capture CO2 through the optimization of the Calvin cycle, a process used by plants to convert carbon dioxide into organic compounds. Additionally, they have been engineered with synthetic metabolic pathways that allow them to directly convert captured CO2 into biopolymers, which can be harvested and utilized in various applications.

5Materials Used
  • Bacteria
  • Algae
  • CRISPR-Cas9
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves genetic engineering of microorganisms, typically bacteria or algae, to include synthetic metabolic pathways that enhance their carbon fixation capabilities. These organisms are then cultured under controlled conditions for mass production.

7Build Process

The build process includes gene synthesis and assembly, followed by the integration of these genes into host microorganisms using techniques such as CRISPR-Cas9. The resulting organisms are then tested in laboratory settings before scaling up to industrial-scale operations.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Engineered microbe — the living catalyst that fixes CO2 and converts it into biopolymer. Key parts: chassis organism (e.g. cyanobacteria, acetogen), genetic circuits for carbon fixation, genes for polymer synthesis.
  • CO2 supply and delivery — provides concentrated CO2 to the bioreactor. Key parts: gas capture unit, compression and storage, sparging or membrane delivery system.
  • Bioreactor — controlled environment for growth and production. Key parts: photobioreactor or fermenter vessel, mixing and mass transfer system, temperature, pH and dissolved gas sensors.
  • Metabolic pathway — converts CO2 into precursor molecules for polymer synthesis. Key parts: carbon fixation cycle (e.g. Calvin-Benson, Wood-Ljungdahl), energy supply (light or chemical reductant), flux-balancing enzymes.
  • Polymer synthesis — assembles precursors into a stable biopolymer. Key parts: polymer synthase genes (e.g. PHA synthase), precursor pathway enzymes, regulatory elements for pathway control.
  • Downstream processing — harvests cells and extracts the biopolymer. Key parts: centrifuge or filtration, cell disruption unit, solvent extraction and drying.
  • Containment and safety — prevents escape and survival of engineered organisms. Key parts: genetic kill switches, physical containment (closed vessels), sterilisation and waste treatment.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select chassis organism. Choose a fast-growing, genetically tractable microbe that can use CO2 as its main carbon source, such as a cyanobacterium or an acetogen.
  2. Engineer carbon fixation. Introduce or enhance genes for efficient CO2 fixation, possibly using a synthetic or optimised natural cycle to boost carbon uptake.
  3. Optimise metabolic flux. Tune gene expression and enzyme activity so most fixed carbon flows toward the desired polymer precursor rather than being wasted.
  4. Add polymer pathway. Insert genes for biopolymer synthesis, such as polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) enzymes, so the cell accumulates the polymer as granules.
  5. Design bioreactor. Build a closed system that delivers CO2 efficiently to the culture, controls light or chemical energy, and maintains optimal growth conditions.
  6. Scale up. Move from lab flasks to pilot-scale bioreactors, testing gas transfer rates, productivity and contamination control at larger volumes.
  7. Harvest and extract. Develop cost-effective methods to separate cells from broth, break them open, and purify the biopolymer without degrading it.
  8. Test stability and safety. Confirm the polymer is stable for its intended use and that the engineered organism cannot survive or spread outside the bioreactor.
  9. Life-cycle analysis. Verify net carbon removal and economic viability, including energy use, CO2 source, and end-of-life for the polymer.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Synthetic biology tools — gene synthesis, CRISPR editing and promoter libraries are needed to rewire microbial metabolism.
  • EXISTS Gas fermentation — commercial plants already use microbes to ferment industrial waste gases into ethanol, proving the core process.
  • PARTIAL Biopolymer production pathways — many microbes make PHA naturally, but engineering them to do so efficiently from CO2 at scale is not yet demonstrated.
  • PARTIAL Efficient CO2 capture — capturing CO2 from flue gas or air is technically feasible but still costly and energy-intensive.
  • PARTIAL Bioreactor design for gas-to-liquid — gas fermenters exist, but optimising them for CO2 and polymer accumulation remains a scale-up challenge.
  • PARTIAL Containment strategies — kill switches and physical barriers have been demonstrated in labs, but long-term reliability at industrial scale is unproven.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Lab proof-of-concept: engineered microbes produce measurable biopolymer from CO2 in small-scale cultures. — validation of the core metabolic design.
  2. Pilot demonstration: integrated pilot plant runs continuously on a real CO2 source, such as cement or steel off-gas. — data on productivity, stability and operating costs.
  3. Commercial demonstration: first plants converting captured CO2 to biopolymers at kiloton-per-year scale. — a market-ready product and a credible carbon-removal route.
  4. Global scale-up: widespread adoption linked to carbon capture networks and material supply chains. — meaningful atmospheric carbon drawdown alongside fossil-free materials.
