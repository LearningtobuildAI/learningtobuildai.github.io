PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

De novo protein design is a computational approach that involves designing and synthesizing entirely new protein structures from scratch, without relying on existing natural proteins as templates.

Category
Biotechnology
Best use
Creating entirely new proteins with specific functions.
Stage
NOW
2Problem It Solves

It addresses the limitations of traditional protein engineering methods, which often rely on modifying existing proteins rather than creating entirely new ones with novel functions.

3Lifecycle / Journey Stage
early commercial
PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The process begins by defining the desired function of the protein. Computational algorithms are then used to predict potential sequences and structures that could achieve this function. Once promising designs are identified, they are synthesized in the lab and tested for their ability to fold into the predicted structure and perform the intended function.

5Materials Used
  • DNA
  • Protein synthesis reagents
  • Expression vectors
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves synthesizing the designed DNA sequences using techniques like PCR amplification followed by gene synthesis. These genes are then inserted into expression vectors and expressed in host cells such as bacteria or yeast to produce the protein.

7Build Process

The build process includes computational design, sequence optimization, protein synthesis, and functional testing. Computational tools play a crucial role in predicting structural stability and function before physical synthesis.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Computational design engine — generates protein backbones and amino-acid sequences that fold into a desired shape. Key parts: backbone generation algorithms, sequence design software, energy functions and scoring, machine-learning models.
  • DNA synthesis and assembly — turns the designed sequence into physical DNA. Key parts: oligonucleotide synthesis, polymerase assembly, error correction, cloning vectors.
  • Expression system — makes the protein from the DNA. Key parts: host cells (bacteria, yeast, mammalian), expression plasmids, fermentation or cell-free reactors.
  • Purification and analytics — isolates the protein and checks its purity. Key parts: chromatography columns, mass spectrometry, electrophoresis.
  • Structural and functional testing — verifies that the protein actually folds and works as intended. Key parts: cryo-electron microscopy, X-ray crystallography, binding assays, stability measurements.
  • Optimization loop — improves the design iteratively. Key parts: directed evolution, deep mutational scanning, feedback into design software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the target function. Decide what the protein must do — for example, bind a specific receptor, catalyse a reaction, or form a particular scaffold.
  2. Generate a backbone. Software proposes a three-dimensional protein fold that can present the required functional groups in the right geometry.
  3. Design the sequence. Amino acids are chosen so the chain will fold into that backbone and carry out the function, avoiding unwanted interactions.
  4. Synthesise the gene. The DNA sequence is assembled from short oligonucleotides or ordered from a commercial supplier.
  5. Express the protein. The gene is inserted into host cells, which are grown and induced to produce the protein.
  6. Purify the protein. Cells are broken open and the protein is separated from other material using chromatography.
  7. Characterise the design. Structure is solved by cryo-electron microscopy or X-ray crystallography, and function is tested with binding or activity assays.
  8. Optimise and iterate. If the protein is unstable or inactive, mutations are made and the cycle repeats until it meets the specification.
  9. Scale up and formulate. Successful designs are produced in larger quantities and prepared for their final use, such as a drug or an industrial catalyst.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Protein structure prediction — knowing how sequence maps to shape is the foundation for designing a new fold.
  • PARTIAL Generative machine-learning models for proteins — tools can now propose plausible backbones, but reliability varies with the target function.
  • EXISTS Low-cost DNA synthesis — making a designed gene quickly and cheaply is essential for testing many variants.
  • EXISTS High-throughput structural biology — cryo-electron microscopy and X-ray crystallography confirm that the design folded as intended.
  • EXISTS Recombinant protein expression — without a reliable way to produce the protein, a design remains only a computer file.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: designed proteins used as research reagents and as clinical candidates for vaccines and antivirals — proof that de novo designs can function in living systems.
  2. Next: first approved medicines built on de novo designed proteins — a new route to drugs that are hard to make by other means.
  3. Next: designed enzymes for industrial catalysis and environmental clean-up — replacing some chemical processes with milder, more specific biocatalysis.
  4. Longer term: designed proteins as components of smart materials and molecular machines — programmable matter at the nanoscale, from responsive gels to targeted delivery systems.
