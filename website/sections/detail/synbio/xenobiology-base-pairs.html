PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Xenobiology Base Pairs refers to the use of synthetic DNA containing non-natural base pairs (XNA), which can potentially expand the genetic coding system beyond the standard four bases found in nature.

Category
Genetics
Stage
LAB
2Problem It Solves

Current limitations in genetic engineering due to the constraints imposed by the standard four-base DNA system. By expanding this system, researchers aim to create new biological functions that cannot be achieved with conventional DNA.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The process involves synthesizing unnatural nucleotides that specifically pair with each other, allowing for the creation of new DNA structures. These XNA molecules can then be used to encode and express novel amino acids and proteins, which are not possible using only natural DNA.

5Materials Used
  • Unnatural nucleotides
  • Chemical reagents
  • Specialized equipment
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves chemical synthesis of unnatural nucleotides followed by polymerization into XNA strands. This requires specialized equipment and reagents for precise control over base-pairing specificity and stability.

7Build Process

First, the design of specific unnatural bases is carried out through computational modeling to ensure they can form stable pairs with each other. Then, these bases are synthesized chemically before being incorporated into DNA strands via polymerization reactions.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking required for synthesis processes.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Unnatural base-pair chemistry — designed nucleotides that pair only with each other and not with A, T, G or C. Key parts: modified purine and pyrimidine bases, hydrophobic packing or extra hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor patterns, protecting groups for synthesis.
  • Nucleotide supply — provides the raw building blocks, both for test-tube work and for feeding cells. Key parts: multistep nucleoside synthesis, triphosphate conversion, phosphoramidites for solid-phase oligonucleotide synthesis.
  • Replicating polymerases — enzymes that copy, amplify and transcribe the extra letters. Key parts: engineered DNA polymerases, reverse transcriptases, error-prone variants for directed evolution.
  • Cellular import and retention — gets the building block into a living cell and keeps the pair through cell division. Key parts: nucleoside triphosphate transporters, plasmid carrying the extra pair, selection markers.
  • Expanded translation machinery — reads a codon built from the new letters and inserts a non-natural amino acid. Key parts: orthogonal transfer RNA, orthogonal aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, ribosome.
  • XNA backbones — alternative sugar–phosphate backbones that still carry genetic information and can be evolved. Key parts: threose, hexitol, glycol, cyclohexenyl and arabino nucleic acids, XNA polymerases, XNA enzymes (catalysts) and binders.
  • Containment — stops an expanded organism from surviving outside the lab. Key parts: auxotrophy for the unnatural building block, kill switches, biocontainment strains.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the pair. Pick a recognition chemistry — hydrophobic packing or an unnatural pattern of hydrogen bonds — with the right shape so the two partners pair tightly but ignore the four natural bases. Model the geometry before making anything.
  2. Synthesize the nucleosides. Build the modified base and its sugar by multistep organic chemistry, then convert it two ways: to a triphosphate for enzymes, and to a phosphoramidite for chemical DNA synthesis.
  3. Test it in a test tube. A polymerase extends a primer, inserting the unnatural triphosphate opposite its partner. Measure how efficiently and how faithfully this happens, and how often the wrong letter goes in.
  4. Evolve the polymerase. Make large libraries of mutant polymerases and select the variants that copy the pair best, repeating over several rounds; do the same for reverse transcriptases and RNA polymerases.
  5. Write it into a genome. Chemically synthesize an oligonucleotide containing the pair, splice it into a plasmid, and introduce the plasmid plus a transporter protein so the cell can import the unnatural triphosphate from its growth medium.
  6. Add expanded translation. Introduce an orthogonal tRNA and synthetase pair that reads a codon made from the new letters and attaches a non-natural amino acid, so the extra information becomes protein.
  7. Make alternative backbones. For XNA, replace the natural sugar with another ring and evolve polymerases that synthesize, reverse-transcribe and mutate it, so the molecule can be copied and selected like DNA.
  8. Lock it down. Tie survival to being fed the unnatural building block, add fail-safes, and verify the organism cannot grow or pass the extra pair on without laboratory supply.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Solid-phase oligonucleotide synthesis — lets researchers write defined stretches of DNA, including a site reserved for an unnatural pair.
  • EXISTS Directed evolution of polymerases — natural enzymes mostly reject unnatural nucleotides, so they must be re-engineered to copy them.
  • EXISTS Structural biology of polymerase–DNA complexes — shows which parts of the enzyme block the bulky or differently shaped extra bases.
  • PARTIAL Triphosphate transporters for cell uptake — known transporters import the building block into microbes, but only for a narrow set of compounds and with limited efficiency.
  • PARTIAL Orthogonal tRNA and synthetase pairs — they let a new codon mean a new amino acid, but only a few such pairs work cleanly in a given organism.
  • NOT YET Biocontainment for synthetic genomes — there is no agreed, proven way to guarantee an organism with an expanded alphabet can never escape or exchange genes.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: six- to eight-letter DNA copied in test tubes, and bacteria that carry one extra base pair in their genome — proof that heredity can run on more than four letters.
  2. Next: cells that use several new codons at once to build proteins with multiple non-natural amino acids — enzymes and materials with chemistry that natural proteins cannot perform.
  3. Next: XNA binders and catalysts entering preclinical and early clinical evaluation — medicines that resist the body's DNA- and RNA-degrading enzymes.
  4. Longer term: fully orthogonal, self-contained genetic systems with their own safety locks — long-lived information storage, new biomanufacturing routes, and screening rules for organisms that no natural predator can read.
