PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Minimal & Synthetic Genomes involve creating cells with either the smallest possible genome or a fully rewritten one. This technology aims to understand the essential genes required for life by reducing complexity and studying the core functions necessary for cellular survival.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Genomes are synthesized chemically or trimmed gene-by-gene through genetic engineering techniques. The process involves identifying and removing non-essential genes while ensuring that the remaining genes can support cell viability. This is achieved using computational models, experimental biology, and iterative refinement to determine the minimal set of genes required for a cell to function.

5Materials Used
  • DNA
  • Reagents
  • Bacterial or yeast cells
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves chemical synthesis of DNA sequences, followed by their integration into bacterial or yeast cells. This can be done through electroporation, conjugation, or other gene transfer methods.

7Build Process

Genomes are built using a combination of computational design to predict minimal sets and experimental validation to test the viability of these designs in living organisms. The process often involves iterative cycles of genome reduction, transplantation into host cells, and functional testing.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Genome design layer — software that specifies which genes stay, which go, and in what order. Key parts: annotation and essentiality datasets, codon-recoding tools, watermark sequences, assembly planning.
  • DNA synthesis and assembly — turns the design into physical DNA, from short pieces up to whole chromosomes. Key parts: oligonucleotide synthesisers, error correction, in-vitro assembly (Gibson-style), yeast or bacterial hosts for stitching large fragments.
  • Delivery and transplantation — gets a genome-sized piece of DNA into a living cell. Key parts: spheroplast fusion / genome transplantation, chromosome-scale editing, selection markers.
  • Minimisation analysis — finds out which of the genes are genuinely needed. Key parts: transposon mutagenesis, growth-rate assays, single-cell screening, comparative genomics.
  • Chassis cell — the host organism whose genome is being rebuilt or trimmed. Key parts: fast-growing bacterium (e.g. Mycoplasma, E. coli), budding yeast, growth media, containment provisions.
  • Verification — proves the written sequence matches the living cell. Key parts: whole-genome sequencing, phenotypic tests, proteomics and metabolomics.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick a chassis. Start with an organism that grows fast and has a naturally small genome, so the target is tractable.
  2. Design the genome on a computer. Annotate every gene, mark the ones known to be essential, strip out the rest, rewrite codons to a chosen set, and insert harmless watermark sequences so the synthetic DNA can be told apart from natural DNA.
  3. Synthesise short DNA. Build the sequence chemically as many thousands of short oligonucleotides, a few hundred bases each.
  4. Assemble into longer pieces. Join the oligonucleotides in vitro into cassettes of a few thousand base pairs, then correct errors before scaling up further.
  5. Stitch in a living carrier. Combine cassettes step by step inside yeast, which tolerates very large foreign DNA, until a complete genome of around half a million to several million base pairs is assembled.
  6. Transplant or replace. Move the finished genome into a recipient cell that has had its own DNA removed, or swap it into a native chromosome segment by segment, as in the synthetic yeast projects.
  7. Boot the cell. Select for cells in which the synthetic genome takes over and drives normal growth and division; sequence them to confirm nothing was lost.
  8. Trim to the minimum. Scramble genes one at a time with transposons to test which can be deleted; the smallest bacterial cell built this way carried 473 genes, and roughly a third of those still have no known function.
  9. Test as a chassis. Once the gene set is known and stable, add engineered pathways on top to see whether a minimal cell is a more predictable factory than a natural one.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Accurate large-scale DNA synthesis — cost per base pair has fallen enormously, but assembling millions of error-free base pairs is still slow and expensive.
  • EXISTS Whole-genome sequencing — without cheap, fast reading of DNA you cannot check that the synthesised genome is correct.
  • PARTIAL Genome transplantation and large-DNA delivery — moving a whole genome into a cell has been done in a few bacterial species, not routinely.
  • PARTIAL Gene-essentiality data — you can only delete what you know is dispensable; a large share of minimal-cell genes are still unexplained.
  • PARTIAL Containment and biosecurity practice — cells built from scratch remove natural barriers, so engineering controls must replace them.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: a viable bacterial cell running on a chemically synthesised genome of 473 genes; individual synthetic chromosomes running inside yeast — proof that a genome can be written rather than copied.
  2. Next: a wholly synthetic eukaryotic genome, with a yeast cell carrying entirely man-made chromosomes — design rules for larger, more complex cells.
  3. Next: streamlined industrial chassis with no unexplained genes and built-in virus resistance through codon recoding — fermentation that is easier to control and harder to infect.
  4. Longer term: genomes designed for specified functions, with robust safety and screening rules — predictable engineered cells — and a serious governance problem to solve alongside them.
