PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Light absorber — soaks up sunlight and separates electrons from holes to drive the chemistry. Key parts: semiconductor photoelectrode (silicon, bismuth vanadate, hematite, or III–V tandems), protective coating against corrosion, tandem junction for extra voltage.
  • Catalysts — speed up the two half-reactions — oxidising water and reducing protons or carbon dioxide. Key parts: oxygen-evolution catalyst (cobalt phosphate, nickel–iron layered hydroxide), hydrogen- or CO2-reduction catalyst (platinum, copper, molecular complexes).
  • Separator and membrane — keeps the two products apart so they do not simply recombine or form an explosive mix. Key parts: ion-exchange membrane, bipolar or gas-diffusion barrier, flow channels.
  • Reactor and product handling — houses the electrodes, collects gases or liquids and manages water flow. Key parts: sealed cell or module, gas–liquid separators, pumps and pressure control.
  • Biological interface (biohybrid route) — uses engineered microbes or isolated enzymes to convert the captured electrons into complex molecules. Key parts: engineered bacteria or algae, light-harvesting nanoparticles or wired electrode, nutrient and culture control.
  • Downstream upgrading — turns the primary product into something worth selling. Key parts: concentration and purification, catalytic conversion to fuels or polymers.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the molecule. Decide the target — hydrogen, carbon monoxide, formate, methanol or a fine chemical — because that choice fixes the catalysts, the membrane and the whole reactor design.
  2. Grow or deposit the absorber. Crystalline silicon wafers, sputtered or solution-grown oxide films, or epitaxial III–V layers are prepared and stacked into a junction that delivers enough voltage to split water.
  3. Deposit the catalysts. Catalyst films are electrodeposited or photodeposited directly onto the absorber, so the active sites sit within nanometres of where the charges arrive.
  4. Protect the surface. Thin oxides, nitrides or atomic-layer coatings seal the absorber against corrosion, which is what usually kills these devices first.
  5. Assemble the cell. Photoanode and photocathode (or a photovoltaic wired to dark electrodes) are mounted either side of a membrane in a sealed housing with inlets and outlets.
  6. Wire up the biology, if used. For biohybrid systems, microbes are cultured and then coupled to the electrode or to semiconductor particles that inject electrons into their metabolism.
  7. Test under a solar simulator. Efficiency is measured as solar-to-hydrogen or solar-to-chemical conversion and logged against time to find the degradation rate.
  8. Scale into modules. Many small cells are manifolded together with shared gas collection, because large single electrodes lose efficiency and are hard to seal.
  9. Run outdoors. Field trials expose the module to real weather, dust and temperature swings, the conditions that decide whether it lasts years rather than days.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Efficient, stable photoelectrodes — lab devices reach only a few per cent solar-to-fuel efficiency and degrade within hours to days, while commercial targets sit near 10% over thousands of hours.
  • PARTIAL Earth-abundant catalysts — the best catalysts rely on platinum and iridium; cheap alternatives work in the lab but not yet durably at scale.
  • NOT YET Product separation and safety — hydrogen and oxygen made in the same cell must be kept apart, and dilute liquid products must be concentrated economically.
  • PARTIAL Engineered microbes and enzymes for the biohybrid route — bacteria have been driven by light-harvesting particles in the lab, but yields, lifetimes and sterility are far from industrial.
  • EXISTS A cost benchmark to beat — solar panels plus commercial electrolysers already deliver around 20% sunlight-to-hydrogen, so artificial photosynthesis must undercut an existing product, not just work.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: millimetre-scale lab cells splitting water or reducing carbon dioxide for hours at a few per cent efficiency — understanding of which absorbers, catalysts and coatings survive contact with each other.
  2. Next: sealed modules that hold roughly 10% efficiency for a thousand hours with separated products — credible pilot plants and honest cost estimates.
  3. Next: first commercial niches in high-value chemicals such as hydrogen peroxide or specialty intermediates — revenue that funds development before bulk fuels become viable.
  4. Longer term: solar-fuel plants competing with photovoltaic-plus-electrolysis for hydrogen and drop-in fuels — chemical feedstocks and transport fuels made directly from sunlight, air and water.
