PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Xenobiology is a field of synthetic biology that involves the creation of life forms using non-canonical amino acids or nucleic acids, which are not found in nature. These xenobiotic organisms can potentially perform functions that natural life cannot.

2Problem It Solves

Xenobiology addresses the limitations of natural life by providing a platform for creating organisms with novel biochemical capabilities. This can lead to breakthroughs in biocatalysis, where enzymes or other biological molecules are used to accelerate chemical reactions that would otherwise be slow or unfeasible under natural conditions.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The process begins by engineering ribosomes and polymerases to be capable of processing unnatural building blocks. This is achieved through genetic modifications that allow the synthetic cells to incorporate these non-canonical components into their proteins or nucleic acids, thereby altering their properties and potentially enabling new functions.

5Materials Used
  • non-canonical amino acids
  • non-canonical nucleic acids
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves genetic engineering techniques and cell culture methods. It requires precise control over the synthesis of unnatural building blocks and their integration into the xenobiological system.

7Build Process

The build process includes designing the genetic modifications, synthesizing the non-canonical components, introducing them into host cells, and optimizing the expression and function of the resulting xenobiotic organisms.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Unnatural genetic polymer — stores genetic information in a non-natural alphabet, either as an expanded base pair or a xeno-nucleic acid (XNA) backbone. Key parts: unnatural base pairs (e.g. dNaM–dTPT3), XNA polymers (e.g. HNA, TNA, CeNA), modified nucleoside triphosphates.
  • Replication and transcription machinery — copies and transcribes the unnatural genetic material with high fidelity. Key parts: engineered DNA polymerases, engineered RNA polymerases, proofreading and repair enzymes.
  • Orthogonal translation system — incorporates non-canonical amino acids (ncAAs) into proteins at defined positions. Key parts: orthogonal tRNA, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, engineered ribosome, suppressor codons.
  • Metabolic supply and transport — delivers or synthesises the unnatural building blocks inside the cell. Key parts: nucleotide transporters, amino acid transporters, biosynthetic pathways for unnatural precursors.
  • Containment and safety — prevents the organism from surviving or spreading outside controlled conditions. Key parts: auxotrophy for an unnatural nutrient, kill switches, genetic safeguards.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the unnatural building block. Choose or synthesise a base pair or amino acid that is chemically stable, orthogonal to natural biochemistry, and compatible with polymerases or the ribosome.
  2. Engineer enzymes to recognise it. Use directed evolution and rational design to adapt polymerases, synthetases and other enzymes so they accept the unnatural substrate without losing fidelity.
  3. Build an orthogonal translation system. Create tRNA/synthetase pairs that charge only the non-canonical amino acid and suppress a dedicated codon, and modify the ribosome if needed for efficient incorporation.
  4. Assemble the chassis cell. Introduce the unnatural building block, transporters and engineered machinery into a robust host such as Escherichia coli, keeping the new chemistry separate from natural metabolism.
  5. Optimise and evolve. Adapt the organism to grow and replicate with the unnatural component, improving uptake, retention, fidelity and yield over many generations.
  6. Test containment. Verify that the organism depends on the supplied unnatural nutrient and cannot survive if it escapes, using auxotrophy and kill switches.
  7. Apply for biocatalysis. Engineer metabolic pathways that use the expanded chemistry to produce novel enzymes, polymers or small molecules not possible with standard biology.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Synthetic chemistry for unnatural nucleotides and amino acids — researchers can already synthesise many modified bases, sugars and amino acids in the laboratory.
  • PARTIAL Engineered polymerases for XNA replication — some XNA polymers can be copied in the lab, but efficiency and fidelity remain far below natural DNA replication.
  • PARTIAL Orthogonal tRNA/synthetase pairs for multiple ncAAs — single ncAAs are routinely incorporated, but doing so for many different ncAAs simultaneously is still limited.
  • PARTIAL Ribosomes engineered for multiple ncAAs — modified ribosomes can incorporate a few ncAAs, but broad tolerance and high yield are not yet demonstrated.
  • PARTIAL Reliable containment strategies — auxotrophy and kill switches work in principle, but escape or mutation remains a concern for environmental release.
  • PARTIAL Understanding of minimal genomes — a reduced genome would simplify xenobiological systems, but the minimal gene set for robust growth is not fully defined.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: lab strains with one unnatural base pair and a handful of non-canonical amino acids — proof that expanded genetic and protein alphabets can be maintained in living cells.
  2. Next: organisms with multiple unnatural base pairs and dozens of ncAAs — synthesis of novel biopolymers and enzymes with new catalytic functions.
  3. Next: robust containment and scalable fermentation for xenobiological cells — safe industrial use of xenobiology for biocatalysis and materials production.
  4. Longer term: commercial production of chemicals, drugs or materials using xenobiological organisms — a new manufacturing platform that extends biology beyond its natural building blocks.
