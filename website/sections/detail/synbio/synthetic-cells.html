PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Synthetic cells are artificially constructed microorganisms or cell-like structures that mimic natural cells in performing specific biological functions. They are engineered through synthetic biology techniques to carry out precise tasks such as gene expression, biosynthesis of therapeutic molecules, and targeted drug delivery.

Biotechnology

2Problem It Solves

Synthetic cells address the limitations of traditional biotechnological approaches by offering more precise control over cellular functions and enabling novel applications like gene therapy and drug delivery that are difficult to achieve with natural cells.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These cells are typically created by assembling genetic material (DNA) into a minimalistic genome or by encapsulating functional proteins within artificial membranes. CRISPR technology is often used for precise editing of the genetic code to ensure that only desired functions are performed. The resulting synthetic cells can be designed to respond to specific stimuli, such as pH changes, enzyme activity, or molecular signals, allowing them to perform targeted tasks in a controlled manner.

5Materials Used
  • DNA
  • Proteins
  • Artificial membranes
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing synthetic cells involves several steps, including designing the genetic material, synthesizing DNA sequences, assembling these into minimal genomes or encapsulating them within artificial membranes. These processes require highly specialized equipment such as PCR machines, sequencers, and cell culture facilities.

7Build Process

The build process of synthetic cells starts with computational design using software tools like CRISPR-Cas9 for gene editing. This is followed by laboratory synthesis of the genetic material, which is then integrated into a host cell or encapsulated within an artificial structure. Quality control measures are essential to ensure that only functional and safe cells are produced.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking and high-temperature sterilization processes.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Chassis — the container and background machinery that everything else sits in — either a stripped-down living cell or an artificial vesicle. Key parts: lipid bilayer membrane, minimal genome or cell-free extract, energy and metabolism module.
  • Genetic program — the DNA instructions that make the cell sense and respond. Key parts: synthetic genes, promoters and logic gates, guide RNAs for CRISPR targeting.
  • Sensing and targeting module — decides where the cell acts — a tumour, an inflamed tissue, a specific organ. Key parts: surface receptors, targeting ligands or antibodies, signal-processing circuit.
  • Payload and output — what the cell actually delivers or does. Key parts: therapeutic protein or RNA, small-molecule drug, secretory pathway.
  • Containment and safety — keeps an engineered cell from surviving or spreading where it should not. Key parts: kill switch, auxotrophy (dependence on a supplied nutrient), non-replicating design.
  • Manufacturing and quality control — makes the construct reproducibly and proves it is sterile, stable and correctly made. Key parts: bioreactor or microfluidic assembly, purification, potency and sterility assays.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the chassis. Top-down route: take a bacterium and delete genes until only the minimum for life remains (as with JCVI-syn3.0 in 2016). Bottom-up route: start with an empty lipid vesicle and add only what you choose.
  2. Design the circuit. Sequence a DNA program on a computer: promoters, ribosome binding sites and genes arranged as switches or logic gates that fire only under chosen conditions.
  3. Synthesise and assemble the DNA. Short synthesised fragments are joined into a full construct and verified by sequencing.
  4. Edit or load the genome. CRISPR-Cas9 systems — described in 2012 — cut the target genome so the new program can be inserted; alternatively the DNA is simply mixed into a cell-free extract.
  5. Form the membrane. For bottom-up cells, lipids are forced into water-in-oil droplets or passed through microfluidic junctions to make uniform vesicles.
  6. Encapsulate the machinery. The DNA program plus the transcription, translation and energy machinery is sealed inside the vesicle, so the cell can make proteins on its own.
  7. Fit targeting and safety. Receptors or antibodies are displayed on the surface; a kill switch or nutrient dependence is added so the cell cannot escape.
  8. Test and characterise. Function is measured in culture, then in animal models; potency, off-target effects and leakage are quantified.
  9. Scale and quality control. For therapeutic use, batches are grown or assembled under good-manufacturing conditions; for patient-derived cell therapies, the patient's own cells are engineered and returned.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS DNA synthesis and assembly — writing custom genetic programs depends on cheap, accurate synthesis of long DNA.
  • EXISTS CRISPR genome editing — gives precise control over where a program lands in a living genome.
  • PARTIAL Cell-free protein expression — lets a vesicle with no metabolism of its own still make proteins for hours; yields are still low.
  • PARTIAL Minimal-genome and protocell biology — defines how few parts a cell really needs, the core question for building one from scratch.
  • PARTIAL Biocontainment and regulation — an engineered cell that replicates inside a patient needs provable safeguards and a regulatory path.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: engineered living cell therapies in the clinic — CAR-T cells against blood cancers have been approved since 2017 — proof that reprogrammed cells can be safe medicines.
  2. Next: non-replicating synthetic cells and cell-free systems used for targeted drug delivery and diagnostics — dosing that is easier to control than a living, dividing cell.
  3. Next: synthetic cells that sense disease markers inside the body and release a payload on cue — autonomous, localised therapy instead of whole-body drugs.
  4. Longer term: fully bottom-up synthetic cells that grow, divide and make their own energy — programmable biology built from defined parts, and a clearer answer to what life minimally requires.
