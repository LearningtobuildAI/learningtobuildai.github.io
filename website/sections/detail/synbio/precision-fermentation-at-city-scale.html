4How It Works

Advanced biotechnology is employed to optimize fermentation processes. This involves genetically modifying microorganisms such as yeast or bacteria to efficiently produce desired bio-products like proteins, enzymes, or chemicals. The process occurs in large-scale bioreactors located in city facilities, enabling continuous and controlled production under optimal conditions.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves setting up bioreactor systems within urban areas. These facilities are equipped with advanced control systems to monitor and maintain optimal conditions for fermentation. Raw materials such as glucose, amino acids, and other nutrients are continuously supplied to the microorganisms in these reactors.

7Build Process

Construction of city-scale precision fermentation facilities requires significant investment in infrastructure, including bioreactor units, filtration systems, and waste management solutions. The build process also involves rigorous testing and validation of equipment and processes.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Host strain — the engineered microbe that produces the target protein. Key parts: yeast, bacteria or fungal cells, synthetic DNA constructs, gene editing tools, selection markers.
  • Upstream seed train — grows a small culture up to the volume needed to inoculate the production vessel. Key parts: cryovials, shake flasks, seed bioreactors, sterile transfer lines.
  • Production bioreactor — the main vessel where fermentation happens under controlled conditions. Key parts: stainless-steel or single-use bioreactor, agitator, sparger for air or oxygen, pH and temperature probes, control system.
  • Media and feed system — supplies nutrients and carbon source to the culture. Key parts: sugar or glycerol feedstock, nitrogen source, vitamins and trace elements, feed pumps, sterilisation filters.
  • Downstream processing — separates and purifies the protein from the fermentation broth. Key parts: centrifuge or filtration, chromatography columns, ultrafiltration/diafiltration, spray dryer or freeze dryer.
  • Utilities and waste — provides clean steam, water, air and treats waste streams. Key parts: clean-in-place (CIP) and sterilise-in-place (SIP) systems, chillers, air compressors, wastewater treatment.
  • Quality control — ensures product safety, purity and consistency. Key parts: HPLC, mass spectrometry, microbial testing, documentation systems.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Engineer the production strain. Choose a safe host organism, insert genes for the target protein, and optimise expression levels.
  2. Develop the lab-scale process. Test in shake flasks and small bioreactors to find the best temperature, pH, and feed strategy for growth and product yield.
  3. Scale up the seed train. Stepwise increase volume from a frozen vial to a seed bioreactor, maintaining strict sterility at every transfer.
  4. Design and build the production bioreactor. Size the vessel for the target output, and install agitation, aeration, sensors, and automated control systems.
  5. Prepare and sterilise media. Mix carbon and nitrogen sources with micronutrients, sterilise by heat or filtration, and transfer aseptically into the bioreactor.
  6. Run the fermentation. Inoculate the vessel, hold conditions steady, feed nutrients on a schedule, and monitor cell growth and product formation.
  7. Harvest and purify. Separate cells from the broth, then use filtration and chromatography to isolate and concentrate the target protein.
  8. Formulate and package. Stabilise the protein with food-grade or pharmaceutical excipients and package it for distribution.
  9. Clean and validate. Run clean-in-place and sterilise-in-place cycles, validate cleaning between batches, and release product through quality control.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Recombinant DNA and gene editing — needed to create the production strain that makes the desired protein.
  • EXISTS Industrial-scale fermentation — the engineering is proven for antibiotics and enzymes, but capacity must expand for food proteins.
  • PARTIAL Low-cost, sustainable feedstock — sugar is cheap but competes with food; waste-derived feedstocks are still being developed.
  • PARTIAL Affordable downstream purification — chromatography is expensive; new methods are needed to make commodity proteins cost-competitive.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory pathways for novel foods — approvals exist in some countries but are not yet harmonised globally.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: precision fermentation makes high-value proteins such as recombinant insulin and chymosin at commercial scale — proof of the technology and established manufacturing know-how.
  2. Next 5–10 years: production costs for commodity proteins (dairy, egg) drop through cheaper media and continuous processing — wider adoption in everyday foods.
  3. Next 10–20 years: city-scale biorefineries use local waste streams and renewable energy as feedstock and power — circular, resilient protein supply for urban populations.
  4. Longer term: integration with agriculture and waste systems replaces a significant share of animal protein — reduced land and water use, and lower emissions from protein production.

18Glossary
Precision Fermentation
A biotechnological process where microorganisms are engineered to produce specific bio-products in controlled, large-scale bioreactors.
Bioreactor
A vessel used for the cultivation of biological organisms under controlled conditions. In precision fermentation, it is used to grow genetically modified microorganisms for the production of desired bio-products.
Genetic Engineering
The direct manipulation of an organism's genes using biotechnology. In precision fermentation, this involves altering the genetic makeup of microorganisms to enhance their ability to produce specific bio-products.
