PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Plastic-eating microbes are engineered microorganisms, typically bacteria or fungi, that produce and secrete PETase (polyethylene terephthalate) enzymes to break down plastic waste into its constituent monomers.

2Problem It Solves

The rapid accumulation of plastic waste in landfills and oceans poses significant environmental challenges. Plastic-eating microbes offer an innovative solution for breaking down plastics more efficiently than traditional methods.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Through metabolic engineering, these organisms are modified to enhance the production of PETase enzymes. When exposed to plastic waste, the microbes secrete these enzymes directly onto the plastic surface, initiating a chemical reaction that degrades the polymer chains into smaller molecules (monomers).

5Materials Used
  • PETase enzyme
  • microorganisms (bacteria/fungi)
  • bioreactors
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves genetic engineering to introduce or enhance the PETase gene within microorganisms, followed by cultivation under controlled conditions to produce large quantities of these engineered organisms.

7Build Process

Genetic modification is performed using CRISPR-Cas9 or other genome editing tools. The modified microbes are then cultured in bioreactors with appropriate nutrients and growth media to ensure optimal enzyme production.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Microbial chassis — the host organism that produces and secretes the enzymes. Key parts: engineered bacterium or yeast, genetic control elements, containment features.
  • Enzyme payload — breaks the ester bonds in PET plastic. Key parts: PETase, MHETase, accessory cutinases.
  • Secretion system — exports enzymes out of the cell to act on plastic surfaces. Key parts: signal peptides, secretory pathway, membrane transporters.
  • Bioreactor — provides controlled conditions for microbial growth and enzyme activity. Key parts: stirred-tank reactor, temperature and pH control, feedstock input.
  • Feedstock preparation — turns plastic waste into a form microbes can access. Key parts: shredder, washer, pretreatment unit.
  • Downstream recovery — separates and purifies the monomers released from plastic. Key parts: filtration, crystallization, distillation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select a host microbe. Choose a robust, well-understood organism such as a bacterium or yeast that can grow on industrial feedstocks and is safe to contain.
  2. Insert enzyme genes. Add genes for PETase and MHETase, often improved by directed evolution for higher activity and stability.
  3. Engineer secretion. Attach signal peptides so the enzymes are exported outside the cell, where they can reach the plastic.
  4. Test in the lab. Grow the engineered microbe with small pieces of PET film and measure how quickly the plastic breaks down into monomers.
  5. Scale up to pilot bioreactors. Run the process in larger stirred tanks with shredded plastic waste, tuning temperature, pH and mixing.
  6. Recover monomers. Filter out microbes and undigested plastic, then purify terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol by crystallization and distillation.
  7. Repolymerize. Use the recovered monomers to make new PET or other chemicals, closing the loop.
  8. Validate at scale. Demonstrate the process on real mixed plastic waste and compare cost and carbon footprint with mechanical recycling.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS PETase enzymes — naturally occurring enzymes that break PET were first described in 2016.
  • EXISTS Metabolic engineering tools — standard methods for editing microbial genomes and controlling gene expression.
  • PARTIAL Enzyme secretion systems — exporting large amounts of active enzyme outside the cell is demonstrated in labs but not yet at industrial flux.
  • PARTIAL Bioreactor scale-up for solid substrates — getting microbes and enzymes to act efficiently on solid plastic surfaces in large tanks is difficult.
  • EXISTS Plastic sorting and pretreatment — mechanical recycling already shreds and washes plastic waste, providing a feedstock.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory framework for engineered microbes — contained use is regulated, but rules for waste-processing facilities are still developing.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: lab-scale demonstrations of engineered microbes degrading PET — proof that secretion and breakdown can work together.
  2. Next: pilot bioreactors processing sorted PET waste — realistic rates and process economics.
  3. Next: integration with recycling plants to produce virgin-quality monomers — a complement to mechanical recycling for colored or mixed plastics.
  4. Longer term: enzymes for other plastics such as polyurethane and polyethylene — treatment of a wider range of waste streams.
