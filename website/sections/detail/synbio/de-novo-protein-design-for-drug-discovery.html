PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

De novo protein design is a computational approach that involves designing entirely artificial proteins from scratch using computer algorithms. These proteins are created with specific functional properties, such as binding to certain molecules or catalyzing particular reactions.

Category
Biotechnology
Best use
New drug development, personalized medicine
Stage
FAR
2Problem It Solves

Traditional protein engineering relies on modifying existing proteins, which can be limited by evolutionary constraints and may not result in optimal therapeutic candidates. De novo design allows for the creation of entirely new proteins with tailored properties that are not constrained by natural evolution.

3Lifecycle / Journey Stage
early commercial
PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The process begins by defining the desired function of the protein and then using computational methods to predict and design the amino acid sequence that will best achieve this function. Once a potential sequence is identified, it is synthesized in the laboratory and tested for its ability to fold into the correct structure and perform the intended function.

5Materials Used
  • DNA
  • Proteins
  • Bacteria
  • Yeast
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves synthesizing the de novo designed DNA sequence using automated DNA synthesis techniques, followed by expressing the protein in host cells such as bacteria or yeast. The expressed protein is then purified and characterized to ensure it meets the desired functional criteria.

7Build Process

De novo protein design requires advanced computational tools for predicting protein structure and function, as well as experimental validation through molecular biology techniques like gene synthesis, protein expression, and purification.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Target and epitope selection — choose the disease-relevant protein surface the new protein must bind. Key parts: disease target data, structural models, epitope mapping.
  • Computational design engine — invents new protein backbones and sequences that fold and bind as intended. Key parts: generative backbone models, sequence design algorithms, structure prediction, energy-based scoring.
  • High-throughput testing — makes and measures thousands of designs to find the few that work. Key parts: gene synthesis, cell-free expression, display libraries, binding assays.
  • Protein production — manufactures the chosen protein in pure, stable form. Key parts: expression hosts (bacteria, mammalian cells), purification, formulation.
  • Delivery and pharmacology — keeps the protein active in the body long enough to work. Key parts: half-life extension, targeting moieties, injection devices.
  • Clinical development — proves the designed protein is safe and effective in patients. Key parts: preclinical models, clinical trials, regulatory filings.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a target. Identify a protein on a cancer cell or immune cell whose activity drives disease; map the exact surface to bind.
  2. Generate backbones. Run generative models to propose thousands of new protein folds that could present the desired binding surface.
  3. Design sequences. For each backbone, compute amino-acid sequences predicted to fold into that shape and stick to the target.
  4. Filter in silico. Use structure prediction and physics-based scoring to discard unstable or poorly binding designs.
  5. Synthesize and express. Order synthetic genes, express the proteins in bacteria or cell-free systems, and purify small amounts.
  6. Test binding and function. Measure binding affinity and biological activity in high-throughput assays; keep the best.
  7. Optimize. Iterate design and testing to improve affinity, stability and specificity.
  8. Test in animals. Assess efficacy, dosing, immune response and toxicity in animal models.
  9. Manufacture and trial. Scale up production under pharmaceutical standards and begin human clinical trials.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Accurate protein structure prediction — tools such as AlphaFold predict folded shapes well enough to guide design.
  • PARTIAL Generative models for protein backbones — diffusion-based methods are recent and still being validated for drug-like proteins.
  • EXISTS High-throughput DNA synthesis and screening — makes it possible to test thousands of designs quickly.
  • PARTIAL Protein delivery and half-life extension — many designed proteins are cleared from the body too quickly to be useful drugs.
  • NOT YET Immunogenicity prediction — the immune system may attack a designed protein that looks foreign.
  • EXISTS Clinical trial and regulatory pathway — there is a standard route for protein therapeutics once a candidate is chosen.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: designed binders and mimics that work in the lab — new research tools and diagnostics.
  2. Next: first de novo designed proteins enter clinical trials for cancer or autoimmune disease — proof that the approach can be safe in humans.
  3. Next: designed proteins with multiple functions (e.g., bind two targets at once) — treatments for complex diseases that single antibodies cannot reach.
  4. Longer term: personalized proteins designed for an individual patient's tumour — on-demand, truly personalized medicine.
