4How It Works

Scientists use computational tools and molecular biology techniques to design DNA sequences that code for specific functions. These are then synthesized and integrated into living organisms to create novel biological systems with desired traits.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves the assembly of designed DNA sequences using automated synthesis machines or natural organisms as hosts for gene expression.

7Build Process

The process starts with computational design, followed by DNA synthesis, transformation into host cells, and optimization through iterative cycles of testing and refinement.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Design and software layer — turns a desired function into a DNA sequence. Key parts: sequence and part libraries, computer-aided design tools, machine-learning models for protein and pathway design.
  • DNA synthesis and assembly — makes the designed sequence as physical DNA. Key parts: oligonucleotide synthesisers, assembly enzymes, error-correction and sequencing.
  • Genome editing tools — writes the change into a living cell. Key parts: CRISPR nucleases, base and prime editors, site-specific recombinases, delivery vectors.
  • Chassis organism — the host cell that does the work. Key parts: bacteria such as E. coli, yeast, mammalian cell lines, minimal or recoded genomes.
  • Pathway and circuit engineering — wires enzymes, promoters and regulators into a working production line. Key parts: enzymes, promoters and ribosome-binding sites, feedback and logic circuits, compartmentalisation.
  • Bioprocess and scale-up — grows the engineered cells in large volumes and recovers the product. Key parts: seed and production bioreactors, sterile aeration and mixing, centrifugation and filtration, chromatography and purification.
  • Containment and biosafety — keeps engineered organisms where they belong. Key parts: auxotrophy and kill switches, physical containment, waste inactivation, regulatory documentation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Specify the function. Define the target molecule, enzyme or behaviour, and the purity and cost it must meet.
  2. Design the DNA. Software chooses codon usage, promoters and gene order; protein-design models increasingly propose enzyme sequences that do not exist in nature.
  3. Synthesise the DNA. Chemical synthesis builds short strands, which are assembled into genes, error-corrected and sequence-verified.
  4. Build the strain. The construct is inserted into the chassis on a plasmid or into the chromosome, and the edit is confirmed.
  5. Run design-build-test-learn cycles. Hundreds to thousands of variants are screened in parallel using robotic liquid handling and cell sorting to find the best performers.
  6. Test in a bioreactor. Winners move to controlled vessels of a few litres, then hundreds of litres, where oxygen, temperature, pH and feed are managed.
  7. Scale to production. Fed-batch or continuous fermentation in vessels of tens to hundreds of cubic metres; the hard part is keeping yield high as the tank gets bigger.
  8. Recover and purify. Cells are separated from broth, and the product is concentrated and purified by filtration, chromatography or crystallisation.
  9. Contain and release. The production organism is inactivated, waste is treated, and the process is documented for regulators before the product is sold.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Cheap, accurate DNA synthesis — cost per base has fallen enormously, but long, error-free constructs are still slow and expensive.
  • EXISTS Programmable genome editing — CRISPR-based tools let engineers change specific genes in many organisms reliably.
  • PARTIAL Predictive protein and pathway models — structure prediction is now strong, but designing a novel enzyme or predicting metabolic flux is still unreliable.
  • EXISTS Industrial fermentation capacity — brewing, antibiotics and amino-acid plants already run at large scale, giving a base to build on.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory and containment frameworks — rules differ widely between countries and lag behind engineered organisms released outside a tank.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: engineered enzymes, medicines and precision-fermentation proteins made in yeast or bacteria — a commercial base in detergents, food ingredients and pharmaceuticals.
  2. Next: AI-designed enzymes and cell-free synthesis routes — faster design of reactions that no living organism performs today.
  3. Next: engineered cells as therapies and cultivated animal products at scale — medicine and food made biologically rather than chemically or from livestock.
  4. Longer term: programmable cells for materials, agriculture and sensing — living systems that make novel materials, fix nitrogen or detect pollutants — provided containment and public acceptance hold.

host cells
Living organisms used as platforms to express or produce desired products from synthetic genes.
