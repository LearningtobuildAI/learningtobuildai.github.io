PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Available today

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Motion sensor — turns movement across a surface into digital X/Y motion. Key parts: light source (LED or infrared laser), lens, image sensor array, digital signal processor.
  • Buttons — register clicks and holds. Key parts: main microswitches, side buttons, shell-mounted actuators.
  • Scroll wheel — scrolls vertically and often clicks sideways. Key parts: wheel, optical or mechanical encoder with detents, centre switch.
  • Control electronics — converts sensor and button states into standard reports the computer understands. Key parts: microcontroller, firmware, USB HID interface, memory for profiles.
  • Connectivity and power — carries data and supplies energy. Key parts: USB cable or dongle, 2.4 GHz radio or Bluetooth chip, battery (wireless models), charging contacts or coil.
  • Housing and glide — defines comfort, weight and how smoothly it slides. Key parts: moulded plastic shell, PTFE glide feet, cable strain relief.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the shape. The shell is modelled in CAD from hand scans and ergonomic studies, then 3D-printed for test fits.
  2. Design the circuit board. A small PCB is laid out to carry the sensor, microcontroller, switches and radio, keeping the sensor close to the underside window.
  3. Fabricate the board. Surface-mount components are placed and reflow-soldered; the optical sensor is mounted to tight positional tolerance.
  4. Mould the parts. Shell halves, buttons and the wheel are injection-moulded in ABS or polycarbonate, then painted or textured.
  5. Write the firmware. Firmware polls the sensor thousands of times per second, filters noise, scales motion to counts per inch, and reports over USB HID.
  6. Assemble. Switches, wheel, sensor and battery are fitted into the lower shell, the top shell snaps or screws on, and glide feet are applied.
  7. Calibrate and test. Tracking is checked on varied surfaces, click force and lifespan are measured, and wireless latency and battery life are verified.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Optical image sensors — the sensor must photograph the surface fast enough to measure tiny movements.
  • EXISTS Low-power microcontrollers and radio chips — wireless mice must run for months on a small battery.
  • EXISTS USB HID standard — a standard device class means no driver install — the mouse works on any modern computer.
  • EXISTS Injection moulding tooling — shells need cheap, precise mass production with good surface finish.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: high polling rates (1,000 Hz and well beyond) and lightweight shells with optical switches — lower click latency and longer switch life for gaming and precision work.
  2. Next: wireless links matching wired latency at longer battery life — wireless as the default even for competitive use.
  3. Next: onboard haptics and per-user shape or weight adjustment — feedback and fit that suit individual hands and tasks.
  4. Longer term: tighter integration with headsets and spatial interfaces — pointer control inside mixed-reality workspaces.
