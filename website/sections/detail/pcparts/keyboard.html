4How It Works

When keys are pressed, switches or sensors within the keyboard send electrical signals to the computer's motherboard, which then translates these signals into corresponding characters or commands on the screen.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Key switches — the moving element under each key that registers a press. Key parts: membrane dome or scissor mechanism (laptops and cheap boards), mechanical switch with spring, stem and metal contacts, optical switch using an infrared beam, Hall-effect or inductive switch sensing a magnet.
  • Keycaps — the surfaces your fingers touch, labelled with characters. Key parts: moulded ABS or PBT plastic cap, double-shot or dye-sublimated legends, stem mount.
  • Plate and case — hold the switches in a rigid grid that stops flex and damps sound. Key parts: metal or plastic plate, case top and bottom, feet or tilt mechanism.
  • Circuit board and matrix — wires hundreds of keys into a small number of signal lines. Key parts: printed circuit board, diodes to stop ghosting, hot-swap sockets (some boards).
  • Controller and firmware — scans the matrix, debounces signals and reports key events. Key parts: microcontroller, firmware (open-source layouts such as QMK or vendor equivalents), onboard memory for custom layouts.
  • Interface and power — carries data to the host. Key parts: USB or USB-C cable, Bluetooth or 2.4 GHz radio and battery (wireless models).
  • Backlighting — illuminates legends for use in the dark. Key parts: per-key LEDs or an edge-lit light guide, diffuser.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the layout and matrix. Decide the key arrangement, then assign each key a row and column so a modest number of pins can scan them all.
  2. Lay out the circuit board. Traces, diode positions and switch footprints are drawn, then boards are etched and assembled with surface-mount diodes and a microcontroller.
  3. Make the switches. Contacts, springs and stems are stamped and moulded, then snapped together in clean conditions so grit cannot jam them.
  4. Mould the keycaps. Plastic is injected into polished moulds; double-shot moulding or dye sublimation applies legends that will not rub off.
  5. Fabricate plate and case. Metal plate is stamped or laser-cut with switch cut-outs; the case is injection-moulded or machined.
  6. Assemble. Switches are pressed into the plate and soldered or socketed to the board, which is screwed into the case and topped with keycaps.
  7. Flash firmware. Matrix mapping, debounce timing, keymap and lighting behaviour are compiled and loaded.
  8. Test. Automated rigs press every key and check for missed or doubled presses; a human types a sample before packing.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS USB HID standard — lets any keyboard report keystrokes to any computer without a custom driver.
  • EXISTS Low-cost microcontrollers — each keyboard needs a chip to scan the matrix and speak USB or Bluetooth.
  • EXISTS Precision plastic injection moulding — switch housings and keycaps must fit within fractions of a millimetre.
  • EXISTS Contact or magnetic-sensing switch design — determines feel, lifespan and whether the press is binary or analogue.
  • EXISTS Printed circuit board fabrication — the matrix and its diodes live on a custom board, often made in small batches.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: mechanical and membrane keyboards with per-key lighting and 1000 Hz USB polling — reliable, cheap text input for nearly every computer.
  2. Next: magnetic and optical switches with adjustable actuation and rapid-trigger modes — faster, more precise control in games and lower latency typing.
  3. Next: analogue key sensing reported to software as continuous values — pressure-sensitive input in games, music and accessibility tools.
  4. Longer term: adaptive key surfaces and haptic feedback instead of fixed rows of keys — layouts that reconfigure for the task, language or user.
