PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Neuro-Linked Telepresence is an advanced technology that enables a user's brain signals to control a remote robotic body in real-time, allowing for full sensory and haptic feedback.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The system bypasses peripheral nerves by directly interfacing with the motor cortex of the brain. It uses non-invasive or minimally invasive electrodes to capture neural signals, which are then processed and translated into commands for the robotic body. The robot provides haptic and sensory feedback to the user's brain via similar means.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves creating high-precision electrodes, signal processing devices, and robotic bodies. The process requires advanced materials science and microfabrication techniques.

7Build Process

The build process includes designing the neural interface hardware, calibrating it to individual users, integrating with the robotic body, and testing the system for reliability and safety.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. Power consumption for both brain-computer interfaces and robotic bodies must be optimized for practical use.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Neural interface — records motor commands from the brain and, for feedback, stimulates sensory areas. Key parts: penetrating microelectrode arrays (e.g. Utah array), electrocorticography (ECoG) grids, non-invasive EEG caps (lower fidelity), connectors and hermetic feedthroughs.
  • Signal processing and decoding — turns raw neural activity into real-time motor commands. Key parts: low-noise amplifiers, spike sorting and feature extraction, machine-learning decoders (e.g. Kalman filters, neural networks).
  • Robot platform — the remote body that acts on the user's intent. Key parts: robotic arms and hands, mobile base or drone, cameras and depth sensors, onboard compute.
  • Sensory feedback system — delivers touch, pressure and limb position back to the user. Key parts: cortical or peripheral nerve stimulation electrodes, encoding algorithms that map sensor data to stimulation patterns, haptic sensors on the robot.
  • Communication link — carries commands to the robot and feedback to the user with minimal delay. Key parts: wireless transmitters (e.g. UWB, Wi-Fi), secure low-latency network, redundant channels.
  • Implant electronics and power — keeps the neural interface running safely inside the body. Key parts: inductive wireless power coils, batteries or supercapacitors, hermetic titanium or ceramic packaging.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design a long-lived electrode array. Engineers fabricate microelectrodes with many channels, often silicon or flexible polymer, aiming for years of stable recording without scarring.
  2. Implant the interface. A neurosurgeon places the array into the motor cortex (and sometimes sensory cortex) or onto the brain surface, connecting it to a sealed electronics package in the skull.
  3. Record and decode neural signals. The system amplifies and digitises brain activity, then trains a decoder so that imagined movements map to robot commands.
  4. Integrate with the robot. The decoder output is streamed to the robot's controller, which executes arm, hand or mobility movements in real time.
  5. Implement artificial sensation. Robot sensors (touch, force, joint angle) feed algorithms that generate stimulation patterns delivered to the user's sensory cortex or peripheral nerves.
  6. Close the control loop. The user sees, feels and adjusts the robot's actions, refining both the decoder and the feedback encoder through practice.
  7. Test in clinical trials. Participants with paralysis use the system in supervised tasks to measure safety, reliability and benefit over months or years.
  8. Scale manufacturing and safety. Long-term biocompatibility, wireless updates, cybersecurity and fail-safe shutdowns are refined before any commercial release.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-channel-count, long-lasting neural electrodes — current arrays can record dozens to hundreds of neurons for a few years, but signal loss and immune rejection remain unsolved.
  • EXISTS Real-time neural decoding — decoders already translate motor cortex activity into cursor or robotic arm movements in lab and clinical settings.
  • PARTIAL Artificial sensory feedback — researchers have evoked simple touch and pressure sensations via brain or nerve stimulation, but not yet rich, naturalistic haptics.
  • PARTIAL Wireless power and data for implants — inductive links and infrared or radio telemetry work in the lab, but need to be reliable and safe for decades inside the body.
  • PARTIAL Biocompatible, hermetic packaging — sealing electronics against body fluids for many years is difficult; leaks cause failure and risk to the user.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: lab and early clinical demos of paralysed people controlling robotic arms or cursors with limited or no feedback — proof that direct neural control is possible and can improve quality of life.
  2. Next: two-way interfaces in clinical trials for medical use, adding simple touch feedback — more dexterous and intuitive control for people with severe motor impairment.
  3. Next: commercial medical devices for daily use at home — a regulatory and manufacturing base for implanted neurotechnology.
  4. Longer term: high-fidelity telepresence with full-body sensation for able-bodied users — remote work in hazardous environments, telemedicine and new forms of embodied communication — if safety, ethics and cost are addressed.

18Glossary
neural interface
A device that connects the human nervous system with an external electronic or computational system.
motor cortex
The region of the cerebral cortex responsible for controlling voluntary movements.
haptic feedback
Sensory information that provides tactile sensations, such as touch and force, to enhance interaction with a system or environment.
