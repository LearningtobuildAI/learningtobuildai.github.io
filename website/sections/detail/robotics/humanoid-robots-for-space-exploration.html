PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Legs and locomotion — move and stay upright where gravity is a third of Earth's, or effectively zero. Key parts: articulated leg joints, wide, pressure-tolerant feet, balance controller, tether or anchorage points for microgravity.
  • Arms and hands — use the same tools and controls designed for astronauts. Key parts: shoulder, elbow and wrist joints, multi-fingered hands with force sensing, tool and connector interfaces.
  • Head and perception — build a 3D picture of an unfamiliar, poorly mapped environment. Key parts: stereo and depth cameras, LiDAR, inertial measurement unit, pan-tilt neck.
  • Avionics and autonomy — decide what to do when nobody can steer it in real time. Key parts: radiation-tolerant processors, local task-planning software, simulation-trained motion control, data recorder.
  • Power and thermal control — survive vacuum, hard sunlight and deep cold, and keep working. Key parts: battery pack, radiators and heaters, insulation, solar charging when parked.
  • Shielding and mechanisms — resist abrasive dust, radiation and the risk of vacuum welding. Key parts: sealed joints, space-rated lubricants, dust covers, hardened electronics.
  • Communications and teleoperation — let controllers on Earth supervise, despite long signal delays. Key parts: radio or relay link, supervisory control station, delayed-command software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the jobs. Start from tasks astronauts actually do — inspection, sample handling, carrying tools, plugging connectors — and define the reach, strength and precision needed.
  2. Design the kinematics. Size the limbs so the robot can reach human workspaces and grip human-rated tools; allow for the fact that legs must both walk in gravity and anchor in free fall.
  3. Fabricate the frame. Machine limbs from lightweight aluminium or titanium alloys and composites, avoiding thin sections that crack under launch vibration.
  4. Build the actuators. Fit high-torque electric motors with gear reducers or series-elastic joints, using dry or vacuum-compatible lubricants so parts cannot seize or cold-weld in vacuum.
  5. Add sensing and compute. Install cameras, LiDAR and joint torque sensors, cabled to radiation-tolerant computers rather than ordinary consumer chips.
  6. Write the control software. Train walking and manipulation policies in physics simulation, then adapt them to the real robot, and layer in autonomy so it can act during multi-minute communication gaps.
  7. Test in relevant conditions. Thermal-vacuum chambers for temperature and vacuum, vibration tables for launch loads, neutral-buoyancy pools and parabolic flights for reduced-gravity movement, sand and dust for Mars-like surfaces.
  8. Deploy and supervise. Launch it to a station or surface, begin with tightly supervised tasks, and widen its autonomy only as reliability is proven.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Dynamic legged locomotion in low and zero gravity — hopping and floating gaits have been studied in parabolic flights and on research quadrupeds, but nothing walks reliably on the Moon or Mars yet.
  • PARTIAL Dexterous robotic hands — multi-fingered hands and teleoperated grasping are demonstrated in the lab and on the ISS, but ruggedness over years of dust and thermal cycling is unproven.
  • PARTIAL Radiation-tolerant high-performance computing — space-hardened processors lag far behind the chips that modern vision and AI software assume, limiting onboard intelligence.
  • NOT YET Autonomy under long communication delays — a Mars robot faces delays of roughly 4–24 minutes each way, so it must plan and recover from errors without a human in the loop.
  • EXISTS Space-rated actuators, seals and lubricants — these exist for rovers and spacecraft mechanisms, but humanoid joints demand far more cycles and higher loads.
  • EXISTS Reduced-gravity power and thermal management — batteries, heaters and radiators are mature from rover and station experience.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: humanoids on space stations and in research labs doing supervised or teleoperated chores — real operating data on how robots cope with vacuum, vibration and crewed workspaces.
  2. Next: robots performing routine exterior inspection and simple maintenance without a spacewalk — less crew time and risk spent outside habitats and vehicles.
  3. Next: supervised humanoids working inside habitats and on planetary surfaces ahead of crews — pre-positioning equipment and checking habitats before people arrive.
  4. Longer term: teams of robots building and maintaining a surface base largely on their own — settlements that can grow without every task depending on a suited human.
