PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Soft continuum body — flexible shaft that bends along winding vessels without damaging walls. Key parts: medical-grade silicone or thermoplastic elastomer, reinforcing fibres, segmented stiffness zones.
  • Actuation — makes the body bend and steer; two main routes are fluidic and electro-active. Key parts: hydraulic or pneumatic micro-channels, electro-active polymer (EAP) layers, compliant electrodes, drive tether.
  • Sensing and shape estimation — tells the surgeon where the tip is and what force it applies. Key parts: fiber Bragg grating strain sensors, force/torque sensors, tip camera or ultrasound.
  • Control and teleoperation — translates surgeon input into safe, precise motion. Key parts: master console, kinematic model, real-time controller, safety limits.
  • Imaging integration — lets the robot be seen inside the body. Key parts: fluoroscopy or MRI compatibility, image registration software.
  • Therapeutic tip — does the actual work — grasping, cutting, ablating or delivering therapy. Key parts: micro-grippers, laser fibres, ablation electrodes, drug delivery channels.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the continuum structure. Define bending segments, stiffness and channel layout using models of the target vessels.
  2. Fabricate the soft body. Cast medical-grade elastomer in moulds, or 3D-print it, embedding channels or EAP layers as it forms.
  3. Build the actuators. For fluidic versions, create sealed micro-channels; for EAP versions, deposit compliant electrodes on dielectric elastomer or ionic polymer films.
  4. Integrate sensors. Embed fiber Bragg gratings or strain gauges along the body and place a force sensor at the tip.
  5. Add the therapeutic tip. Mount micro-tools, cameras, laser fibres or electrodes on the distal end.
  6. Connect drive and control. Link the body to pumps and valves, or high-voltage amplifiers, and write control software with safety limits.
  7. Test in phantoms and animals. Navigate silicone vessel phantoms and then animal models to measure reachability, force and tissue damage.
  8. Prepare for clinical trials. Sterilise, package and run the regulatory safety studies needed for first-in-human use.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Biocompatible soft materials — medical-grade silicones and elastomers are already used in catheters and implants.
  • PARTIAL Micro-fabrication of soft actuators — moulding and 3D printing can make channels and EAP layers, but repeatability and miniaturisation remain difficult.
  • PARTIAL Shape and force sensing in flexible bodies — fiber Bragg grating sensors work in labs, but integrating many into a millimetre-scale catheter is not routine.
  • PARTIAL Real-time control of continuum robots — kinematic models exist, but controlling a soft body under unpredictable tissue contact is still hard.
  • NOT YET Miniaturised high-voltage drivers for EAPs — many EAPs need hundreds to thousands of volts, which is unsafe and bulky for a clinical device.
  • PARTIAL Medical imaging compatibility — MRI-safe and fluoroscopy-visible soft robots are demonstrated but not standard.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: research prototypes and early clinical trials of steerable soft catheters — proof that soft robots can reach and treat specific anatomy safely.
  2. Next: first regulatory approvals for soft robotic tools in niche procedures — commercial use in cardiology, gastroenterology or bronchoscopy.
  3. Next: integration with real-time 3D imaging and haptic feedback — more precise, intuitive surgery in complex anatomy.
  4. Longer term: semi-autonomous or autonomous navigation inside the body — procedures in remote or emergency settings, and reduced surgeon fatigue.
