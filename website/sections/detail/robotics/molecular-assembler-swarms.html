PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Assembler unit — a single machine, a few nanometres across, that holds and positions reactive chemical groups so they bond where you want. Key parts: reactive tool tip, rigid molecular frame (e.g. a diamondoid or polymer scaffold), substrate binding site.
  • Positioning drive — moves the tool with sub-nanometre precision and applies the force needed to make or break a bond. Key parts: molecular motor, track or hinge, force-limited linkage.
  • Power supply — delivers energy to each unit locally, since a wire is impossible. Key parts: chemical fuel such as a high-energy bond, or light or an external electric field, local energy-harvesting group.
  • Signalling and coordination — implements stigmergy: units release marker molecules and sense the gradients left by others, so the swarm organises without central control. Key parts: emitter groups, receptor groups, diffusion-limited local memory.
  • Instruction and error control — tells each unit what to build and stops small mistakes compounding. Key parts: sequence-defined polymer or DNA strand as program, proofreading or rejection step, kill switch.
  • Containment — keeps the swarm where it is wanted — inside a reactor, or inside a body or a spill zone. Key parts: physical enclosure, chemically triggered disablement, immune or environmental compatibility (medical and cleanup use).
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the building block. Chemists specify a rigid molecular framework carrying exactly one reactive tool tip, small enough (roughly 1–10 nm) to work on single bonds.
  2. Synthesise the unit. Stepwise organic synthesis, or self-assembly of DNA strands into a shaped scaffold, produces identical copies in bulk — as DNA origami already does for static shapes.
  3. Add motion and fuel. A molecular motor powered by a chemical fuel, light or an electric field is attached, together with a mechanical limit so it cannot crush what it is building.
  4. Give it a voice. Emitter and receptor groups are added so each unit deposits and senses signalling molecules, letting neighbours follow and reinforce a trail rather than needing a controller.
  5. Prove one unit works. A single unit, held in place by a scanning probe or a surface, is shown placing atoms in a chosen pattern — the kind of manipulation already done with microscope tips, but automated and repeated.
  6. Run many in parallel. Arrays of thousands of identical units work side by side on a surface, since one unit working alone would be hopelessly slow.
  7. Add error correction. A proofreading step checks each bond or building block, rejecting and retrying failures so defects do not propagate through the product.
  8. Contain and test. First enclosed in a sealed vessel, then in controlled trials for a single task such as capturing a specific pollutant; replication, if used at all, is kept under outside control.
  9. Scale manufacture. Units are made by ordinary chemistry or self-assembly in bulk, not by building each one atom by atom — the only route that could ever produce trillions.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Mechanosynthesis — individual atoms have been positioned and individual reactions driven with scanning probe tips, but not at useful rates, yields or scale; the feasibility of general molecular manufacturing is still debated, notably in the 2003 exchange between Richard Smalley and K. Eric Drexler.
  • PARTIAL Synthetic molecular machines — rotary motors and switches won the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, proving molecules can do mechanical work — but no such machine yet builds arbitrary molecules.
  • EXISTS Programmable self-assembly — DNA nanotechnology builds designed nanometre shapes reliably, giving a possible scaffold and program carrier, though only for a narrow set of structures.
  • NOT YET Nanoscale coordination — stigmergy is well understood in ant colonies and used in swarm robotics, but chemical signalling between artificial units at trillion-unit density has not been demonstrated.
  • NOT YET Atomically precise power and cooling — reactions release heat in nanometre volumes; removing it without damaging the machine is unsolved.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: probe-based atom manipulation and synthetic molecular motors in laboratories — tools that can touch and move single atoms and molecules.
  2. Next: parallel arrays of tips or molecular machines performing the same reaction at thousands of sites at once — realistic throughput for atomically precise surface chemistry.
  3. Next: an enclosed demonstrator that follows a stored program and builds a simple target molecule with error checking — proof that programmed molecular construction is possible at all.
  4. Next: task-specific swarms for medicine or pollution capture, steered by external fields and local chemical trails — useful work from nanoscale agents without needing general-purpose manufacturing.
  5. Longer term: general molecular manufacturing, if replication, error rates, energy and control can all be solved — materials and medicines built to atomic specification — otherwise the concept stays a research program.
