PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Radiation-hardened electronics and sensors — keep working inside intense gamma and neutron fields. Key parts: shielded camera and lighting modules, hardened motor drives and controllers, dosimeters and temperature sensors.
  • Mobility platform — move over uneven floors, steps and debris without getting stuck. Key parts: tracked or wheeled chassis, sealed motors and gearboxes, tether or battery power.
  • Manipulators and tooling — grasp, cut, drill and sort radioactive components. Key parts: hydraulic or electric arms, quick-change tool heads, grippers, shears and saws.
  • Teleoperation and control — let a human operator work from a safe distance with enough feedback to be precise. Key parts: master arms with force feedback, control console and displays, low-latency communication link.
  • Shielding and contamination control — protect the robot's own electronics and stop radioactive dust spreading. Key parts: lead or tungsten shielding, sealed enclosures and bellows, filtered ventilation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Survey and dose-map the cell. Use existing cameras, LiDAR and dosimeters to build a 3D model and radiation map of the space the robot will enter.
  2. Choose radiation-tolerant components. Select motors, cameras and chips rated for the expected total dose, or plan to shield them; standard electronics fail within hours in high fields.
  3. Build the chassis. Fabricate a tracked or legged base from steel and aluminium, with sealed bearings and cable routing that can be decontaminated.
  4. Integrate arms and tools. Fit manipulators with quick-change couplings so the robot can switch between grippers, cutting tools and vacuum heads.
  5. Install electronics and shielding. Place control electronics inside lead or tungsten boxes, run cables through shielded conduits, and add cameras behind lead glass.
  6. Write the control software. Implement teleoperation with force feedback, plus collision avoidance and simple autonomy for repetitive cuts.
  7. Test in a mock-up. Rehearse every task in a full-size non-radioactive replica of the cell, then dose-test critical parts in a gamma facility.
  8. Deploy and operate. Lower or drive the robot into the cell, work remotely in shifts, and monitor its dose and health.
  9. Recover and decontaminate. Retrieve the robot, wash or bag it, and either reuse it after decontamination or dispose of it as radioactive waste.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Radiation-hardened electronics — semiconductor devices degrade and fail under gamma and neutron dose; hardening adds cost and reduces performance.
  • EXISTS Low-latency teleoperation — haptic feedback and precise control need round-trip delays under about 50 milliseconds; fibre or shielded cable works.
  • PARTIAL Radiation-tolerant vision — camera sensors darken and lenses brown; shielded cameras and lighting are bulky and still degrade.
  • EXISTS Remote cutting and handling tools — hydraulic shears, saws and lasers have been used in nuclear plants for decades.
  • EXISTS Simulation and digital twins — operators need to plan and train without entering the cell; physics engines model debris and collisions.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: teleoperated robots perform specific tasks inside damaged or highly radioactive cells, often custom-built for one job — lower radiation dose to human workers and access to spaces people cannot enter.
  2. Next: semi-autonomous robots that perform repetitive cutting and sorting with one operator supervising several machines — faster dismantling and lower cost per tonne.
  3. Next: modular robot systems that can be airlifted or lowered through narrow openings and decontaminate themselves — reuse of robots across sites instead of one-off builds.
  4. Longer term: fully autonomous decommissioning for standard reactor types — routine, safer and cheaper removal of old reactors.
