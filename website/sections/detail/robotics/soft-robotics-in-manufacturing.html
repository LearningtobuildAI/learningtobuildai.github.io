PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Compliant body — the part that bends, stretches or inflates instead of using rigid joints. Key parts: moulded silicone or polyurethane chambers, fibre reinforcement to steer bending, bellows or pleated sections.
  • Actuation — supplies the force without metal gears. Key parts: pneumatic supply and miniature proportional valves, tendon cables and motors, dielectric elastomer or shape-memory actuation (emerging).
  • Sensing — lets the controller know how hard it is squeezing and where the object is. Key parts: embedded strain or conductive-elastomer sensors, air-pressure sensing in pneumatic chambers, camera or depth vision, force/torque sensor at the wrist.
  • Control — soft bodies are hard to model, so control leans on feedback and learning. Key parts: hyperelastic material models, impedance or force control, teach-by-demonstration and learned grasp policies.
  • Mounting and tooling — connects the soft effector to a standard industrial arm. Key parts: wrist interface or tool changer, tubing and cabling routing, quick-swap finger cassettes.
  • Safety layer — allows close work with people. Key parts: power and force limits, rounded, pinch-free geometry, light curtains or speed-and-separation monitoring.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the compliance principle. Decide whether the part should inflate (pneumatic chambers), bend (fibre-reinforced fingers, including fin-ray-style designs that curl around an object), stiffen on demand (granular jamming), or be cable-driven through a soft core.
  2. Model and simulate. Because silicone does not behave like steel, the design is checked with hyperelastic material models and large-deformation simulation rather than simple rigid-body analysis, and the mould is designed at the same time.
  3. Make the mould. A mould is machined, or 3D-printed for small runs, sometimes as a lost-core or two-part tool that can be pulled out of undercut chambers.
  4. Cast the elastomer. Two-part platinum-cure silicone is mixed, degassed under vacuum to remove bubbles, poured or injected, and cured in an oven; multi-material parts are cast in successive pours with surface treatment or overmoulding between layers so the layers bond.
  5. Add reinforcement and sensors. Fibre wraps, fabric plies or a stiffer second elastomer are laid in to control the direction of bending, and conductive elastomer channels or flexible strain gauges are cast into the body.
  6. Assemble and plumb. Fingers are bonded to a rigid base, tubing and valves are connected, and the effector is fitted to the robot wrist.
  7. Characterise. The gripper is tested for force-versus-displacement, grip on different shapes and surfaces, cycle life, tear resistance and creep, and the results are written into the controller.
  8. Integrate the control loop. Grasp policies are tuned by demonstration or learning, with vision and pressure feedback closing the loop on a standard industrial arm.
  9. Validate in production. The cell is qualified for the real line — cycle time, contamination, washdown or cleanroom compatibility, and human-robot safety limits.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Elastomer materials and multi-material moulding — the whole approach depends on tough, fatigue-resistant silicone and polyurethane that bond reliably in layers.
  • EXISTS Miniature proportional pneumatics — inflatable grippers need fast, precise pressure control, not just on/off air.
  • PARTIAL Flexible and stretchable electronics — sensors that survive millions of stretch cycles are still the weak point of most soft machines.
  • PARTIAL Soft-body simulation and control — infinite degrees of freedom make classic robot control inadequate; learning-based methods help but need data.
  • EXISTS Collaborative-robot safety standards and practice — force and pressure limits for contact with people set the rules that let soft effectors work alongside workers.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: commercial soft and compliant grippers handling food, glass, fabric and packed goods — automation of pick-and-place tasks that rigid jaws bruise or crush.
  2. Next: soft arms and hands with integrated sensing working in shared cells — assembly and inspection steps that mix human judgement with machine repeatability.
  3. Next: on-demand stiffening (jamming, fibre or fluid) so one effector can be soft for gripping and rigid for pushing — fewer tool changes and wider task range per robot.
  4. Longer term: self-modelling soft robots that learn their own shape and adapt through wear — durable, general-purpose soft manipulators rather than single-task grippers.
