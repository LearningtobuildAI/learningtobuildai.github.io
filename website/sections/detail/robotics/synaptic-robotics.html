PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Neural culture — the living processor: a few hundred thousand neurons that grow into a spontaneously active network. Key parts: cortical neurons derived from induced pluripotent stem cells (or rodent primary cells), astrocyte support cells, glucose-and-salt culture medium, adhesion coating such as poly-D-lysine and laminin.
  • Electrode interface — the two-way plug between wet tissue and dry electronics. Key parts: microelectrode array (often 60 electrodes at roughly 100–200 µm spacing), high-density CMOS array with tens of thousands of electrodes at 10–20 µm spacing, biocompatible passivation layer, on-chip amplifiers and multiplexers.
  • Life support — keeps tissue at body conditions for weeks to months, not hours. Key parts: incubator held near 37 °C with 5 % CO2, microfluidic perfusion pump, medium reservoir and waste trap, bubble traps and sterile fluid lines, oxygen, glucose and pH sensors.
  • Readout electronics — detects the millivolt spikes of individual neurons and turns them into data. Key parts: low-noise amplifiers, band-pass filters around 300–3000 Hz, analogue-to-digital converters, spike-sorting and feature-extraction processor.
  • Stimulation path — writes signals back into the network — the basis of any learning. Key parts: constant-current stimulator, charge-balanced biphasic pulses, optogenetic light source and genetically encoded ion channels, where used.
  • Closed-loop controller and body — decodes network activity into action and encodes the robot's senses back as stimulation. Key parts: real-time computer running a decoding model, robot actuators and sensors, encoder mapping sensor readings to stimulation patterns.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Derive and quality-check neurons. Skin or blood cells are reprogrammed to stem cells, then driven to become cortical neurons over several weeks and screened for healthy spontaneous firing.
  2. Fabricate the chip. A CMOS or glass substrate is patterned with metal electrodes, insulated except at the tips, and coated with adhesion proteins that let neurons grip it.
  3. Seed and mature the culture. Cells are plated at a few thousand per square millimetre, fed every few days, and left for two to four weeks until the network settles into stable, patterned activity.
  4. Close the electrical loop. The array is wired to amplifiers and a computer that can record spikes and deliver stimulation within a few milliseconds — fast enough for the loop to feel instantaneous to the cells.
  5. Train the network. Stimulation patterns are paired with feedback chosen to exploit spike-timing-dependent plasticity, so the network's firing slowly reorganises toward the task rather than drifting randomly.
  6. Connect a body. Decoded output drives robot joints or a virtual agent's controls; the robot's sensors are re-encoded as stimulation, so the culture acts on the world it is being told about.
  7. Engineer field life support. For anything beyond a bench, the incubator becomes a miniaturised, shock-tolerant perfusion system with sterile fluidics, temperature control and waste removal — the hardest step, since a robot that falls over must not kill its own brain.
  8. Build in redundancy. Cultures die, so a practical machine would carry several independent units, automatic health monitoring and the ability to switch control when one degrades.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Reproducible neuron and organoid culture — without a steady supply of healthy, uniform neural tissue there is no reliable device.
  • PARTIAL Bidirectional high-density electrode arrays — laboratory CMOS arrays can stimulate and record thousands of sites, but not yet for months at a time in a moving machine.
  • PARTIAL Real-time spike sorting and decoding — the loop must close in milliseconds, which needs dedicated hardware rather than recorded-and-analysed-later data.
  • PARTIAL In-vitro learning and memory — cultures do show plasticity, but the stable, generalisable learning needed for useful tasks is not demonstrated.
  • NOT YET Long-term life support outside an incubator — there is no established way to keep cortical tissue viable for years in a portable, powered, moving platform.
  • NOT YET Oversight for neuron-based machines — rules for devices containing human-derived neural tissue, and for when they should be switched off, do not yet exist.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: cultured networks on multi-electrode arrays displaying stimulus-dependent activity; in 2022 human and mouse neurons in a dish were reported to learn a simple paddle game within minutes — proof that living networks can be trained through an electrode interface at all.
  2. Next: closed-loop laboratory hybrids where a neural culture controls a simple robot or simulated body and improves with practice — benchmark tasks and the first honest comparison against conventional spiking hardware on energy per decision.
  3. Next: neural cultures used as low-power accelerators for narrow pattern-recognition problems, accessed remotely as a service — a commercial reason to fund the life-support engineering.
  4. Longer term: portable, self-maintaining neural units with reliable fluidics and months-long viability — hybrid machines that leave the lab.
  5. Speculative: directed neuron-to-tissue interfaces for prosthetics, where cultured or grafted neurons talk to a patient's nervous system — prosthetic control that borrows biology's own signalling — far more plausible near-term than fully biological robots doing general labour.
