PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Vacuum-Insulated Building Envelopes are wall systems designed to minimize heat transfer through the building envelope by utilizing a partial vacuum between two layers of material, typically glass or polymer.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These envelopes consist of double-walled glass or polymer shells with an evacuated interior space. The vacuum significantly reduces thermal conduction and radiation, leading to improved insulation properties compared to traditional materials like fiberglass or foam.

5Materials Used
  • glass
  • polymer
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves precision engineering to create double-walled shells that can maintain a vacuum over long periods. The process requires high-quality materials and controlled environments, including vacuum baking to remove moisture and ensure seal integrity.

7Build Process

Installation typically follows standard construction practices but with special attention to maintaining the vacuum integrity during assembly and sealing gaps between the walls.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking and precision engineering requirements.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Core material — provides structural support against atmospheric pressure while minimising solid conduction. Key parts: fumed silica, aerogel, glass fibre, opacifier (e.g. silicon carbide).
  • Barrier envelope — maintains the vacuum by blocking air and moisture. Key parts: multilayer polymer-metal film, edge seal, protective outer layer.
  • Getter and desiccant — absorbs residual gases and moisture that would otherwise degrade the vacuum. Key parts: getter material (e.g. barium), desiccant (e.g. zeolite).
  • Structural framing and integration — holds panels in place, protects them from puncture, and manages thermal bridging at edges. Key parts: timber or steel studs, thermal breaks, airtight tapes and sealants.
  • Vacuum glazing — transparent vacuum insulation for windows. Key parts: two glass panes, spacer pillars, edge seal, low-emissivity coating.
  • Monitoring and quality control — verifies vacuum integrity and thermal performance over the building's life. Key parts: pressure sensors, thermal imaging, moisture sensors.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Prepare the core. Fumed silica is mixed with an opacifier to reduce radiative heat transfer, then pressed into boards of the desired thickness.
  2. Encapsulate the core. The porous core is placed inside a multilayer barrier film bag that will hold the vacuum.
  3. Evacuate and seal. The bag is put in a vacuum chamber, evacuated to below about 1 millibar, and heat-sealed to create an airtight panel.
  4. Add getters and desiccants. Small amounts of gas-absorbing and moisture-absorbing materials are inserted to mop up outgassed species over time.
  5. Assemble panels into the wall. Panels are laid in a tight grid within the wall cavity, with minimal gaps that are sealed using airtight tape and sealant.
  6. Install structural framing. Timber or steel studs, thermal breaks, and protective layers are added to support the panels and prevent puncture or edge heat loss.
  7. Fabricate vacuum glazing. For windows, two glass panes are separated by tiny support pillars, edge-sealed with a low-melting-point glass or metal, then evacuated and sealed.
  8. Commission and monitor. Thermal imaging and pressure or moisture sensors check that the vacuum is intact and the envelope performs as designed.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-barrier films — they must block air and water vapour for decades; food-packaging films are a starting point but need higher performance.
  • EXISTS Porous core materials — fumed silica and aerogel already provide the low solid conduction and compressive strength needed.
  • EXISTS Vacuum sealing at scale — industrial vacuum chambers and heat sealers are used today to make vacuum insulation panels for appliances.
  • PARTIAL Vacuum glazing — commercially available but limited in size, expensive, and not yet widely used in building envelopes.
  • PARTIAL Building integration and standards — standards exist for individual panels, but full wall systems with vacuum insulation lack widely accepted test methods.
  • PARTIAL Durable edge seals — seals must survive thermal cycling and handling without leaking, which is a key reliability challenge.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: vacuum insulation panels in appliances and niche building retrofits — very low thermal conductivity in thin profiles where space is tight.
  2. Next: larger vacuum insulation panels and vacuum glazing in high-performance walls and windows — thinner, highly insulating building envelopes for extreme climates.
  3. Next: prefabricated vacuum-insulated wall and roof modules with integrated monitoring — faster, more reliable construction and easier quality control.
  4. Longer term: full building envelopes using vacuum insulation at mainstream cost — near-zero heating and cooling demand in very cold or very hot regions.
