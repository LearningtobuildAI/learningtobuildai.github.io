PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Automated Modular Assembly is a robotic technology that enables the precise assembly of pre-fabricated, high-precision modules on-site. This method allows for modular construction in various settings, including residential and commercial buildings.

2Problem It Solves

Manual on-site assembly of high-precision components is labor-intensive, error-prone, and time-consuming. Automated Modular Assembly addresses these issues by providing a more efficient, accurate, and repeatable method for construction.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

High-torque robotic arms are used to place modules with millimeter-perfect precision. Digital twin synchronization ensures real-time alignment between the virtual model and physical assembly process.

5Materials Used
  • high-torque robotic arms
  • precision sensors
  • digital twin software
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves the production of high-torque robotic arms, precision sensors, and digital twin software. These components are then integrated into an automated assembly system.

7Build Process

Modules are pre-fabricated off-site, transported to the construction site, and assembled using the robotic system. The process is guided by a digital twin that ensures accurate placement of each module.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. Overall operational power consumption is moderate but varies based on system size and complexity.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Robotic manipulator — Lifts and positions prefabricated modules weighing hundreds of kilograms with high precision. Key parts: Articulated arm with high-torque joints, Servo motors and brakes, Wrist with multiple degrees of freedom.
  • Mobile platform or gantry — Moves the manipulator around the construction site to reach different assembly points. Key parts: Wheeled or tracked base, Overhead gantry rails, Stabilizing outriggers.
  • End effector — Grips the module and makes fine adjustments for millimetre-level alignment. Key parts: Vacuum grippers or mechanical clamps, Fine-adjustment actuators, Force/torque sensors.
  • Sensing and metrology — Measures the position of the robot, module and target within a few millimetres. Key parts: Laser trackers or total stations, Stereo cameras and LiDAR, Inertial measurement units.
  • Digital twin and control software — Maintains a live model of the building and plans robot motions without collisions. Key parts: BIM model with as-built updates, Real-time synchronization engine, Path planning and collision avoidance.
  • Module interface system — Provides standardized connections so modules can be joined quickly and accurately. Key parts: Self-aligning connectors, Bolted or welded joints, Quick-release fasteners.
  • Safety system — Allows robots and workers to share the site safely. Key parts: Proximity sensors, Emergency stops, Geofencing and light curtains.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design for robotic assembly. Create a BIM model with standardized lifting points, connection interfaces and tolerances (e.g. ±2 mm) suitable for robotic handling.
  2. Prefabricate modules off-site. Manufacture wall panels, floor cassettes or service modules in a factory where jigs and robots can hold tight tolerances.
  3. Transport and stage modules. Deliver modules to site in the planned assembly sequence and place them within reach of the robot.
  4. Set up the robotic system. Deploy the mobile robot or gantry and establish a site coordinate system using laser trackers or total stations.
  5. Synchronize the digital twin. Survey the site and update the digital twin to match as-built conditions so the robot's plan reflects reality.
  6. Robotic pick and place. The manipulator picks a module, moves it to the target location and uses vision and force feedback to align it within millimetres.
  7. Fasten and connect. The robot or a worker secures bolts, welds or grouted connections between the placed module and the structure.
  8. Verify installation. Scan the installed module and compare it to the digital twin; correct any deviation before proceeding.
  9. Repeat and hand over. Continue for all modules, then hand the completed shell to trades for finishing and services.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-payload mobile robots — Industrial arms can lift heavy loads, but mobile, outdoor versions that work on uneven ground are still rare.
  • PARTIAL Real-time digital twin synchronization — BIM is widely used, but live sync with robotic control on a construction site is only emerging.
  • EXISTS Precise outdoor localization (millimetre to centimetre) — Laser trackers and total stations already provide this accuracy in surveying and industrial assembly.
  • PARTIAL Standardized modular interfaces — Some standards exist for containerized modules, but no universal interface allows any robot to join any module.
  • PARTIAL Safety standards for human-robot collaboration on site — Collaborative robot standards exist in factories, but construction sites add weather, uneven ground and untrained workers.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: Semi-automated tools for specific tasks (e.g. bricklaying, drilling) with human supervision — Productivity gains on repetitive tasks and data on robot performance in real sites.
  2. Next: Single robotic systems that place modular panels in controlled low-rise projects — Faster assembly of repetitive building types such as warehouses or housing.
  3. Next: Multi-robot teams assembling complete low-rise buildings with minimal human intervention — Round-the-clock construction and reduced labour on site.
  4. Longer term: Fully autonomous assembly of high-rise or complex structures integrated with design and supply chain — Buildings erected with millimetre precision and predictable schedules.
