PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Microbial Induced Calcite Precipitation (MICP) is a bio-engineering technique that uses specific bacteria to induce the precipitation of calcite (calcium carbonate) within materials, such as concrete or soil, to enhance their structural integrity and stability.

2Problem It Solves

MICP addresses issues of infrastructure degradation due to cracking and erosion by providing a self-healing mechanism that can extend the lifespan of concrete structures and stabilize soil environments without the need for frequent maintenance interventions.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

In MICP, selected bacteria are introduced into the material. These bacteria consume organic matter and release enzymes that facilitate the chemical reaction between calcium ions from the surrounding environment and carbonate ions, precipitating calcite crystals. Over time, these crystals fill cracks and bind aggregates, effectively healing or stabilizing the material.

5Materials Used
  • calcium ions
  • carbonate ions
  • bacteria strains
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves selecting appropriate bacteria strains, preparing bacterial suspensions, and formulating them into a solution or paste that can be applied to the material. The application method can vary from direct injection to surface coating.

7Build Process

During construction, MICP solutions are typically applied after initial curing of concrete or soil stabilization is achieved. This involves applying the bacterial suspension to cracks or areas requiring repair and allowing it to interact with the environment over time to induce calcite precipitation.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. The overall operational power consumption is relatively low compared to traditional repair methods but higher during the initial manufacturing phase.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Bacterial culture — provides the microbes that produce the urease enzyme driving calcite precipitation. Key parts: ureolytic bacteria such as Sporosarcina pasteurii, growth medium, bioreactor.
  • Cementation solution — supplies the urea and calcium that the bacteria convert into calcite. Key parts: urea, calcium chloride or calcium lactate, nutrients and buffers.
  • Carrier and encapsulation — protects bacteria from harsh conditions so they survive mixing, placement or soil application. Key parts: hydrogel beads, porous lightweight aggregate, spore coatings.
  • Delivery system — introduces bacteria and nutrients into the target material. Key parts: mixing into fresh concrete, injection into cracks, percolation or spraying for soil.
  • Target material — the concrete, soil or sand that is to be healed or strengthened. Key parts: concrete cracks, porous soil, sand columns.
  • Monitoring and testing — confirms that calcite has formed and measures the resulting improvement. Key parts: permeability tests, strength tests, microscopy and chemical analysis.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select and culture bacteria. A suitable ureolytic strain is grown in a bioreactor, often to the spore-forming stage so it can survive storage and mixing.
  2. Encapsulate the bacteria. For concrete, spores are trapped in hydrogel beads or porous aggregate to protect them from the high pH and mechanical stress of mixing.
  3. Prepare the cementation solution. Urea, a calcium source such as calcium lactate, and nutrients are dissolved in water to form the feed that will drive precipitation.
  4. Place the bacteria and feed. In concrete, encapsulated bacteria are added during batching; in soil, bacteria and feed are percolated or sprayed into the ground.
  5. Trigger precipitation. When water is present, the bacteria produce urease, which breaks down urea and raises the local pH, causing calcium carbonate to crystallise.
  6. Let calcite grow. Calcite crystals fill cracks, coat sand grains and bind them together, reducing permeability and increasing strength.
  7. Test and verify. Samples are tested for crack closure, water tightness and compressive strength; field performance is monitored over time.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Ureolytic bacteria that tolerate concrete and soil — strains such as Sporosarcina pasteurii are well studied and can be grown at scale.
  • PARTIAL Encapsulation and immobilisation techniques — lab methods work, but keeping bacteria alive through real mixing and placement is still being proven.
  • EXISTS Low-cost urea and calcium sources — these are commodity chemicals, though the volumes needed for large projects matter for cost.
  • PARTIAL Understanding of precipitation kinetics in porous media — uniform precipitation is hard to control; it can clog pores unevenly or be too slow.
  • NOT YET Scalable production and quality control — there is no established supply chain for bacterial additives at infrastructure scale.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot trials of self-healing concrete and soil stabilisation — real-world performance data and early niche applications.
  2. Next: commercial crack repair and dust suppression products — reduced maintenance costs for concrete structures and unpaved roads.
  3. Next: standardised bacterial additives for concrete batching — wider use in new construction, cutting repair needs and cement demand.
  4. Longer term: large-scale soil improvement and erosion control — lower-carbon ground improvement compared with cement or chemical grouts.
