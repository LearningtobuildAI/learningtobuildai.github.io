PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Fungal inoculum — provides living hyphae that digest the waste and bind it into a solid mass. Key parts: selected fungal strain (e.g. oyster mushroom or turkey tail), grain or sawdust spawn, sterile culture technique.
  • Waste substrate — the agricultural residue that feeds the fungus and forms the bulk of the brick. Key parts: hemp hurd, straw, corn stalks or sawdust.
  • Mould and growth chamber — shapes the brick and holds the warm, humid, clean conditions the fungus needs. Key parts: brick moulds, incubation room, temperature and humidity control.
  • Mycelial binder network — the hyphae wrap around substrate particles and act as a natural glue. Key parts: hyphal network, extracellular matrix.
  • Heat treatment and drying — stops fungal growth, kills spores and sets the composite. Key parts: oven or kiln, ventilation.
  • Finishing and protection — makes the brick water-resistant, fire-safe and ready for building. Key parts: coatings or sealants, fire retardants, structural frame (for hybrid designs).
  • Testing and quality control — checks strength, density, thermal performance and fire behaviour. Key parts: compression testing, thermal conductivity measurement, fire testing.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Prepare the substrate. Agricultural waste is chopped to a uniform size and pasteurised or sterilised to remove competing moulds and bacteria.
  2. Inoculate. The cooled substrate is mixed with fungal spawn so the hyphae can spread through it.
  3. Fill the moulds. The mixture is packed into brick-shaped moulds, often with a light press to set the density.
  4. Incubate. Moulds are kept dark at around 20–25 °C and high humidity for days to weeks while the mycelium grows through and binds the particles.
  5. Demould and heat-treat. Bricks are removed and heated to roughly 70–80 °C to kill the fungus and drive off moisture, stopping further growth.
  6. Finish and protect. Surfaces may be sanded and coated with a water-resistant or fire-retardant finish, or combined with a structural frame.
  7. Test and certify. Samples are tested for compressive strength, density, thermal conductivity and fire performance before use.
  8. Assemble. Bricks are laid with mortar or dry-jointed as non-structural infill, insulation or acoustic panels; load-bearing use requires a validated hybrid system.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Fungal strain selection and sterile culture — mycology and mushroom farming already provide reliable strains and clean handling methods.
  • EXISTS Agricultural waste supply chain — farms produce straw, hemp hurd and other residues in large volumes.
  • EXISTS Controlled environment incubation — mushroom farms already control temperature, humidity and cleanliness at scale.
  • PARTIAL Fire and building code standards for bio-composites — mycelium composites are being tested, but codes and certifications are still developing.
  • PARTIAL Moisture resistance and durability data — mycelium absorbs water unless coated, and long-term outdoor performance is not fully proven.
  • PARTIAL Structural reinforcement or hybrid design — plain mycelium bricks are weak in compression and tension, so they need frames or engineered composites for load-bearing walls.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: non-structural insulation, acoustic panels, packaging and temporary pavilions — low-carbon, compostable materials for interiors and short-life structures.
  2. Next: engineered mycelium composite bricks with coatings and frames for non-load-bearing walls — wider use in low-rise construction as infill and cladding.
  3. Next: load-bearing mycelium composites validated by building codes — carbon-negative structural elements for houses and small buildings.
  4. Longer term: mass production integrated with local agricultural waste streams — circular, locally made building materials that displace concrete and fired clay in some applications.
