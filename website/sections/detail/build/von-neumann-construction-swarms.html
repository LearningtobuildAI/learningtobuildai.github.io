PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Prospecting and mining units — find and extract usable material — regolith, ice, metal-bearing rock — without human crews. Key parts: mobile excavators, sampling sensors, site mapping.
  • Material refinery — turn raw dug material into usable feedstock. Key parts: crushing and sorting, electrolysis or reduction cells to free metals and oxygen, powder and wire production.
  • Fabrication line — make parts from that feedstock rather than shipping them from Earth. Key parts: additive manufacturing (sintering, powder-bed, wire-feed), forming and joining tools, small foundry.
  • Assemblers — position, join and test new machines, including copies of themselves. Key parts: manipulator arms, mobile assembly platforms, metrology and inspection.
  • Power and thermal — keep the whole loop running day and night, in vacuum and extreme temperatures. Key parts: solar arrays or small fission reactor, batteries, radiators.
  • Swarm control — coordinate thousands of units toward one structure without a human directing each one. Key parts: distributed task allocation, localisation and comms mesh, replication gating and shutdown logic.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Deliver a seed factory. Launch a compact starter kit of machines — a mining unit, a refinery, a printer and a few assemblers — since no design starts fully self-replicating.
  2. Close the loop on simple mass. Measure the closure fraction: what share of the factory's mass, by part type, can be made from local material. Start with structural beams, panels and fasteners.
  3. Extract feedstock. Mine regolith or rock, reduce oxides to get metals and oxygen, and mill the output into powder and wire the printers can use.
  4. Print and machine parts. Fabricate the simple, bulky components locally, while continuing to import the hard ones — chips, bearings, motors, seals — from Earth.
  5. Assemble a copy. Robots build a second set of machines from those parts, using their own arms and the same jigs they were made with. This step, not the printing, is the hard one.
  6. Raise the closure fraction by generation. Each generation makes a slightly larger share of itself; the goal is to shrink the imported 'vitamin' list toward nothing.
  7. Scale the swarm. Once copies can be made, unit count grows on a doubling curve, and control software partitions work across the whole population.
  8. Assemble the megastructure. The swarm converges on the target — a large solar array, a habitat, a radio telescope dish — building it in parallel from many small robots.
  9. Inspect, repair, retire. Autonomous inspection and self-repair keep the population working; failed units are recycled back into feedstock.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Theory of self-reproducing machines — von Neumann's universal-constructor work in the mid-20th century set out what replication logically requires; NASA's 1980 summer study on advanced space automation sketched a self-replicating lunar factory.
  • PARTIAL In-situ resource utilisation — small-scale extraction has been demonstrated in labs and on Mars, but not as an industrial supply chain.
  • PARTIAL Additive manufacturing from local feedstock — regolith and metal powders can be sintered or melted into parts, but strength, tolerance and repeatability are far below aerospace standards.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous multi-robot coordination — swarms of tens to hundreds have been demonstrated; thousands of units operating for years without intervention is untested.
  • NOT YET Radiation-tolerant, repairable electronics — the computing and motor components are exactly the parts hardest to make off-Earth, and the ones most likely to fail.
  • PARTIAL Low-cost heavy launch — a seed factory only pays off if its mass is small and its delivery cheap.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: terrestrial testbeds where robot teams mine, refine and print simple parts — data on closure fractions and failure modes.
  2. Next: a robotic outpost that makes structural elements and oxygen from local rock — parts of a base built without launching every kilogram.
  3. Next: a factory that builds a meaningful share of its own replacement parts — growth limited by manufacturing rate rather than launch cadence.
  4. Longer term: supervised replication at scale, building large orbital or lunar structures — megastructures whose mass never has to leave Earth — contingent on solving electronics, control and safe replication limits.
