PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Carbon-negative concrete is a type of concrete that incorporates captured carbon dioxide (CO2) to form calcium carbonate through mineralization processes.

2Problem It Solves

Addressing climate change by reducing CO2 emissions and sequestering carbon dioxide while improving structural properties of traditional concrete.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

During production, CO2 is injected into the concrete mix. The CO2 reacts with the cement paste and other minerals in the mixture, forming stable calcium carbonate structures which permanently trap the CO2 within the concrete matrix. This process not only reduces atmospheric CO2 levels but also enhances the compressive strength of the resulting material.

5Materials Used
  • CO2
  • cement
  • sand
  • aggregate
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves capturing CO2 from industrial sources or direct air capture, then injecting it into a concrete mixture. This requires specialized equipment for CO2 injection and handling, as well as additional steps in the curing process to ensure complete mineralization.

7Build Process

Similar to conventional concrete, but with an added step of CO2 injection during mixing. The concrete is then cured under controlled conditions to allow for full mineralization of the incorporated CO2.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking (high kWh per ton).

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Binder — the cementitious glue; in a low-carbon mix it replaces most or all Portland clinker. Key parts: ground granulated blast-furnace slag, fly ash or calcined clay, alkali activator (sodium silicate/hydroxide), low-lime calcium silicate clinker.
  • Aggregate — bulk filler, which can itself be a carbonated material made from captured CO2. Key parts: sand and gravel, crushed steel slag or mine tailings, carbonate-bonded lightweight aggregate.
  • CO2 supply — provides the gas that will be chemically locked into the concrete. Key parts: flue-gas capture unit at a kiln, steel mill or refinery, purification and compression, tank, pipeline or truck delivery.
  • Injection or curing system — brings CO2 into contact with the fresh paste so it mineralises as calcium carbonate. Key parts: in-line injection manifold for mixing trucks, sealed curing chamber, pressure, temperature and humidity control, CO2 sensors and dosing controller.
  • Batching and placement — mixes and places the concrete fast, because carbonation accelerates setting. Key parts: batching plant, mixer or truck, superplasticiser admixtures, formwork and pumps.
  • Testing and accounting — proves the concrete meets structural standards and measures how much CO2 is genuinely bound. Key parts: compressive strength testing, thermogravimetric carbonate measurement, full life-cycle assessment software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Capture the CO2. A concentrated stream is captured from a cement kiln, steel plant or refinery flue gas, then purified and compressed for delivery. Using the kiln's own CO2 closes the loop at the source of most cement emissions.
  2. Prepare the binder. Slag, fly ash or calcined clay is ground and blended; a low-lime calcium silicate clinker may replace ordinary Portland clinker. The activator or clinker dose is kept as low as the strength target allows, since activators carry their own emissions.
  3. Batch and mix. Binder, aggregate, water and superplasticiser are combined. If carbonation happens in the mixer, CO2 is dosed into the drum under controlled pressure so it reacts with calcium in the paste before the mix stiffens.
  4. Place promptly. Concrete is poured into formwork within a shortened working window, because carbonation accelerates setting and reduces slump.
  5. Cure under CO2. Precast units go into a sealed chamber where CO2 concentration and pressure are raised for hours to days. The gas diffuses into the paste and reacts with calcium silicates and hydroxide to form stable calcium carbonate.
  6. Demould and finish. Forms are stripped and surfaces finished; a period of conventional moist curing may follow to reach the specified strength.
  7. Verify strength and storage. Cores are crushed for compressive strength and samples are analysed for carbonate content, so the amount of CO2 chemically locked in is measured rather than assumed.
  8. Account and deploy. A life-cycle assessment covering mining, transport, activator production, curing energy and end-of-life is run. Only if stored CO2 exceeds all of those emissions can the product honestly be called carbon-negative.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Low-clinker binders (alkali-activated slag/fly ash, calcined clay) — they cut the calcination emissions that make up most of cement's footprint, but are not yet permitted by every structural code.
  • EXISTS CO2 capture from industrial flue gas — amine scrubbing and similar processes operate commercially in other industries; fitting them to cement kilns is only now beginning.
  • PARTIAL Mineral carbonation chemistry at scale — CO2 reacting with calcium and magnesium silicates to form permanent carbonates is well understood and demonstrated, but reaction rates and reactor throughput still limit use.
  • PARTIAL Standards and product specifications — engineers will not specify a new concrete for bridges and buildings until standards define its strength, durability and testing.
  • PARTIAL Cheap low-carbon heat and electricity — grinding, activating and curing all consume energy; the emissions saved by the chemistry can be given back by a fossil-heavy grid.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: carbon-cured precast blocks and pavers sold commercially, with CO2 injected during mixing or curing — a working supply chain and a handful of verified tonnes of permanently stored carbon per plant.
  2. Next: mineralised aggregate and alkali-activated structural concrete accepted into building codes — replacing clinker in ready-mix and cast-in-place work, where the vast majority of concrete volume sits.
  3. Next: batching plants sited beside cement kilns and steel mills and run on their captured flue gas — concrete production turned from a pure emissions source into a modest carbon sink.
  4. Longer term: whole-life carbon accounting that credits the slow natural reabsorption of CO2 by concrete without double-counting — honest, audited net-negative claims and a durable storage route for captured carbon.
