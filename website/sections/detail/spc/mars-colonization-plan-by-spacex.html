PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Launch and transfer system — gets mass off Earth and onto a Mars-bound trajectory at acceptable cost. Key parts: reusable super-heavy booster, reusable upper stage / crew vehicle, Raptor-class methane engines, orbital propellant depot.
  • Transit habitat — keeps a crew alive and healthy during the months-long cruise. Key parts: pressurised living volume, air and water recycling, radiation storm shelter, food and consumable stores.
  • Surface habitat — a place to live and work on Mars, shielded from radiation and cold. Key parts: pressurised modules, regolith or water shielding, airlocks, power and thermal control.
  • Power system — supplies electricity for life support, ISRU and heating, including through dust storms. Key parts: solar arrays with storage, small fission reactors, backup batteries.
  • ISRU propellant plant — makes the return fuel and breathing oxygen from the Martian atmosphere and ice. Key parts: water extraction from ice, electrolysis unit, Sabatier reactor (CO2 + hydrogen to methane), oxygen separator, cryogenic tanks and liquefaction.
  • Life support and food — closes the loops on air, water and eventually food. Key parts: CO2 removal, oxygen generation, water recovery, hydroponic or bioreactor food production.
  • Surface works and mobility — builds, maintains and explores. Key parts: pressurised rovers, cranes and regolith-moving equipment, construction robots.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Prove the rocket. Fly the fully reusable stack repeatedly, demonstrate booster recovery and upper-stage reuse, and cut cost per tonne to orbit.
  2. Master orbital refuelling. Launch tanker flights, dock them with the Mars vehicle and transfer cryogenic methane and oxygen in orbit — the step that makes a heavy Mars payload possible.
  3. Send robotic cargo first. Land uncrewed ships carrying power, habitats, rovers and ISRU equipment, ahead of any crew.
  4. Start making propellant. Robotic systems extract water ice, electrolyse it, and combine hydrogen with atmospheric CO2 to make methane and oxygen, storing it for the return trip.
  5. Build and shield the base. Modules are deployed and covered with regolith or water shielding; life support is commissioned and leak-tested.
  6. Land the first crew. A small crew arrives, lives in the base for a limited period, and verifies that locally produced propellant can actually launch them home.
  7. Grow the settlement. More crews and cargo arrive, food production scales up, and industry (metals, ceramics, fuel) is built from local materials.
  8. Reach self-sufficiency. The settlement can replace its own critical equipment and expand without resupply from Earth — the actual definition of a colony.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Rapidly reusable super-heavy launch — without it, the mass needed for a settlement cannot be lifted affordably; booster recovery is demonstrated, full reuse is not.
  • NOT YET Orbital cryogenic propellant transfer — Mars vehicles must be refuelled in orbit; no large-scale transfer of liquid methane and oxygen has been demonstrated.
  • NOT YET Very large Mars entry, descent and landing — landed mass so far is about a tonne; a settlement requires tens of tonnes per landing.
  • PARTIAL ISRU propellant production — oxygen from Martian CO2 was demonstrated at small scale by the MOXIE instrument on NASA's Perseverance rover; full methane/oxygen plants remain unbuilt.
  • PARTIAL Closed-loop life support and radiation protection — the ISS recycles air and water but not food, and no practical shielding exists for the cruise and surface.
  • PARTIAL Surface power that survives dust storms — solar output drops sharply in global dust events; small fission reactors have been ground-tested but never flown.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: repeated flight tests and orbital refuelling demonstrations — confidence that the transport architecture works and its real cost.
  2. Next: robotic precursor landings with power and ISRU hardware — proof that fuel and oxygen can be made on Mars before people depend on it.
  3. Next: first crewed landing and return using locally made propellant — the first human footprints, and the end of one-shot missions.
  4. Longer term: a permanently occupied base with growing food and local manufacturing — repeated crews and expansion without constant resupply.
  5. Longer term: a settlement that can sustain itself if Earth stops sending ships — a genuine second home for humanity — the stated goal, and the hardest engineering problem of all.

18Glossary
in-situ resource utilization (ISRU)
The process of using local resources on a planet for various purposes, such as producing water and oxygen from Martian soil or CO2 from the atmosphere.
reusable rockets
Spacecraft designed to return to Earth after launch in order to be used multiple times, reducing overall mission costs.
