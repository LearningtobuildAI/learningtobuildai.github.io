PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Launch and in-space propulsion — escape Earth's gravity and travel between planets efficiently. Key parts: super-heavy-lift rockets, in-space transfer stages, nuclear thermal or electric propulsion, orbital propellant depots.
  • Life support and habitation — keep humans alive and healthy for years in deep space and on other worlds. Key parts: closed-loop air and water recycling, food production systems, radiation shielding, pressurised habitat modules.
  • Power and thermal control — provide electricity and manage heat where sunlight is weak or intermittent. Key parts: solar arrays, radioisotope thermoelectric generators, fission reactors, radiators.
  • Communications and navigation — maintain contact with Earth and determine position without GPS. Key parts: deep space network antennas, optical communication terminals, autonomous navigation sensors.
  • Entry, descent, and landing — deliver heavy payloads and humans safely to planetary surfaces. Key parts: heat shields, parachutes, retro-rockets, sky-crane or thruster landers.
  • In-situ resource utilisation — use local materials to make fuel, water, oxygen, and building materials. Key parts: regolith excavators, water extraction plants, electrolysis units, Sabatier reactors, 3D printing systems.
  • Robotics and autonomy — scout ahead, build infrastructure, and assist human crews. Key parts: rovers, drones, robotic arms, AI for autonomous operation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Robotic reconnaissance. Send orbiters, landers and rovers to map terrain, radiation, and resources such as water ice on the Moon and Mars.
  2. Demonstrate closed-loop life support. Test air, water and waste recycling on the International Space Station and in ground analogues, then add food production.
  3. Build deep space habitats. Design and test pressurised modules with radiation shielding, and decide whether artificial gravity is needed for multi-year trips.
  4. Create heavy-lift and in-space transport. Develop reusable super-heavy rockets, orbital propellant depots, and nuclear thermal or electric stages to cut travel time.
  5. Perfect heavy-payload entry, descent and landing. Scale up heat shields, supersonic retro-propulsion and precision landing so that habitats and crews can touch down on Mars.
  6. Prove in-situ resource utilisation. Extract water from lunar or Martian soil, split it into oxygen and hydrogen, and combine hydrogen with carbon dioxide to make methane fuel.
  7. Establish a lunar outpost. Use the Moon as a proving ground: live off the land, operate rovers remotely, and practice long-duration stays close to Earth.
  8. Send humans to Mars. Assemble an interplanetary transport in orbit, fly a crew to Mars, land, and live on resources produced ahead of time by robots.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Super-heavy-lift launch vehicles — existing rockets can send probes, but human Mars missions need larger, reusable launchers that are still in development.
  • PARTIAL Closed-loop life support — the ISS recycles most water and air, but full food production and decades-long reliability are unproven.
  • NOT YET Deep space radiation shielding — galactic cosmic rays and solar flares damage human tissue; no lightweight shield has been validated for long missions.
  • PARTIAL In-situ resource utilisation — instruments like MOXIE on Mars have made small amounts of oxygen, but extracting water and making fuel at scale is untested.
  • NOT YET Deep space propulsion — chemical rockets are too slow and fuel-hungry; nuclear thermal or electric propulsion could cut trip times but is not flight-ready.
  • EXISTS Autonomous robotics — rovers and landers already operate with increasing autonomy, and they are essential for pre-positioning supplies.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: robotic orbiters and rovers exploring the Moon, Mars and beyond — detailed maps of resources, hazards and landing sites.
  2. Next: NASA's Artemis programme returns humans to the Moon and builds a lunar Gateway — testing of habitats, spacesuits and ISRU in a real planetary environment.
  3. Next: Mars sample return and robotic demonstration of fuel production on Mars — proven technology for making rocket propellant and oxygen from Martian materials.
  4. Longer term: first human missions to Mars, using pre-positioned habitats and fuel — direct experience of interplanetary travel and surface operations.
  5. Far future: sustainable, Earth-independent settlements on the Moon and Mars — long-term human presence across the solar system.

18Glossary
in-situ resource utilization (ISRU)
The process of using resources found on other planets or moons to support human activities, such as producing water from ice or oxygen from the Martian atmosphere.
habitable module
A sealed, pressurized structure designed for crewed habitation that provides necessary life support systems and accommodations.
