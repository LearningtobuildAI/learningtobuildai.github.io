PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sail — the light-catching surface — a reflective membrane maybe 4 m across but weighing grams, since acceleration comes from having almost no mass. Key parts: sub-micron reflective film, metallic or dielectric coating, nanofabricated stiffening ribs and shape control.
  • Probe payload — a wafer-scale spacecraft carrying everything needed to observe and report back. Key parts: camera, processor and memory, laser or radio transmitter, photovoltaic power, attitude sensor.
  • Laser array — a ground installation kilometres across whose beams combine into one powerful, steerable pulse. Key parts: many laser emitters, phase-locking and beam-combining optics, adaptive optics, cooling and power supply.
  • Beam pointing and tracking — keeps the combined beam centred on the sail as it shrinks into the distance. Key parts: fast steering mirrors, sail-position sensing, feedback control.
  • Deployment and release mechanism — folds the sail for launch and releases it cleanly in vacuum. Key parts: stowed sail, hinges and tethers, separation system.
  • Communications downlink — returns data across light-years. Key parts: probe transmitter, large ground telescope receiver, photon-counting detectors.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fabricate the sail membrane. A reflective film is deposited onto a sacrificial substrate and patterned at the nanoscale so it stays flat and stiff while weighing almost nothing; the substrate is then etched away. The concept calls for a film hundreds of atoms thick, many times thinner than any sail flown so far.
  2. Build the probe. Camera, processor, transmitter and power source are integrated on a wafer-scale board of a few grams, using the same fabrication techniques as microelectronics.
  3. Assemble probe and sail. The sail is stowed with the probe at its centre, on a carrier spacecraft that will release it once in vacuum.
  4. Build the laser array. Thousands of emitters are phased together so their light adds constructively, with adaptive optics correcting for the atmosphere. Power must reach the gigawatt class, orders of magnitude beyond today's laser installations.
  5. Launch and deploy. A conventional rocket carries the carrier to space; the stowed probe separates and the sail unfurls, held by centrifugal spin or lightweight booms.
  6. Accelerate. The array fires for a few minutes over a fraction of an astronomical unit, pushing the sail with photon pressure. A perfect reflector feels about 2P/c, so gigawatts across a few square metres can drive a gram-scale probe to tens of thousands of kilometres per second — a large fraction of light speed — within minutes.
  7. Coast. The laser cannot follow the probe far; it coasts for years. At 20% of light speed the nearest stars are roughly two decades away.
  8. Image and transmit. The probe photographs its target during a flyby lasting hours and sends the data back by laser; the signal takes another four years or so to reach Earth.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-power, phase-locked laser arrays — beam-combining is proven at kilowatt to megawatt scale, but the concept needs gigawatts held on a target for minutes.
  • PARTIAL Ultra-thin sail materials — IKAROS in 2010 and LightSail 2 in 2019 proved sunlight can push a sail, but their films are thousands of times thicker than what is needed for laser acceleration.
  • PARTIAL Gram-scale integrated spacecraft — a single chip must combine camera, processor, power and a transmitter that survives interplanetary radiation.
  • PARTIAL Deep-space optical communication — laser links have been demonstrated near Earth; a multi-light-year downlink from a gram-scale transmitter is a different problem.
  • NOT YET Beam pointing and station-keeping — the entire drive only spends minutes accelerating the sail, so any drift off-beam wastes the shot.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: solar sails flying in interplanetary space; laser arrays demonstrating beam combining — proof that light pressure can guide and propel spacecraft, at modest speed.
  2. Next: a laser-accelerated gram-scale sail reaching kilometres per second in Earth orbit — a working beam-riding demonstration and better sail materials.
  3. Next: solar-system probes driven by laser or sunlight at hundreds of kilometres per second — fast access to the outer planets and a test bed for long-duration sails and comms.
  4. Longer term: a gram-scale probe sent toward the nearest star system — the first data from another star — decades after launch, and only if sails, laser arrays and downlinks all improve together.
