PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Survey and prospecting instruments — find and characterise a target body from a distance and up close. Key parts: visible and near-infrared spectrometers, gamma-ray and neutron spectrometers, radar, sampling mechanism, return capsule.
  • Autonomous guidance and navigation — fly, land and work around a body with tens of minutes to hours of light-travel delay. Key parts: terrain-relative navigation, optical and LiDAR sensors, onboard flight software.
  • Anchoring and movement system — hold position against a body whose gravity is a millionth of Earth's. Key parts: harpoons or drills, cold-gas thrusters, crawling or hopping legs.
  • Excavation and handling — loosen and collect loose regolith or harder rock in vacuum and low gravity. Key parts: scoops, augers, crushers and sieves, dust-tolerant mechanisms.
  • Processing plant — turn raw material into something worth shipping — water, oxygen, metals. Key parts: volatile bake-out ovens, electrolysis or carbonyl refining cells, solar concentrators or reactors, product storage tanks.
  • Transport and delivery — move product to where it is used — a depot, a station or Earth. Key parts: electric propulsion tugs, pressurised tanks or containers, aeroshell and parachute for Earth return.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the target. Asteroids are picked mainly by orbit: bodies reachable with about 5 km/s of velocity change are far cheaper to visit than those with huge ores but awkward orbits. Spectral type hints at composition — carbon-rich for water and organics, metallic for iron, nickel and platinum-group metals.
  2. Prospect it. Telescopes give only a rough type. A spacecraft uses spectrometers and radar to map composition, then a lander or hopper measures strength, porosity and buried ice. This is the step sample-return missions have already rehearsed.
  3. Test extraction at small scale. Sampling arms proved regolith can be grabbed in microgravity, and the MOXIE instrument on NASA's Perseverance rover electrolysed Martian carbon dioxide into a few grams of oxygen per hour in 2021–2023. Mining would scale that idea to tonnes.
  4. Anchor and mine. A miner must react against something to dig. Options include drilling anchors, harpoons, or simply using the spacecraft's own mass and thrusters to push a scoop into loose regolith while ejecta is captured.
  5. Separate the product. Carbonaceous material is heated in a sealed vessel so water and other volatiles boil off and are condensed; ice is then electrolysed into hydrogen and oxygen. Lunar regolith, roughly 40% oxygen by mass locked in oxides, can be reduced by molten-salt electrolysis or hydrogen reduction to release oxygen and metal.
  6. Store and ship. Products are stored as cryogenic liquids or compressed gas in tanks sized for the trip. Because returning mass to Earth's surface is so costly, the first customers would be in space: propellant depots and life-support for satellites and stations.
  7. Build a market. Mining only pays if something needs the product. The viable early loop is water to propellant at a depot, with metals used for in-space construction rather than shipped home.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Autonomous robotics and onboard AI — communication delays of minutes to hours make joystick mining impossible; robots must decide for themselves.
  • PARTIAL In-situ resource processing (ISRU) — oxygen from carbon dioxide and water from regolith have been shown in labs and one Mars instrument, never at production scale.
  • NOT YET Low-gravity anchoring and dust-tolerant machinery — no machine has ever dug into an asteroid; abrasive dust and no reaction force defeat conventional designs.
  • PARTIAL Cheap heavy launch and in-space propulsion — every kilogram of hardware must be launched or assembled in orbit, so cost per kilogram sets the whole economics.
  • PARTIAL Legal framework for resource ownership — national laws such as the US Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act of 2015 grant rights to extracted materials, but no international regime governs asteroid claims.
  • NOT YET A customer in space — with no propellant depots or orbital construction at scale, there is nothing to sell water or metal to.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Done: sample returns from near-Earth asteroids — JAXA's Hayabusa2 brought back about 5 grams from Ryugu in 2020, and NASA's OSIRIS-REx about 120 grams from Bennu in 2023 — ground-truth data on asteroid composition and the handling technology to collect regolith.
  2. Now: prospecting missions to metallic and ice-bearing bodies, such as NASA's Psyche spacecraft launched in 2023 and due at the metal-rich asteroid Psyche around 2029, plus lunar polar ice surveys — maps of where valuable material actually is.
  3. Next: small robotic demonstrations that extract water or oxygen from lunar or asteroid material and store it — a working ISRU supply chain.
  4. Next: in-space propellant depots buying water-derived fuel — the first paying market, and a reason to scale up mining.
  5. Longer term: commercial extraction of metals for orbital construction — a genuinely off-Earth industrial economy, if launch costs and law allow.
