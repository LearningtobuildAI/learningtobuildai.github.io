PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Available today

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Propulsion — makes thrust; modern launchers use staged-combustion or full-flow engines burning kerosene, methane or hydrogen. Key parts: turbo pumps, thrust chamber and nozzle, regenerative cooling channels, gimbal actuators.
  • Airframe and tanks — holds cryogenic propellant while staying light enough to reach orbit. Key parts: aluminium-lithium or stainless steel tank barrels, common bulkhead, interstage.
  • Recovery system — brings the booster back so it can fly again. Key parts: grid fins, landing legs, cold-gas thrusters, re-entry burn and restartable engines.
  • Avionics and flight software — flies the ascent, the landing and the payload deployment. Key parts: flight computers, inertial measurement units, GNSS receivers, software.
  • Spacecraft bus — the modular platform that carries the payload once in orbit. Key parts: structure, deployable solar arrays, batteries, electric thrusters, radios and antennas.
  • Ground segment — gets the vehicle to the pad, tested and tracked. Key parts: launch pad and strongback, propellant farms, range radar and telemetry, recovery ships.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design and simulate. Engineers size the tanks and engines against a target orbit, then run thousands of simulated flights to tune the guidance.
  2. Form the tanks. Aluminium-lithium panels are joined by friction stir welding, or stainless steel rings are welded into barrels; lighter tanks mean more payload.
  3. Build and test engines. Turbopumps, injectors and chambers are machined — often 3D-printed in one piece — then fired repeatedly on test stands to prove they survive throttling and restart.
  4. Integrate the stages. Tanks, engines, plumbing and avionics are stacked and the whole stage is pressure- and leak-tested.
  5. Static fire. The stacked rocket fires its engines held down on the pad, checking thrust, software and sensors before launch.
  6. Launch and stage separation. The first stage burns for a few minutes, separates, and the upper stage finishes the job of reaching orbital velocity.
  7. Recover the booster. After separation the first stage flips, coasts, re-enters, steers with grid fins, and relights engines to land on a ship or at the pad.
  8. Refurbish and refly. The booster is inspected, engines cleaned and components replaced as needed, then flown again — the core of the cost saving.
  9. Deploy and commission. The payload separates, solar arrays unfurl, and the satellite or crewed vehicle is checked out and handed over to its operator.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Cryogenic rocket engines and propellant handling — liquid oxygen with kerosene, methane or hydrogen is the only practical way to get useful mass to orbit.
  • EXISTS Precision guidance and landing — a booster only survives re-entry if it can steer and kill its velocity to within metres.
  • PARTIAL Reusable thermal protection and materials — heat shields that survive repeated re-entry are still the hardest part of full reuse.
  • EXISTS Electric (ion) propulsion — high-efficiency thrusters make small satellites and long-life spacecraft practical.
  • PARTIAL High-cadence low-cost manufacturing — reuse only pays off if vehicles can be built and turned around quickly.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: reusable first stages flying many missions each — routine, much cheaper access to low Earth orbit and large satellite constellations.
  2. Next: fully reusable two-stage vehicles and higher launch cadence — costs closer to the price of propellant, and much larger payloads.
  3. Next: in-space propellant transfer between vehicles — deep-space missions that would otherwise need enormous single launches.
  4. Longer term: orbital depots and deep-space propulsion such as nuclear thermal — sustained lunar and Mars operations; nuclear thermal is still at the reactor-test stage.
