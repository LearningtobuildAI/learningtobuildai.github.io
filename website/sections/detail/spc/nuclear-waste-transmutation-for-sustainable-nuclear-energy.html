1Definition

Nuclear Waste Transmutation for Sustainable Nuclear Energy is a process that converts long-lived radioactive isotopes from spent nuclear fuel into stable or shorter-lived elements.

4How It Works

This technology uses high-energy particles to transform the atomic nuclei of transuranic and fission products, reducing their radioactivity over time. It can be achieved through various methods such as particle accelerators, spallation neutron sources, or advanced reactors designed for this purpose.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Partitioning plant — separates spent fuel into streams so only the long-lived fraction is transmuted. Key parts: reprocessing line (uranium and plutonium recovery), minor-actinide separation (americium, curium, neptunium), long-lived fission product separation (technetium-99, iodine-129).
  • Target and fuel fabrication — turns the separated actinides into ceramic or metal pins that can survive intense irradiation. Key parts: inert-matrix or oxide fuel pins, remote hot cells with shielding, pellet pressing and pin welding.
  • Neutron source — supplies the neutrons that convert long-lived nuclei. Key parts: fast reactor core, or, high-power proton accelerator, spallation target (liquid lead-bismuth or lead).
  • Transmutation core — where neutrons are captured and the actinides largely fission. Key parts: subcritical core (ADS) or critical fast core, fuel assemblies with actinide targets, neutron reflectors and absorbers.
  • Cooling and heat removal — carries away the high power density from a compact core. Key parts: liquid-metal coolant loop, intermediate heat exchanger, steam generator or turbine.
  • Shielding, remote handling and safeguards — handles material that is intensely radioactive and weapons-usable. Key parts: biological shielding, remote manipulators, nuclear material accountancy.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Separate the spent fuel. Used fuel is reprocessed to recover uranium and plutonium, then the minor actinides and long-lived fission products are isolated in a second, harder separation step.
  2. Fabricate targets remotely. Americium and curium emit strong gamma and neutron radiation, so pins must be made and inspected behind shielding with remote manipulators.
  3. Build the neutron source. Either a fast reactor core is assembled, or a megawatt-class proton accelerator and a liquid-metal spallation target are built to generate neutrons without criticality.
  4. Load the core. Actinide-bearing assemblies are loaded alongside driver fuel, arranged so the neutron flux reaches every target pin.
  5. Irradiate. Neutrons are captured; most minor actinides fission into shorter-lived products, while technetium-99 and iodine-129 capture neutrons to become stable ruthenium and xenon.
  6. Cool the core. Liquid metal carries heat to heat exchangers, which can raise steam for electricity.
  7. Reprocess and repeat. Targets are unloaded, cooled, reprocessed and re-irradiated several times to reach high destruction rates.
  8. Condition the residue. What remains is far shorter-lived; it is vitrified and destined for geological disposal, with much less long-term heat and radiotoxicity.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Industrial reprocessing and advanced partitioning — the PUREX process is mature, but cleanly separating americium and curium from each other and from fission products is not yet routine.
  • PARTIAL Fast-spectrum reactors — only fast neutrons can fission minor actinides efficiently; a handful of fast reactors operate, mostly for power and research rather than waste burning.
  • NOT YET Accelerator-driven systems — a subcritical core needs a very reliable continuous megawatt proton beam; Belgium's MYRRHA is a long-running attempt to build one.
  • PARTIAL Actinide fuel fabrication — americium-bearing fuels have been irradiated in experiments, but not manufactured at industrial throughput.
  • PARTIAL Liquid-metal coolant handling — sodium and lead-bismuth loops exist but demand careful chemistry control and freeze protection.
  • EXISTS Safeguards and disposal policy — separated plutonium and americium raise proliferation concerns that must be managed alongside geological disposal.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: research irradiations of minor-actinide targets in test and fast reactors — evidence that transmutation works and how fast.
  2. Next: a dedicated accelerator-driven pilot facility — demonstration of subcritical waste burning with a reliable accelerator.
  3. Next: an industrial-scale transmutation plant coupled to a reprocessing centre — multi-recycling of actinides and a real reduction in long-lived waste.
  4. Longer term: closed fuel cycles where only short-lived fission products go to disposal — smaller disposal burden and wider public acceptance of nuclear power.

18Glossary
transmutation
The process of converting one atomic nucleus into another by changing the number of protons or neutrons.
spent nuclear fuel
Nuclear fuel that has been used in a reactor and is no longer capable of sustaining a chain reaction due to depletion of fissile material.
