PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Warm-water intake — feeds surface seawater at roughly 25 °C into the evaporator. Key parts: screened intake, large low-head pumps, pipework.
  • Cold-water pipe — reaches down 600–1000 m for water at around 4–5 °C — the single hardest piece of the plant. Key parts: long large-diameter HDPE or composite pipe, weighted intake head, suspended or seabed-anchored riser.
  • Heat exchangers — evaporate and condense the working fluid across a very small temperature difference, so they must be enormous. Key parts: evaporator, condenser, titanium or coated-aluminium tubes, biofouling and cleaning systems.
  • Power loop — turns the vapour pressure difference into electricity. Key parts: low-boiling-point working fluid (ammonia or refrigerant), turbine, generator.
  • Platform or shore plant — houses the machinery — either on land beside deep water or on a moored floating hull. Key parts: hull or concrete building, mooring for floating variants, seawater outfalls.
  • Export and co-product system — delivers power to shore and exploits the cold water for other uses. Key parts: subsea power cable, desalination unit, aquaculture or seawater air-conditioning take-off.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Select the site. Map the temperature profile with depth and confirm at least about 20 °C difference reachable within a short distance of a steep seabed, typically in the tropics.
  2. Fabricate the cold-water pipe. Long lengths of large-diameter plastic or composite pipe are made on shore, fitted with a weighted intake head, and floated ready for towing.
  3. Build the plant. A moored floating hull is assembled, or a shore station is built at a site where deep water comes close to land.
  4. Install the heat exchangers. Very large evaporator and condenser banks are fitted with corrosion-resistant tubes and access for cleaning.
  5. Sink and connect the cold-water pipe. The pipe is towed to site, sunk under controlled ballasting, connected to the plant's cold-water pump, and its intake positioned at depth.
  6. Charge the power loop. The closed loop is filled with working fluid and the turbine-generator commissioned.
  7. Commission and manage fouling. Warm and cold flows are ramped up while operators watch for biological growth, corrosion and the effect of storms on the pipe.
  8. Deliver power and co-products. Electricity goes ashore by subsea cable; the spent cold water can feed desalination or aquaculture.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Long deep-water pipes surviving at sea — pipes reaching about 1000 m have worked in pilot plants, but a storm-resilient, cheap version at full scale is unproven.
  • PARTIAL Very large low-temperature heat exchangers — a temperature difference of only 20 °C means vast surface area and high parasitic pumping losses.
  • EXISTS Offshore floating platforms and mooring — the oil and gas industry already builds and moors large hulls in deep water.
  • EXISTS Subsea power cables — power must reach a coastal grid, often from several kilometres offshore.
  • PARTIAL Corrosion and biofouling control in warm seawater — tropical seawater attacks tubes and grows films that blunt heat transfer.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot plants of roughly 100 kW or less on tropical coasts and islands, often sharing a site with aquaculture or desalination — real operating data on pipes, heat exchangers and fouling.
  2. Next: pre-commercial plants of a few megawatts serving island grids — a credible cost comparison with diesel generation on remote islands.
  3. Next: floating plants that make power, fresh water or hydrogen offshore — marine energy for places with no suitable coastal shelf.
  4. Longer term: 100 MW-class plants in the tropics — firm, round-the-clock baseload power from the ocean's temperature gradient.
