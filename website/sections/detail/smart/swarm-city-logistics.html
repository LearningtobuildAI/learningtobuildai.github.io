PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Delivery pods — small ground robots that carry individual parcels or food orders the last few kilometres. Key parts: electric drivetrain, battery, sensor suite (cameras, lidar, ultrasound), lockable cargo compartment.
  • Trunk and hub layer — moves goods in bulk between neighbourhood hubs, where pods pick them up. Key parts: electric vans or light trucks, urban consolidation centres, parcel sorting equipment.
  • Coordination layer — lets hundreds of pods share routes, kerbs and charging points without a central dispatcher for every decision. Key parts: swarm or multi-agent planning software, traffic simulation, fleet telemetry, communication network.
  • Street interface — the physical places where pods stop, load and park. Key parts: kerbside micro-depots, loading bays, lockers, charging and battery-swap stations.
  • Remote supervision — humans watch the fleet and take over when a pod is stuck. Key parts: teleoperation consoles, intervention software, incident logging.
  • Standards and access rules — without shared pod dimensions and data formats, operators cannot hand goods to each other. Key parts: common pallet and tote sizes, data-exchange protocols, vehicle certification.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix a pod standard. Agree common footprint, weight limit, tote sizes, battery voltage and a data format, so any operator's pod can carry any operator's parcel. This is the step that makes a city-wide mesh possible at all.
  2. Build neighbourhood hubs. Convert underused buildings or car parks within a few kilometres of homes into micro-depots where trunk vehicles unload and pods are charged and dispatched.
  3. Instrument the kerb. Map and digitise loading bays, footpath widths, crossings and steep slopes, then add sensors or beacons so pods know where they may stop.
  4. Train the swarm in simulation. Run millions of simulated deliveries on a digital twin of the city to test routing, congestion and kerb allocation before any real robot appears.
  5. Pilot in one district. Deploy a few dozen pods in a single walkable neighbourhood, with remote supervisors, to learn failure modes and public reaction.
  6. Connect hubs into a mesh. Link multiple districts with trunk movements so goods can travel across the city by being handed from pod to van to pod.
  7. Scale and standardise operations. Move from company-specific fleets to shared depots and interoperable pods, with cities setting access rules.
  8. Harden for all weather and demand. Solve rain, snow, flooding, hills, crowded markets and peak days, which are what break pilots.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Autonomous navigation among pedestrians — sidewalk robots operate today but only in limited areas, at low speed, with remote help when stuck.
  • PARTIAL Swarm coordination at city scale — multi-agent planning works in simulation and small fleets; thousands of units sharing kerbs is untested.
  • NOT YET Standardised modular load carriers — without shared pod and tote dimensions, goods cannot pass between competing fleets.
  • PARTIAL Digital kerb and street mapping — cities rarely hold accurate, machine-readable data on loading bays, footpaths and crossings.
  • PARTIAL Cheap, dense charging and swapping — a city-wide fleet needs many small, low-power charging points on kerbs and in depots.
  • NOT YET Liability and right-of-way rules — who pays when a pod blocks a footpath or collides is largely unsettled.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: sidewalk delivery robots and autonomous vans in limited districts — real data on navigation, public acceptance and last-mile cost.
  2. Next: shared micro-depots and digital kerb management in a handful of cities — pods can be loaded and charged outside company silos.
  3. Next: one city district where pods, vans and lockers are coordinated by common software — proof that a mesh beats individual fleets on cost and congestion.
  4. Longer term: open pod standards and cross-operator handover city-wide — goods move between any carrier, the way parcels already move between postal networks.
  5. Speculative: cities where a large share of physical goods are moved autonomously — quieter, less congested streets — but only if energy, safety and public consent all hold.
