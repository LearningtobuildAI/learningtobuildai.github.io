PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Autonomous Delivery Robots are small self-driving vehicles designed to transport goods along sidewalks and within campuses for last-mile delivery tasks.

2Problem It Solves

They address the inefficiencies and high costs associated with last-mile delivery by automating the process, reducing human intervention needed for navigation and parcel handling.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These robots use a combination of cameras, lidar sensors, and detailed mapping data to navigate their environment. They can operate on pedestrian paths, cross streets, and handle parcel handoffs with the assistance of remote operators who are available as backups in case of issues.

5Materials Used
  • aluminum

  • sensors
  • electronics
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing involves assembling components such as wheels, sensors (cameras, lidar), computing units, and communication systems. The robots are built in a modular fashion to allow for easy updates and maintenance of hardware and software.

7Build Process

Robots are typically assembled using off-the-shelf parts and custom-built enclosures. They undergo rigorous testing in controlled environments before deployment in real-world settings.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Chassis and drivetrain — a small wheeled platform stable enough to carry a parcel over kerbs and uneven paving. Key parts: six-wheel drive with suspension, weather-sealed body, cargo compartment with powered lid, lights and audible warning.
  • Perception sensors — sense pedestrians, cyclists, pets, kerbs, crossings and obstacles. Key parts: cameras (fisheye and stereo), lidar, ultrasonic and radar sensors, IMU and wheel odometry.
  • Onboard autonomy — drive the route without a human, at walking pace, and stop safely when confused. Key parts: local compute, HD map and localisation, path planner, obstacle detection and braking.
  • Remote supervision — one human watches many robots and drives any that get stuck. Key parts: teleoperation link over cellular, fleet console, incident logging.
  • Power and charging — run a shift on a battery and return to a base to recharge. Key parts: lithium-ion pack, charging contacts and dock.
  • Handoff and order backend — links the robot to a shop or app and lets the customer open it. Key parts: order dispatch software, unlock code or app control, fleet scheduling.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Map the operating area. Sidewalks, crossings, kerb heights and gradients are surveyed and turned into a high-definition map, because pavement maps rarely exist in usable form.
  2. Design the chassis. The platform is sized to carry a small parcel, climb kerbs of roughly 10 cm, and stay upright on slopes and in rain.
  3. Assemble the sensor suite. Cameras, lidar and ultrasonics are mounted, sealed and calibrated so their measurements agree.
  4. Train the autonomy software. Engineers collect and label thousands of hours of footage, then test perception and planning in simulation before road trials.
  5. Build the robots. Units are assembled in low volumes, each tested on an indoor track for braking, steering and lid closure.
  6. Run a controlled pilot. A campus or neighbourhood is mapped, a remote-operations centre is staffed, and a small fleet runs short routes with heavy supervision.
  7. Scale the fleet. Route density rises, more robots share one operator, and the service integrates with delivery apps and shops.
  8. Operate and maintain. Robots run shifts at about walking pace, return to dock to charge, and are cleaned and repaired between shifts.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Low-cost lidar and camera modules — perception used to be too expensive for a machine costing less than a car.
  • PARTIAL High-definition pedestrian and kerb maps — usable sidewalk geometry is missing in most cities.
  • PARTIAL Low-speed autonomy in crowded spaces — the hard problems are unpredictable pedestrians, dogs, roadworks and wet leaves.
  • EXISTS Cheap lithium-ion batteries and motors — they make a shift's worth of range affordable.
  • PARTIAL Remote teleoperation over cellular networks — stuck robots need a fallback, and one operator must cover many robots to make the economics work.
  • PARTIAL City permits for pavement robots — rules vary from city to city and often cap fleet size or speed.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot fleets delivering food and groceries on campuses and in a few neighbourhoods — real data on reliability, cost per delivery and pedestrian behaviour.
  2. Next: one remote operator supervising many robots, with denser routes and app integration — a cost per delivery that competes with a human courier.
  3. Next: purpose-built lightweight robots that cross roads and last several years — coverage of ordinary residential streets, not just campuses.
  4. Longer term: shared curb-side charging and regulated pavement access in many cities — robots as routine local infrastructure alongside bikes and scooters.
