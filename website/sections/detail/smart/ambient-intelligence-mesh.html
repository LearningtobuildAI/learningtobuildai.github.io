9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sensing layer — detects people, air, sound and objects without anybody touching a device. Key parts: millimetre-wave radar and passive infrared occupancy sensors, temperature, humidity and CO₂ sensors, microphones and ambient-light sensors, camera modules (where privacy allows).
  • Compute and edge layer — processes sensor data locally so raw footage and audio never leave the room. Key parts: microcontrollers and low-power AI accelerators, on-device inference models, federated or anonymising software.
  • Networking — connects hundreds of nodes per building without new cabling to each one. Key parts: low-power wireless mesh (Thread, Zigbee, Bluetooth mesh), Wi-Fi and 5G/6G small cells, Power-over-Ethernet for wired nodes.
  • Output layer — presents information on surfaces, in the air or as sound. Key parts: laser projectors and waveguides embedded in glass, transparent and micro-LED panels, directional speakers, tunable and addressable lighting.
  • Power — keeps nodes alive for years without maintenance. Key parts: coin cells and thin-film batteries, indoor photovoltaic, thermal and radio-frequency energy harvesting, Power-over-Ethernet.
  • Building integration and control — ties the mesh to heating, lighting and access systems, and to a digital twin of the building. Key parts: building-management-system bridges, data model and naming standards, consent and access rules.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the interfaces. Agree on data models and radio standards first, so sensors from different makers can join one mesh; without this, the building becomes a set of islands.
  2. Make the sensor nodes. Silicon chips with integrated radar or MEMS sensing elements are fabricated, packaged into millimetre-scale modules and tested in volume.
  3. Embed them in materials. Nodes, antennas and conductive traces are laminated into ceiling tiles, wall panels, flooring and glass during manufacture, or printed onto flexible substrates that are glued behind finishes.
  4. Provide power and backhaul. Each panel gets either a wired Power-over-Ethernet drop or a harvester plus battery sized for a ten-year life; mesh nodes relay to a few wired gateways.
  5. Install edge compute. Small servers placed in service cupboards run the inference models and the middleware that turns raw signals into events such as 'room occupied' or 'filter due'.
  6. Integrate the output layer. Projectors, waveguide glass and directional speakers are aligned with the surfaces they serve; light-field or true volumetric displays would be built as an array of phase modulators behind the glass — a step that remains laboratory work.
  7. Calibrate each space. Every room is walked, measured and tuned so it knows its own geometry, noise floor and normal occupancy pattern.
  8. Operate and update. The mesh runs alongside the building-management system, with models retrained as furniture, partitions and usage change over the building's life.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Low-cost MEMS sensors and radar-on-chip — the mesh needs thousands of cheap, tiny, low-power detectors rather than a few expensive instruments.
  • EXISTS Low-power wireless mesh networking — nodes must self-form a network and relay through each other without per-device cabling.
  • PARTIAL Edge AI accelerators — inference must run inside the room at a few watts, not in a distant data centre.
  • PARTIAL Energy harvesting and battery-free electronics — maintenance cost, not hardware cost, kills large sensor deployments.
  • NOT YET Light-field and waveguide projection — free-space images that many people can see from any angle in a lit room do not yet exist outside the laboratory.
  • PARTIAL Privacy-preserving sensing and consent — a mesh that senses everyone continuously is only acceptable if it cannot be turned into surveillance.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: smart buildings with networked sensors, occupancy-driven lighting and heating, and voice interfaces in the home — the sensing and control groundwork, in partial form.
  2. Next: standardised, interoperable mesh at building scale, with most processing on-device — retrofits that work across vendors, and cheaper energy use.
  3. Next: displays and controls woven into glass and wall panels in commercial pilot buildings — interfaces that appear where they are needed instead of on a carried screen.
  4. Longer term: true volumetric or light-field output embedded in building materials, with personalised services delivered per person — the screenless environment the concept describes — contingent on the display and privacy problems being solved.
