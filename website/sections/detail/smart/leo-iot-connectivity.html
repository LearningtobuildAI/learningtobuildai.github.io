PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Direct-to-Device Satellite IoT is a system where small satellites in Low-Earth Orbit (LEO) communicate directly with ground-based sensors or mobile phones, enabling data transmission without the need for intermediate ground stations.

Category

2Problem It Solves

Addressing the limitations of traditional satellite communication systems by reducing dependency on ground infrastructure, thus enhancing coverage in remote locations and providing reliable data transmission for applications like asset tracking and environmental monitoring.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Satellites in LEO orbit at altitudes between 160 and 2,000 kilometers, providing a wide coverage area. They relay short, low-power messages from unmodified or lightly modified devices to other satellites or directly to Earth-based receivers, extending connectivity to remote areas such as oceans, deserts, and rural regions.

5Materials Used
  • low-power communication chips
  • solar panels
  • antennas
  • microcontrollers
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves designing and building small satellites (CubeSats or smaller) capable of handling low-power communications. Components such as solar panels, batteries, communication antennas, and microcontrollers are assembled to ensure robust performance in space conditions.

7Build Process

Satellites undergo rigorous testing for thermal, vibration, and electromagnetic compatibility before launch. The build process includes component selection, assembly, integration, and final testing to meet the stringent requirements of LEO operations.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Satellite payload — receives very faint signals from small, low-power devices and relays or processes them. Key parts: large deployable phased-array antenna, software-defined radio, digital beamforming processor, regenerative or bent-pipe repeater.
  • Spacecraft bus and constellation — keeps the payload pointed and supplies power across a fleet of satellites. Key parts: solar arrays and batteries, electric or chemical propulsion, attitude control and star trackers, orbital planes in low Earth orbit.
  • Ground segment — connects the satellites to terrestrial networks. Key parts: gateway earth stations, feeder-link antennas, telemetry and control centre.
  • Device and radio link — the sensor or handset at the far end of a very long, very lossy path. Key parts: narrowband modem (NB-IoT NTN or proprietary), compact antenna, battery or energy harvester.
  • Core network and service layer — makes satellites look like a normal mobile network to apps and operators. Key parts: 5G NTN core integration, roaming and billing, device and data management platform.
  • Spectrum and regulatory layer — lets satellites and terrestrial networks share scarce frequencies. Key parts: mobile-satellite service allocations, national licensing and coordination.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Plan the link budget. Engineers calculate whether a milliwatt transmitter in a sensor can be heard from 500–2,000 km away, which sets antenna size, altitude and data rate — usually just a few hundred bits per second per device.
  2. Design the payload. A large flat phased-array antenna is built from many small radiating elements so the satellite can form and steer multiple narrow beams and track fast-moving ground devices.
  3. Build and qualify the spacecraft. The bus and payload are integrated and shaken, baked in thermal vacuum and blasted with radio noise to prove they survive launch and the space environment.
  4. Mass-produce the fleet. Satellites are assembled on production lines in flat, stackable form so dozens can launch on one rocket, driving unit cost down.
  5. Launch and raise orbit. Rockets deploy the satellites into several orbital planes; propulsion spreads them out so coverage is continuous.
  6. Deploy gateways and core network. Ground stations are built where licensed spectrum and fibre backhaul are available, then connected into a mobile core.
  7. Integrate devices and handset firmware. Modems adopt the satellite standard and are tuned to pre-compensate for Doppler shift and the long round-trip delay.
  8. Trial, then commercialise. Field trials with asset trackers, agricultural sensors and emergency-messaging handsets verify coverage before paid service begins.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS 5G non-terrestrial-network standards — 3GPP Release 17 defined how satellites and terrestrial networks share a common air interface, so chipsets can serve both.
  • EXISTS Low-cost satellite manufacturing — hundreds of satellites are needed for continuous coverage, which only works if each one costs far less than a traditional telecom satellite.
  • PARTIAL Large deployable phased-array antennas — the antenna gain is what closes the link to a phone or sensor; large arrays are demonstrated but still being scaled.
  • EXISTS Cheap, frequent launches — constellations must be replenished; rideshare and reusable rockets make that affordable.
  • EXISTS Narrowband IoT chipsets — terrestrial NB-IoT and LoRa silicon is the base that satellite variants build on.
  • PARTIAL Spectrum access and landing rights — a satellite footprint crosses many countries, and each national regulator must permit the service.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: established narrowband satellite messaging services alongside pilot direct-to-phone systems — tracking of ships, livestock, containers and pipelines where no cellular coverage exists.
  2. Next: commercial direct-to-handset messaging and emergency SOS from ordinary phones — coverage in dead zones for consumers and field workers without special hardware.
  3. Next: direct-to-device voice and low-rate data at scale through dedicated satellite fleets — a second path to connectivity that bypasses local towers entirely.
  4. Longer term: satellite and terrestrial networks merged into one seamless core — devices that switch between tower and satellite without the user noticing, and truly global low-power sensing.
