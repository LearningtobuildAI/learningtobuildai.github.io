PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Airframe — carries the package and stays airborne — either a multirotor that hovers or a fixed-wing craft that cruises. Key parts: carbon-fibre composite body, fixed-wing or multirotor configuration, motors and propellers, lithium battery pack, parachute or emergency descent system, skids or winch for package release.
  • Flight control — flies and stabilises the aircraft with no pilot on board, and fails safely if something breaks. Key parts: redundant flight computers, inertial measurement units, GNSS receivers, barometer and pitot tube, flight-termination system.
  • Detect-and-avoid — spots other aircraft, terrain, power lines and people, then steers clear. Key parts: LiDAR, radar, cameras, ADS-B receiver, sensor-fusion processor.
  • Ground infrastructure — launches, recovers, recharges and hands packages over without a person standing outside. Key parts: launch and recovery pad, winch or tether for lowering packages, battery charging bay, package magazine, weather mast.
  • Command and control links — keeps each aircraft in contact with remote pilots and the fleet system. Key parts: cellular modem, satellite backup link, radio line-of-sight link, remote ID transmitter.
  • Fleet orchestration and airspace management — assigns routes, keeps aircraft apart and fits them into managed airspace. Key parts: cloud dispatch, geofence database, uncrewed traffic management (UTM) interface, operations dashboard.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design and mould the airframe. Carbon-fibre cloth is laid into moulds and cured in an autoclave to make light, stiff bodies and wings.
  2. Assemble the powertrain. Motors, electronic speed controllers and battery packs are fitted and bench-tested for thrust and endurance.
  3. Install avionics. Redundant flight computers, inertial sensors, GNSS receivers and the flight-termination system are mounted and wired.
  4. Calibrate the perception suite. LiDAR, radar and cameras are aligned and their outputs fused so the aircraft can tell a bird from a parachute from a power line.
  5. Write and simulate the autonomy stack. Route planning, geofencing, weather limits and contingency behaviour are tested for thousands of hours in software simulation before flight.
  6. Build the ground stations. Launch pads, chargers, package magazines and the winch mechanism are installed and connected to the dispatch cloud.
  7. Fly the test campaign. Flights gradually expand the envelope: hover, cruise, wind limits, sensor-failure cases and parachute deployment.
  8. Certify with the aviation regulator. The operator obtains an airworthiness approval and an operating certificate that permits flights beyond visual line of sight.
  9. Operate and maintain. Remote pilots supervise several aircraft each, while a maintenance loop replaces batteries, motors and propellers on a schedule.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High energy-density lithium batteries — endurance is set by battery mass; today's cells limit most craft to tens of kilometres of range.
  • EXISTS GNSS and inertial navigation — aircraft need reliable position and attitude without a pilot looking out of a window.
  • PARTIAL Detect-and-avoid sensors and sensor fusion — small, light and cheap enough sensors that reliably see other traffic are still maturing.
  • PARTIAL Uncrewed traffic management and remote ID — many aircraft sharing airspace need digital traffic coordination, not radio calls.
  • PARTIAL Beyond-visual-line-of-sight regulation — routine long-range flight is only legal where regulators have written and granted the rules.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: medical delivery along fixed corridors, such as blood and vaccine flights in Rwanda from 2016 — proof that routine autonomous flight can be safe and useful.
  2. Next: suburban parcel delivery in approved corridors, with one operator managing many aircraft — a viable cost per delivery for small, urgent packages.
  3. Next: shared traffic management networks letting several operators fly the same airspace — denser networks and longer routes without new bespoke regulation for each one.
  4. Longer term: dense urban networks with heavier payloads — routine aerial delivery integrated with crewed aviation and ground logistics.
