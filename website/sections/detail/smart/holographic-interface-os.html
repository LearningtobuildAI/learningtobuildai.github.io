PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Voxel medium — the physical thing in mid-air that scatters or emits light so a viewer sees a point of image. Key parts: ionised air (plasma) from a focused pulsed laser, optically trapped particle steered in 3D, ultrasonically levitated bead, near-eye retinal projection (virtual image in the eye instead of in the room).
  • Emitter and scanner — places each voxel at the right coordinate thousands of times per second. Key parts: pulsed or RGB lasers, scanning mirrors or spatial light modulators, phased array of ultrasonic transducers, trap-beam optics.
  • Spatial sensing — knows where the user's head, hands and the room's surfaces are. Key parts: depth cameras, inertial sensors, hand and eye tracking, room mapping.
  • Spatial audio — gives interface elements a position you can hear. Key parts: head-tracked binaural renderer, small speaker array or earbuds.
  • Compute and render stack — turns an application's 3D scene into a timed stream of voxel coordinates. Key parts: volumetric renderer, voxel scheduler and latency budget, GPU, calibration data.
  • Holographic OS layer — the actual operating system: window manager for volumes, input model, permissions, app framework. Key parts: 3D compositor, spatial anchors, gesture and voice input, developer APIs.
  • Safety envelope — keeps lasers and ultrasound from injuring eyes, skin or lungs, and manages exhaust gases. Key parts: laser-safety interlocks and beam enclosures, ultrasound exposure limits, ozone and nitrogen-oxide handling, field-of-view keep-out zones.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the voxel mechanism. Decide how light will exist in empty space. Options in the lab are laser-induced plasma, a laser-trapped particle, an ultrasonically levitated bead, or projection straight into the viewer's eye. Each choice fixes the rest of the design.
  2. Build the emitter array. Assemble and align lasers with scanning optics, or an ultrasonic phased array with a trap beam, so voxels can be addressed in three dimensions across the intended volume.
  3. Solve safety and containment. Focused laser pulses must be eye-safe and enclosed in a managed field of view; plasma in air makes ozone and nitrogen oxides that need ventilation; high-intensity ultrasound must stay within heating limits. This step, not the optics, is what decides whether the device can sit in a home.
  4. Make the render pipeline. Write a volumetric renderer and a scheduler that fires every voxel in the right order. Like a CRT, the image relies on persistence: the whole volume must be redrawn faster than the eye can follow, so brightness and complexity trade off directly against refresh rate.
  5. Add tracking and audio. Depth cameras and inertial sensors track head and hands so the interface is anchored to real space; a head-tracked binaural renderer gives each element a sound position.
  6. Write the OS layer. Build a compositor that manages 3D regions the way a desktop manages windows, plus a spatial input model, an app framework, permissions for cameras and microphones, and a way for apps to declare how much of the volume they occupy.
  7. Calibrate and stress-test. Optical and geometric calibration keeps voxels where the renderer thinks they are as the hardware warms and ages; latency, occlusion and multi-user viewing are measured and iterated.
  8. Productise. Shrink power, cooling and optics into something affordable and quiet, and decide the honest target: a controlled desktop volume, a near-eye display that merely looks free-space, or a whole room.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Free-space voxel generation bright enough to see in daylight — plasma and trapped-particle displays demonstrated so far are dim, tiny and monochrome-ish; nothing yet scales to a room-sized, daylight-readable volume.
  • EXISTS Real-time volumetric rendering and low-latency tracking — modern headsets already render stereo 3D with head tracking at low latency, and that pipeline is reusable.
  • PARTIAL Spatial operating systems — headset operating systems with 3D window managers and hand tracking exist, but only behind a display, not in open air.
  • EXISTS Eye-safe laser and ultrasound exposure frameworks — laser and ultrasound safety standards exist and would constrain brightness, range and refresh rate for any free-space display.
  • NOT YET Occlusion and multi-viewer support — a volume of light has no solid surfaces to block what is behind it, so depth cues collapse for anyone but a single tracked viewer.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: spatial computing on headset displays — proven 3D window management, hand tracking and app ecosystems — the software half of a holographic OS.
  2. Next: near-eye light-field and holographic displays that remove bulky optics — thin, comfortable devices with convincing depth, still worn rather than floating in the room.
  3. Next: lab-scale free-space volumetric displays of useful brightness and size — single-user interfaces that genuinely exist in mid-air inside a desk-sized safe volume.
  4. Longer term: room-scale, multi-user, daylight-visible volumetric display with safe power and ventilation — a true holographic operating system, replacing screens rather than supplementing them.
