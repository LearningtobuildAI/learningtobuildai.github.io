PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Precision fermentation at city scale is a method of producing high-value chemicals and pharmaceuticals using genetically engineered microorganisms in large-scale urban facilities.

2Problem It Solves

Traditional manufacturing methods for high-value chemicals and pharmaceuticals often involve complex processes with higher costs and greater environmental footprints. Precision fermentation offers a more sustainable and cost-effective alternative by leveraging the efficiency of biotechnology in urban settings.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Advanced biotechnology, including genetic engineering and cell culture techniques, are used to cultivate microorganisms that produce desired compounds. These organisms are grown in bioreactors within urban facilities, which can be located close to end-users, reducing transportation costs and environmental impact.

5Materials Used
  • genetically engineered microorganisms
  • bioreactors
  • growth media
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Urban facilities are used to house large-scale bioreactors, which can be integrated into existing infrastructure or built as standalone structures within cities. These facilities require controlled environments for optimal microbial growth conditions.

7Build Process

The process involves designing and constructing the facility, sourcing and engineering microorganisms, setting up bioreactor systems, and implementing quality control measures to ensure consistent product output.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Production strain — a microbe engineered to make the target molecule instead of its own products. Key parts: yeast, bacterium or filamentous fungus host, synthetic gene pathway, selection markers and promoters.
  • Seed train — grows a tiny frozen vial up to a volume big enough to inoculate the main vessel without contamination. Key parts: cryovials, shake flasks, lab and pilot seed fermenters, sterile transfer lines.
  • Bioreactor — the vessel where the cells grow and the product accumulates. Key parts: stirred-tank or airlift vessel (single-use bag or stainless steel), agitator and sparger, jacket for heating and cooling, pH, dissolved-oxygen and temperature probes.
  • Feed and media preparation — supplies sugar, nitrogen, minerals and vitamins, sterile and at controlled rates. Key parts: media mixing tanks, steriliser or filtration skid, feed pumps, antifoam dosing.
  • Downstream processing — separates, concentrates and purifies the product to the grade the customer needs. Key parts: centrifuge or membrane separator, cell disruption (if the product is intracellular), chromatography columns, crystalliser or spray dryer.
  • Utilities and clean-in-place — steam, chilled water, sterile air and caustic that keep a biological plant running and clean. Key parts: boiler and chiller plant, oil-free air compressor and sterile filters, CIP/SIP skid, wastewater treatment.
  • Process control and analytics — holds the fermentation at its optimum and catches contamination or drift early. Key parts: SCADA and historians, online spectroscopy (e.g. Raman), soft sensors, batch records.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose and engineer the strain. Genes for the target pathway are inserted into a well-understood host, then the strain is evolved or screened for higher yield and fewer by-products.
  2. Design the process. Bench and pilot runs fix the medium recipe, temperature, pH, feed profile and run length needed to hit the target titre in grams per litre.
  3. Build the facility. Vessels, piping, utilities and clean rooms are installed; for an urban site the building must also handle steam, odour, noise and wastewater.
  4. Prove sterility. The whole train is steamed or chemically sanitised, then run on sterile medium to confirm no contaminating organism survives.
  5. Scale up the seed train. Cells are stepped up through progressively larger sterile vessels until there is enough inoculum for production scale — often hundreds of litres.
  6. Run the fermentation. The vessel runs in fed-batch or continuous mode for days, with feed, aeration and cooling adjusted continuously to keep the cells productive.
  7. Harvest and purify. Cells and broth are separated, the product is captured and polished by filtration or chromatography, then dried or formulated.
  8. Quality release. Every batch is tested against specification — sterility, identity, potency and purity — before it leaves the site under GMP or food-safety rules.
  9. Recover and recycle. Spent broth, heat and carbon dioxide are treated or reused, which is what makes an urban site acceptable to its neighbours.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Recombinant DNA and synthetic biology — without a reliable way to give a microbe a new pathway there is no product to make.
  • EXISTS Industrial aseptic fermentation — antibiotics and industrial enzymes have been made this way for decades; the skill base exists.
  • PARTIAL Cheap low-carbon sugar or gas feedstock — feedstock is a large share of cost; urban plants need local supplies such as food-processing sidestreams.
  • PARTIAL Scalable purification — chromatography is expensive and hard to scale; commodity products need cheaper separation methods.
  • PARTIAL Process analytical technology — real-time measurement of a living culture is still limited, so batches are run conservatively.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory approval for novel products — medicines have a clear path, but novel foods and chemicals face differing national approvals.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pharmaceuticals and speciality enzymes made by fermentation in large industrial plants — a proven technical and regulatory base, though usually outside cities.
  2. Next: modular urban plants colocated with food waste, breweries or sugar refineries and using waste heat — shorter supply chains and lower transport emissions for high-value ingredients.
  3. Next: commodity-scale fermentation of fats, proteins and chemicals at costs near conventional routes — displacement of land-heavy agriculture and petrochemical feedstocks.
  4. Longer term: city biorefineries sharing heat, gas and nutrients with the surrounding grid and wastewater system — a circular urban manufacturing base for materials and food ingredients.
