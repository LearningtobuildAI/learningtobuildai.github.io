9Engineering Breakdown
  • Geospatial data spine — assembles the machine-readable picture of the place being simulated. Key parts: satellite and aerial imagery, LiDAR building and terrain models, cadastral and land-use records, transport, power and water network maps.
  • Environment model — represents the physical world agents act inside. Key parts: GIS and routing engine, network models for power, water and transport, land-use and housing stock model.
  • Synthetic population — a disaggregated set of households, firms and institutions, not an average citizen. Key parts: census and survey microdata, statistical reweighting to published totals, persona and attribute assignment.
  • Behaviour engine — decides what each agent does when the rules change. Key parts: rule-based decision modules, large language model prompted with persona and constraints, learning or adaptation over simulated time.
  • Interaction and scheduling — runs millions of agent-to-agent and agent-to-environment events per simulated day. Key parts: event scheduler, message passing between agents, distributed compute cluster.
  • Policy interface — lets an analyst express a proposed law as machine-readable parameters. Key parts: rule specification format, scenario configuration, versioned run history.
  • Validation and governance layer — keeps the output honest and lawful. Key parts: backtesting against historical outcomes, sensitivity and uncertainty analysis, privacy controls and bias audit.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the question. Decide what decision the simulation must inform and which outcomes count as success, before any code is written.
  2. Build the geospatial base. Fuse remote sensing, cadastral records and utility maps into one consistent three-dimensional model of buildings, parcels, roads and networks.
  3. Generate a synthetic population. Create individual households and firms with attributes drawn from census microdata, reweighted until district-level totals match published statistics.
  4. Encode behaviour. Write decision rules for each agent type; where language models are used, prompt them with a persona, an income, a location and the constraints of the proposed rule, and check their choices against observed behaviour.
  5. Calibrate and backtest. Replay a policy that already happened, or a shock such as a fuel price rise, and tune the model until it reproduces the real outcome within stated error bars.
  6. Run scenarios. Apply the proposed rule as a parameter change and run many randomised replicates, producing distributions of outcomes rather than a single headline number.
  7. Audit and report. Test whether the model penalises particular groups, quantify how much the answer depends on contested assumptions, and publish uncertainty ranges a non-modeller can read.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Agent-based modelling and microsimulation — long-established methods for simulating many interacting decision-makers, already used in tax, transport and epidemic planning.
  • PARTIAL City-scale digital twins with high-resolution data — national and municipal twins exist as pilots and platforms, but coverage and currency vary widely.
  • PARTIAL Large language models as behavioural proxies — demonstrated in research that they can approximate survey responses, but they carry known biases and drift from real populations.
  • EXISTS Computing for million-agent runs — cloud clusters make large ensemble runs feasible, though cost scales with the number of replicates needed for credible uncertainty.
  • PARTIAL Validation data and causal inference methods — without reliable evidence on how real people responded to past rules, a simulation is unfalsifiable.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: digital twins of cities and regions used for transport, energy and environmental planning — a tested geospatial and network backbone that policy models can stand on.
  2. Next: synthetic populations coupled to language-model agents, calibrated to surveys and elections — rapid pre-testing of proposed rules across many assumed behaviours.
  3. Next: regulators formally accepting ensemble policy simulations as one input to impact assessment — fewer unintended consequences, provided uncertainty is reported rather than hidden.
  4. Longer term: audited, contestable twins that citizens and researchers can inspect and challenge — legitimacy for decisions informed by simulation, without pretending the model is the public.
