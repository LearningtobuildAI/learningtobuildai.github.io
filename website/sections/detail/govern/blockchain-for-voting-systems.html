4How It Works

By implementing a distributed network where each participant has a copy of the transaction history, blockchain ensures that once data (votes) are recorded, they cannot be altered or deleted without consensus from the majority of participants. This is achieved through cryptographic hashes, which link blocks in the chain and create an immutable record.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves developing smart contracts to automate certain aspects of the voting process, coding these into a blockchain platform, and integrating with existing voter databases or identity verification systems.

7Build Process

Developing a blockchain-based voting system requires extensive testing in simulated environments, followed by rigorous security audits. Pilot tests are often conducted before full-scale deployment to ensure reliability and address any unforeseen issues.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Eligibility and enrolment — decides who is allowed to vote and issues each voter a one-time credential. Key parts: electoral roll database, identity verification, voter key or token issuance.
  • Ballot client — the device software that presents the ballot and encrypts the choice before anything leaves the voter's hands. Key parts: phone or kiosk app, ballot-marking interface, local vote encryption, verifiable receipt generator.
  • Cryptographic protocol — lets votes be counted without revealing individual choices. Key parts: homomorphic tallying or mix-net, zero-knowledge proofs of ballot validity, threshold key sharing.
  • Distributed ledger — an append-only, tamper-evident record of cast ballots, replicated across independent organisations. Key parts: permissioned validator nodes, consensus rules, transaction log.
  • Public bulletin board — a place where voters and observers can check that their ballot was recorded and included in the tally. Key parts: hash-anchored publication, verifier tools, audit logs.
  • Tally and audit — turns the sealed record into a published result with proofs. Key parts: tallying authorities, risk-limiting or cryptographic audits, result publication.
  • Key management — protects the keys that decrypt the result; whoever holds them could break secrecy. Key parts: hardware security modules, key ceremony procedures, threshold custodians.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the trust model. Decide who runs the nodes, who holds decryption keys and what the system must survive — a corrupt official, a hacked phone, or a lying vendor.
  2. Build the electoral roll and credentials. Link each eligible voter to a single-use credential without storing the link between identity and vote on the ledger.
  3. Build the ballot client. Write software that encrypts the vote on the voter's own device and produces a receipt the voter can later check, and harden it against malware and screen capture.
  4. Design the counting cryptography. Choose either homomorphic encryption, where encrypted votes are added without decrypting them, or a mix-net that shuffles and re-encrypts ballots; add proofs that every recorded ballot is well-formed.
  5. Deploy the ledger. Stand up validator nodes at separate organisations, such as electoral commissions, parties and auditors, and write each cast ballot as a transaction with the voter's proof of eligibility.
  6. Publish a verifiable record. Publish the ballot set, or a hash of it, so anyone can independently verify that the tally matches the recorded votes.
  7. Tally and prove. Combine or decrypt the sealed votes under threshold keys, publish the proofs, and run audits that detect any mismatch.
  8. Operate and rehearse. Run key ceremonies, penetration tests and full failed-election rehearsals, because a voting system must degrade to a paper fallback, not to no result.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL End-to-end verifiable voting cryptography — mix-nets, homomorphic tallying and zero-knowledge proofs exist and have been used in small elections, but not at national scale.
  • PARTIAL Verifiable digital identity — the ledger is only as trustworthy as the enrolment step that stops one person voting twice.
  • NOT YET Malware-resistant voter devices — a phone or home computer can silently alter a vote before it is encrypted, and no ledger can detect this.
  • NOT YET Coercion resistance — remote voting from home cannot prevent a vote being bought or cast under pressure, which is why polling booths exist.
  • PARTIAL High-throughput permissioned consensus — election-night volumes and simultaneous national access stress systems that are proven mainly in small pilots.
  • EXISTS Paper trail and risk-limiting audits — statistical audits of paper ballots catch errors that a purely digital record cannot.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: blockchain-anchored records alongside paper voting, plus pilots for low-risk groups such as overseas or military voters and corporate proxy voting — tamper-evident audit trails and real-world test data without risking a general election.
  2. Next: end-to-end verifiable internet voting with in-person enrolment and single-use credentials, used in binding municipal or organisational elections — voters can check their own ballot was counted, while paper remains the legal record.
  3. Next: coercion-resistant remote voting designs tested in real elections — remote voting that does not create new ways to pressure voters.
  4. Longer term: national general elections counted partly on distributed ledgers with independent node operators — transparent, observer-verifiable tallies — but only if device security, identity and privacy problems are solved first, and blockchain by itself solves none of them.
