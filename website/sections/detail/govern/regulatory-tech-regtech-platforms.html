PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Regulatory Tech (RegTech) platforms are software solutions designed to help organizations meet regulatory requirements efficiently. These platforms leverage technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning (ML), and blockchain to automate compliance processes, enhance transparency, and reduce the risk of non-compliance.

2Problem It Solves

RegTech platforms address the challenges of manual compliance reporting, which are time-consuming, error-prone, and costly. They help organizations in financial services meet regulatory requirements more efficiently while maintaining transparency and accountability.

4How It Works

RegTech platforms typically use AI for data analysis and pattern recognition to ensure that financial transactions comply with regulatory standards. Blockchain technology is employed to create immutable records, providing a transparent audit trail. These systems can integrate with existing enterprise resource planning (ERP) and other business management software to streamline processes.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Data ingestion and entity resolution — pulls transaction, trading, client and ownership data out of core systems and stitches it together. Key parts: connectors to core banking, trading and payment systems, ISO 20022 message parsing, Legal Entity Identifier (LEI) mapping and deduplication, data quality checks.
  • Obligation and rules engine — turns regulation into machine-checkable logic. Key parts: obligation taxonomy, decision tables and rule sets, version control and change history, test cases per rule.
  • Regulatory change monitoring — watches regulators' publications and flags what actually changed. Key parts: document crawlers, natural-language processing and text diffing, impact mapping to internal controls, analyst review queue.
  • Detection and screening analytics — finds suspicious patterns rather than just checking boxes. Key parts: transaction-monitoring models, sanctions and watchlist fuzzy matching, graph analytics over ownership and payment networks, alert scoring and prioritisation.
  • Reporting and submission — assembles and files the reports regulators require. Key parts: report templates, XBRL and ISO 20022 generators, validation and reconciliation checks, secure submission gateways.
  • Case management and workflow — humans review, escalate and sign off on what the machine flags. Key parts: alert queues, evidence attachments, escalation and filing workflows.
  • Audit trail and explainability — lets an examiner reconstruct exactly why a decision was made. Key parts: tamper-evident logs, model versioning, reason codes for every automated decision.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Map the obligations. Legal analysts and engineers read the applicable rules and break them into discrete, testable obligations with owners and evidence requirements.
  2. Model the data. Decide which fields each obligation needs, then build connectors that extract and normalise them — including reference data such as legal entity identifiers.
  3. Write the rules as code. Obligations become decision tables and rule sets in a versioned repository, each backed by test cases drawn from real scenarios.
  4. Train and tune the detection models. Anomaly and screening models are fitted on historical data, then thresholds are set to balance missed cases against alert volume, and results are back-tested.
  5. Build the reporting pipeline. Report templates, validation rules and submission interfaces are implemented against the regulator's published schema and tested end to end.
  6. Instrument the audit trail. Logging is added so every flag, score and override is recorded with its inputs, model version and reason code.
  7. Integrate and harden. APIs connect the platform to core systems; access control, encryption and monitoring are applied, since the data is among the most sensitive a firm holds.
  8. Validate and gain acceptance. Internal audit and, often, the supervisor review the logic and evidence before the platform is relied on for live filings.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Common identifiers and reporting formats — the Legal Entity Identifier, ISO 20022 messaging and XBRL tagging give machines something unambiguous to join and file on.
  • EXISTS Cloud data platforms and APIs — compliance workloads need elastic compute and clean interfaces into legacy core systems.
  • PARTIAL Natural-language processing of legal text — extracting obligations from dense regulatory prose is doable but still needs human confirmation.
  • PARTIAL Explainable machine learning and model risk management — a model that cannot justify its alerts will not survive supervisory scrutiny.
  • PARTIAL Privacy-enhancing computation — sharing risk signals across institutions without pooling raw customer data needs techniques like secure aggregation or zero-knowledge proofs.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: cloud-based platforms automating reporting, sanctions screening and transaction monitoring — lower compliance cost per client and faster filing cycles.
  2. Next: machine-readable regulation, with rules published in structured form and adopted directly by firms — less manual translation between legal text and code, and fewer interpretation gaps.
  3. Next: supervisory technology using the same data feeds, moving from periodic reports to near-continuous oversight — problems surfaced earlier and smaller remediation bills.
  4. Longer term: cross-border data sharing under agreed privacy safeguards, with cryptographic proofs instead of raw record exchange — detection of flows that cross jurisdictions and currently fall between regulators.

18Glossary
RegTech
Short for Regulatory Technology, it refers to software solutions that automate regulatory compliance processes in financial services and other industries.
AI (Artificial Intelligence)
A branch of computer science that focuses on creating systems capable of performing tasks requiring human-like intelligence, such as data analysis and pattern recognition.
Blockchain
A decentralized digital ledger technology that provides a secure, transparent, and immutable record-keeping system for transactions or other activities.
