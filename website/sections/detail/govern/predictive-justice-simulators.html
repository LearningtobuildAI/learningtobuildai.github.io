9Engineering Breakdown
  • Population data layer — represents people, households and firms as individual agents rather than averages. Key parts: census and survey microdata, tax, court and prison administrative records, privacy-preserving record linkage.
  • Behavioural model — decides how each agent responds to a change in the law. Key parts: estimated response elasticities, rule-based decision rules, machine-learning or language-model behaviour predictors.
  • Legal-institution model — encodes the law itself as executable rules, including eligibility, penalties and process. Key parts: rule engine for statutes and thresholds, court and policing workflow timings, agency budget constraints.
  • Simulation engine — runs millions of agents over simulated years and many scenarios. Key parts: agent scheduler, parallel compute cluster, scenario and parameter sweep manager.
  • Calibration and validation layer — tests whether the model reproduces outcomes that already happened. Key parts: backtesting against past reforms, sensitivity analysis, uncertainty quantification.
  • Audit and interface layer — makes results inspectable and contestable rather than oracular. Key parts: scenario dashboard, provenance and version logs, independent expert review.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the question and the outcomes. Decide precisely which legal change is being tested and what counts as success — crime rate, prison population, cost, or distributional effect — before any code is written.
  2. Assemble microdata. Link anonymised census, tax, court and corrections records into a representative synthetic population, using privacy-preserving matching and legal agreements.
  3. Estimate behavioural responses. Use natural experiments from past law changes — where one jurisdiction changed a rule and a neighbouring one did not — to measure how people actually responded, rather than assuming it.
  4. Encode the law as rules. Translate statutes, sentencing guidelines and agency procedures into an executable rule set, then run it on the current law to check it matches the present system.
  5. Calibrate against history. Tune the model until it reproduces observed outcomes for a period it has never seen. A model that cannot retrodict cannot be trusted to predict.
  6. Run counterfactual scenarios. Simulate the proposed change and several plausible alternatives, reporting ranges and tail risks instead of a single headline number.
  7. Adversarially test. Have independent teams try to break the model — gaming, feedback loops, missing populations, and the risk that the model encodes past bias.
  8. Publish with open assumptions. Release the assumptions, code and uncertainty intervals alongside the result, then monitor the real reform to score the prediction.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Linked administrative microdata at population scale — a few countries with national registers can do this; most legal systems have fragmented, incompatible records.
  • EXISTS Causal inference from natural experiments — without measured responses to real past reforms, agent behaviour is guesswork and the simulation is only a spreadsheet.
  • EXISTS Large-scale microsimulation of policy — static tax-benefit microsimulation is already routine in many finance and social ministries; the missing part is behavioural feedback.
  • NOT YET Validated models of social behaviour — unlike physics, societies cannot be put in controlled experiments, so behavioural rules remain the weakest link.
  • PARTIAL Privacy-preserving data linkage and governance — simulating individuals requires access to individual-level data, which carries serious legal and ethical constraints.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: microsimulation models score the budget and distributional effects of proposed tax and benefit changes, and risk scores inform some sentencing and parole decisions — evidence before votes and hearings, on a narrow set of measurable outcomes.
  2. Next: agent-based models that add behavioural response and feedback — how enforcement changes reporting, how sentencing changes plea behaviour — tests of reforms whose main effect runs through people changing what they do.
  3. Next: sandboxed pilots where a legislature simulates a bill before voting and commits to publishing uncertainty estimates — a formal, auditable role for simulation in lawmaking rather than ad hoc consultancy.
  4. Longer term: a continuously updated digital twin of a justice system, scored against real outcomes every year — ongoing monitoring of whether a law worked — and the ability to correct it early.
