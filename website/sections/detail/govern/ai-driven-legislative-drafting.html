PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Corpus pipeline — turns statutes into structured, versioned machine-readable text. Key parts: XML/HTML schemas such as Akoma Ntoso, PDF and gazette parsers, commencement and amendment metadata.
  • Citation and amendment graph — models every provision as a node linked to what it references, amends or repeals. Key parts: provision-level graph database, temporal versioning, cross-reference resolver.
  • Norm formalisation layer — restates selected clauses as rules: who must, may or must not do what, and when. Key parts: deontic logic or rule templates, standards such as OASIS LegalRuleML, domain glossaries.
  • Conflict engine — flags contradictions, duplications and gaps between a draft and existing law. Key parts: retrieval over the corpus, satisfiability/logic solvers, similarity and entailment scoring.
  • Drafting assistant — proposes clause wording and explains each flag in plain language. Key parts: large language model with retrieval augmentation, citation generator, drafting-convention style guide.
  • Review and audit layer — keeps a named human accountable and records why each suggestion was accepted or rejected. Key parts: redline interface, decision log, access controls.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Digitise the statute book. Convert the body of law into a structured format that records each section, its in-force dates and its amendment history.
  2. Build the reference graph. Parse every cross-reference, amendment and repeal so the system knows which provisions depend on which others.
  3. Formalise a narrow domain first. Encode obligations, permissions and prohibitions into machine-readable rules for one tractable area, such as benefit eligibility, rather than the whole code.
  4. Assemble the retrieval layer. Index the corpus so that when a new clause is drafted, the most relevant existing provisions and precedents are surfaced with citations.
  5. Train and prompt the model. Adapt a language model to legislative vocabulary and drafting conventions, testing it on past amendments whose conflicts were found later.
  6. Run conflict checks. For each draft clause, combine textual overlap search, semantic contradiction detection and formal satisfiability testing on the formalised rules.
  7. Integrate into the drafting editor. Embed the checks in the tool drafters already use, so warnings appear inline with links to the offending provisions.
  8. Calibrate against expert review. Measure false positives and false negatives using committees of lawyers, and tune thresholds so alerts stay trustworthy.
  9. Deploy with human sign-off. Drafters remain responsible; the system logs every suggestion, its reasoning and the decision taken.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Machine-readable, versioned legislation — some jurisdictions publish structured, dated statute books, but many still publish only PDFs or scanned gazettes.
  • PARTIAL Legal knowledge representation standards — schemas for rules and norms exist and have been trialled, but coverage of real codes is thin.
  • EXISTS Long-context retrieval over large corpora — finding the relevant few provisions among hundreds of thousands is the core enabling capability.
  • PARTIAL Automated reasoning about norms — detecting genuine logical conflict, not just similarity, remains research-grade outside narrow domains.
  • NOT YET Legal evaluation benchmarks — without agreed tests, claims of accurate conflict detection cannot be verified.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: AI assistants that summarise, search and suggest wording inside legislative editing tools — faster research and drafting, with alerts limited to obvious duplicate or missing references.
  2. Next: rules-as-code pilots in a few narrow domains, with published formalisations — automatic checks of eligibility and procedural logic against the source statutes.
  3. Next: graph-backed consistency checking across a whole jurisdiction's in-force law — reviewers see contradictions and orphaned provisions before a bill is tabled.
  4. Longer term: cross-jurisdiction and treaty-level checking, with auditable reasoning — fewer conflicts between national and supranational law — provided accountability stays with human drafters.
