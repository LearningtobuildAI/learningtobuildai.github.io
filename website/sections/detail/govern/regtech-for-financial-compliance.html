PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Regulatory technology (RegTech) for financial compliance is a set of tools and services designed to help financial institutions meet regulatory requirements and manage associated risks by leveraging automation and data analytics.

2Problem It Solves

The primary problem RegTech addresses is the complexity and ever-changing nature of financial regulations that can lead to significant operational costs and legal risks for financial institutions if not properly managed.

4How It Works

RegTech solutions typically integrate with existing systems, such as accounting software or CRM platforms. They monitor regulatory updates in real-time, flag potential non-compliance issues, and provide automated reports to ensure ongoing adherence to regulatory standards.

The build process includes requirements gathering, design, development, testing, and deployment. It often requires collaboration between regulatory experts, software developers, and legal advisors to ensure that the solution accurately reflects current regulations.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Regulatory intelligence — Tracks and interprets regulatory changes from official sources. Key parts: web crawlers and APIs to official journals, document parsing and change detection, impact tagging.
  • Data integration — Pulls and normalises data from core banking, trading and communication systems. Key parts: ETL/ELT pipelines, entity resolution, data quality checks.
  • Rules and policy engine — Executes compliance rules as code. Key parts: rule authoring interface, version-controlled rule repository, execution engine.
  • Analytics and detection — Flags suspicious activity, risk and anomalies. Key parts: machine-learning models, graph analytics, scenario simulation.
  • Case management and workflow — Routes alerts to investigators and records decisions. Key parts: alert queue, investigation workspace, audit trail.
  • Reporting and submission — Generates and files regulatory reports. Key parts: report templates, validation checks, secure submission gateway.
  • Governance and explainability — Ensures models and rules are auditable and fair. Key parts: model registry, explainability tools, immutable logs.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Map obligations. Legal and compliance analysts break a regulation into concrete, testable requirements.
  2. Model the data. Engineers define which internal systems hold the needed data and build pipelines to extract, clean and link it.
  3. Encode rules. Requirements are translated into machine-executable rules, often in a domain-specific language, with version control.
  4. Train detection models. For risk and AML use cases, historical data and expert labels train and validate machine-learning models.
  5. Integrate workflows. Alerts, case management and reporting are wired into daily operations so staff act on the output.
  6. Validate and test. Backtesting, model validation and controlled pilots check that the system meets regulatory expectations.
  7. Deploy and monitor. The system runs in production with continuous monitoring for model drift, false positives and regulatory change.
  8. Update continuously. Horizon scanning feeds new rules and model retraining as regulations evolve.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Cloud and big-data infrastructure — compliance systems must process millions of transactions and communications per day.
  • PARTIAL Natural language processing — general NLP is mature, but reliably interpreting legal text across jurisdictions is still developing.
  • PARTIAL Machine-readable regulatory data — some regulators publish structured rulebooks, but most rules remain human-readable documents.
  • EXISTS Standardised legal entity identifiers — the Legal Entity Identifier (LEI, ISO 17442) lets systems link parties across datasets.
  • PARTIAL Explainable AI methods — regulators require reasons for decisions, and explainability tools are still maturing.
  • PARTIAL Secure data-sharing and privacy technology — industry-wide utilities need to share risk signals without exposing customer data.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: rule-based and basic analytics deployed in banks — lower reporting cost and faster detection of obvious breaches.
  2. Next: AI and NLP that interpret regulatory change and reduce false positives — proactive compliance and less manual review.
  3. Next: shared industry utilities for KYC and AML checks — network effects that make each institution's checks cheaper and more accurate.
  4. Longer term: real-time embedded compliance and supervisory technology (SupTech) used by regulators — continuous supervision and lower systemic risk.

Short for regulatory technology, it refers to the use of software solutions to help financial institutions comply with regulations and manage risks.
Compliance
The act of adhering to laws, rules, or standards set by regulatory bodies. In finance, this often involves ensuring that business practices meet legal requirements.
