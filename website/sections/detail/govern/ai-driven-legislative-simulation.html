9Engineering Breakdown
  • Population microdata layer — a statistical picture of who lives in the jurisdiction: age, income, location, employment, health, mobility. Key parts: census and register data, household surveys, privacy-preserving aggregation.
  • Agent population — millions of synthetic individuals whose joint statistics match the real population. Key parts: synthetic population generator, household and firm linkage, geographic and network placement.
  • Behavioural engine — decides what each agent does when the rules change. Key parts: rule-based economic behaviour, language-model personas for reasoning and messaging, calibrated response parameters from past policy changes.
  • Environment model — the world agents act in: labour and housing markets, prices, transport, public services. Key parts: market clearing, firm and employer agents, supply chains and prices.
  • Law-injection and scenario layer — translates a drafted law into changed parameters and rules the agents feel. Key parts: legal text to structured rules, eligibility and benefit calculators, comparison baselines.
  • Validation and uncertainty layer — tests whether the simulation reproduces outcomes we already know, and reports the range. Key parts: back-testing against historical reforms, sensitivity analysis, ensemble of differing models.
  • Audit and governance layer — keeps the tool honest and inspectable. Key parts: assumption registers, bias and fairness checks, public transparency and challenge process.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the question and the baseline. Specify which outcomes matter — employment, cost of living, small-business survival — and build a no-change baseline to compare against.
  2. Assemble microdata. Combine census, survey and administrative records under privacy safeguards to get a statistically faithful starting population.
  3. Generate the synthetic population. Sample millions of agents so that aggregate statistics match reality, then link them into households, firms and social networks.
  4. Calibrate behaviour. Fit response rules to natural experiments and past reforms, so agents react the way real people and firms measurably did.
  5. Encode the environment. Implement prices, markets and public services so agents compete for jobs, housing and goods rather than acting in isolation.
  6. Translate the drafted law. Convert legal text into explicit parameters — thresholds, rates, eligibility — and record every interpretation as an assumption.
  7. Run the ensemble. Run the scenario many times with varied assumptions and random seeds, then publish distributions rather than a single number.
  8. Back-test and stress-test. Replay historical laws the model never saw and check whether it would have predicted the observed outcome.
  9. Report, publish and iterate. Release results with error bars and assumptions; feed the disagreements between model and reality back into recalibration.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Agent-based computational social science — economic and epidemic agent models exist and are used, but they rarely capture a whole society's response.
  • EXISTS Large language models — needed to give agents plausible reasoning, speech and heterogeneous reactions.
  • PARTIAL Representative, linkable microdata — accuracy depends on detailed population data, much of which is restricted, sparse or not collected.
  • PARTIAL Credible validation methods — counterfactuals can never be directly observed, so predictive skill has to be inferred indirectly.
  • EXISTS Large-scale computing with privacy guarantees — millions of agents over many runs need heavy compute while protecting personal data.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: agent-based models used for specific questions such as disease spread, tax changes or transport demand — narrow, testable simulation of one policy lever at a time.
  2. Next: language-model personas reproduce survey and voting responses at population level — richer representation of opinion, messaging and behavioural heterogeneity.
  3. Next: retrospective validation: models reproduce the measured effects of past reforms across several countries — justified use as one input in impact assessment, alongside cost-benefit analysis.
  4. Longer term: institutional use as a documented, audited advisory tool with mandatory uncertainty reporting — faster, cheaper pre-testing of draft laws — while keeping the final decision with elected people.
  5. Speculative: continuously updated national digital twins that regulators query in real time — adaptive regulation that is revised as evidence accumulates rather than years later.
