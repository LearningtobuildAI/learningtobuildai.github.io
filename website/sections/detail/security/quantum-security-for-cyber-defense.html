1Definition

Quantum security leverages principles of quantum mechanics, such as superposition and entanglement, to create encryption methods that are theoretically unbreakable.

2Problem It Solves

Traditional cryptographic methods are vulnerable to quantum computing attacks that could break encryption codes in the future. Quantum security provides a way to secure communications and data storage against such threats by ensuring that any interception of key transmission is immediately detected.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Quantum key distribution (QKD) is a method where two parties can generate a shared secret key using the laws of physics. This process involves sending entangled photons over a communication channel; any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept or measure these particles will alter their state, alerting the communicating parties to potential security breaches.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process for quantum security devices involves creating entangled photon sources, typically using lasers or spontaneous parametric down-conversion processes. These components are then integrated into communication systems, which require precise alignment and calibration to maintain the integrity of the quantum states.

7Build Process

Building a quantum secure system requires expertise in both quantum physics and classical cryptography. The process involves designing quantum circuits, implementing error correction protocols, and integrating these with existing communication infrastructure.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking processes. Long-term operation requires stable power supplies and cooling systems to maintain quantum states.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Transmitter — encodes each bit of a key onto a single photon's quantum state. Key parts: faint-pulse laser or single-photon source, phase/polarisation/time-bin modulator, random number generator.
  • Quantum channel — carries photons between two sites without measurement along the way. Key parts: dedicated dark optical fibre or free-space/satellite link, low-loss connectors and repeaters-free spans, trusted intermediate nodes for long routes.
  • Receiver — measures the photons in randomly chosen bases. Key parts: single-photon detectors (superconducting nanowire or InGaAs avalanche), polarisation or interferometric optics, cooling.
  • Classical post-processing — turns raw detections into a short, secret key. Key parts: key sifting, error correction, privacy amplification (hashing), authenticated classical channel.
  • Key management layer — stores keys and hands them to encrypting applications. Key parts: key store, policy engine, integration with network encryptors or TLS.
  • Post-quantum cryptography stack — software algorithms that resist quantum attack and need no special hardware. Key parts: lattice-based key encapsulation and signatures, hash-based signatures, updated libraries and protocols.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the threat model. Decide whether the need is protecting data in transit today, or protecting traffic recorded now and decrypted later once a quantum computer exists.
  2. Lay or secure the channel. QKD needs a dedicated fibre pair or a clear line of sight; sharing fibre with ordinary traffic adds noise that kills the key rate.
  3. Prepare the source. A laser emits highly attenuated pulses, or a defect centre emits one photon at a time, and its state is modulated to encode a bit.
  4. Transmit and detect. The receiver randomly picks a measurement basis for each photon; an eavesdropper measuring in between introduces errors the two sides can see.
  5. Sift, correct and amplify. Matching-basis results are kept, errors are reconciled over an authenticated classical channel, and hashing shrinks the string so any leaked information is squeezed out.
  6. Run classical authentication. The classical channel must itself be authenticated, typically with post-quantum signatures, or the whole exchange can be intercepted.
  7. Deliver keys to applications. Keys are stored in a management system and consumed by encryptors or protocol libraries, refreshed continuously.
  8. Deploy hybrid PQC alongside. Most organisations combine post-quantum algorithms with existing cryptography in software, since that covers stored data and long-lived secrets without new hardware.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Single-photon detectors and sources — QKD depends on counting individual photons reliably at telecom wavelengths.
  • EXISTS Dedicated low-loss fibre or satellite links — photon loss limits QKD to roughly a few hundred kilometres of fibre before trusted nodes are needed.
  • EXISTS Standardised post-quantum algorithms — national standards bodies published the first post-quantum encryption and signature standards in 2024, making software migration possible.
  • NOT YET Quantum repeaters — without them, entanglement cannot be extended across continents without trusted relay nodes.
  • NOT YET A cryptographically relevant quantum computer — no machine can yet run Shor's algorithm at the scale needed to break widely used public-key cryptography.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: post-quantum algorithms standardised and metro-scale QKD links running in trials — inventorying where long-lived secrets sit and starting software migration.
  2. Next: hybrid systems that combine post-quantum software with QKD hardware — protection of both stored data and keys in transit without betting on one approach.
  3. Next: satellite QKD and quantum repeaters in the lab — intercity and intercontinental key distribution without trusting every intermediate node.
  4. Longer term: a network of entangled nodes with device-independent security proofs — communication whose security rests on physics rather than on trusting the equipment.

18Glossary
quantum key distribution (QKD)
A method for secure communication where two parties can generate a shared secret key using the laws of physics, ensuring that any eavesdropping attempt is immediately detected.
entanglement
A phenomenon in quantum mechanics where pairs or groups of particles interact in such a way that the state of one particle cannot be described independently of the state of the others, even when the particles are separated by large distances.
superposition
In quantum mechanics, the property of a physical system existing partly in all its theoretically possible quantum states simultaneously; only when measured or observed does such a system exhibit a single outcome, via a random process.
