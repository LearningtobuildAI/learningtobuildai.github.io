9Engineering Breakdown
  • Issuer and trust framework — the body that vouches for a fact about you — your age, nationality or licence — and signs it. Key parts: accredited issuers such as a government registry or bank, issuer signing keys, published list of recognised issuers.
  • Credential format and signatures — lets the holder later reveal only part of what was signed, without breaking the signature. Key parts: W3C Verifiable Credentials data model, selective-disclosure schemes such as BBS+ signatures or SD-JWT, blinding and derivation logic.
  • Holder wallet — keeps credentials on the user's own device and generates the proof only with their consent. Key parts: mobile or browser app, secure element or keystore, prover library, consent and disclosure screen.
  • Proof system and circuits — turns the claim into a tiny proof that reveals nothing else. Key parts: circuit compiler producing arithmetic constraints, proving key, SNARK or STARK prover, trusted-setup artefacts where required.
  • Verifier — the relying party checks the proof in milliseconds and binds it to the current request. Key parts: verification key, session nonce, browser or server verification library.
  • Revocation and status service — prevents revoked or already-used credentials from working again. Key parts: status lists or cryptographic accumulators, nullifier registry, issuer refresh channel.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the claim and the trust model. Decide precisely what must be proved ('age is over 18', 'resident in this country'), who is allowed to sign it, and what the verifier is entitled to learn.
  2. Issue a signed credential. The issuer signs the user's attributes with a key that supports selective disclosure — for example a BBS+ signature or a salted SD-JWT — and hands the credential to the user's wallet.
  3. Encode the claim as a circuit. The verification logic is written as arithmetic constraints: the computation the proof must attest to, including hidden inputs for the secret attributes.
  4. Set up the keys. Systems like Groth16 need a one-time trusted setup per circuit, whose ceremony must be witnessed and destroyed afterwards; PLONK-style systems use a universal setup and STARKs need none at all.
  5. Generate the witness and proof. The wallet supplies the hidden values plus randomness, runs the prover on the phone or a server, and produces a short proof and a one-time nullifier.
  6. Verify and bind the proof. The relying party runs the verification key against the proof, checks a fresh session nonce so an old proof cannot be replayed, and reads the one public bit it asked for.
  7. Wire in revocation. The verifier checks a status list or accumulator, and records the nullifier so the same credential cannot be presented twice for the same entitlement.
  8. Audit and operate. The circuits, the setup ceremony and the wallet's randomness handling are audited; issuers rotate keys and publish status updates for the life of the system.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Practical zero-knowledge proof systems — Groth16, PLONK and STARKs make proofs small and fast enough to verify on an ordinary server.
  • PARTIAL Selective-disclosure credentials — standards such as W3C Verifiable Credentials and SD-JWT with BBS+ are settled but unevenly deployed.
  • EXISTS Secure wallets in phones and browsers — the secret attributes and signing keys must stay on hardware the user controls.
  • PARTIAL Revocation and anti-replay infrastructure — without status checks and nullifiers a stolen or spent credential still works.
  • PARTIAL Trusted issuer registries — a verifier has to know which keys belong to a legitimate authority before believing any proof.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: zero-knowledge proofs in production since Zcash in 2016 and now in zk-rollups verifying millions of proofs — confidence that the cryptography works at scale, even though identity use is still mostly pilots.
  2. Next: EU Digital Identity Wallet under eIDAS 2.0, with member states required to offer wallets to residents during 2026 — hundreds of millions of people holding government-backed credentials that can prove a fact without handing over the document.
  3. Next: reusable KYC and age checks across unrelated services — one verified credential instead of the same passport scan sent to dozens of companies.
  4. Longer term: general anonymous credentials, plus post-quantum and transparent proof systems — everyday proofs about income, qualifications or entitlements with no central party seeing the underlying data.
