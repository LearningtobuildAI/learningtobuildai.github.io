PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

QKD works by using single photons to transmit information. Any attempt to intercept or measure these photons disrupts their state, alerting both sender and receiver to potential eavesdropping, thus ensuring the security of the key exchange process.

5Materials Used

  • nanostructured materials
  • photonic crystals
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing involves creating single-photon sources and detectors with high precision, often using semiconductor technologies or specialized optical setups.

7Build Process

Components include quantum dots, photonic crystals, and other nanostructured materials. The process is highly complex and requires cleanroom environments.

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking and precise control processes.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Transmitter — prepares and sends the quantum states that carry the key. Key parts: laser, intensity modulator, variable attenuator, phase or polarisation encoder, quantum random number generator for basis choice.
  • Quantum channel — carries single photons or entangled pairs between the two parties. Key parts: dedicated optical fibre (often around 1550 nm), free-space telescope link or satellite, low-loss connectors and filters.
  • Detector module — measures the received states in a randomly chosen basis. Key parts: single-photon detectors (InGaAs avalanche photodiodes or superconducting nanowire detectors), cooling, timing electronics.
  • Classical authenticated channel — lets both sides compare bases, correct errors and compress the key without an eavesdropper altering messages. Key parts: pre-shared secret or post-quantum signature, ordinary network link.
  • Post-processing — turns raw correlated bits into a secret key. Key parts: sifting, error correction (e.g. Cascade or LDPC), privacy amplification by hashing.
  • Key management and network layer — stores keys and feeds them to encrypting equipment. Key parts: key store, trusted-node relay software, interfaces to AES or one-time-pad encryptors.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the link. Choose fibre or free-space, measure the loss over the route, and set a key-rate target that detectors and error correction can meet.
  2. Build the transmitter. Assemble a laser, modulator, attenuator and encoder so each pulse contains on average far less than one photon.
  3. Build the receiver. Install single-photon detectors, a random basis selector and timing electronics in a temperature-controlled enclosure.
  4. Lay or point the channel. Use a dedicated dark fibre pair between the sites, or align a telescope link for free-space or satellite QKD.
  5. Authenticate the classical channel. Load a pre-shared secret or a post-quantum signature key so the basis-sifting messages cannot be spoofed.
  6. Run the protocol. Transmit quantum states, record detections, then publicly compare bases to keep only matching events.
  7. Extract a secret key. Correct remaining errors and apply privacy amplification to shrink any eavesdropper's knowledge to a negligible amount.
  8. Deliver keys to encryptors. Feed the fresh key to AES or a one-time pad for data in transit, and log key consumption.
  9. Extend reach with trusted nodes. Where single-link distance is too short, chain links through secure intermediate nodes that decrypt and re-encrypt.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Single-photon detectors — they must catch individual photons with low noise; superconducting types are fast but need cryogenics.
  • EXISTS Low-loss optical fibre and free-space optics — loss limits both distance and key rate, since photons cannot be copied or amplified.
  • EXISTS Quantum random number generators — basis and bit choices must be unpredictable, not merely pseudo-random.
  • PARTIAL Authenticated classical channel — without authentication, a man-in-the-middle can impersonate either side; post-quantum signatures are the scalable fix.
  • NOT YET Quantum repeaters — they would allow long-distance entanglement without trusted nodes, which no deployed system can yet do.
  • PARTIAL Integrated photonic chips for QKD — they promise lower cost and mass production, but are still maturing.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: point-to-point and metro QKD links, plus satellite demonstrations such as Micius (2016) and the Beijing–Shanghai backbone (2017) — high-value links where a dedicated fibre and trusted nodes are acceptable.
  2. Next: chip-scale QKD integrated into telecom equipment, with higher key rates and automated key management — cheaper deployment in data-centre and enterprise networks.
  3. Next: routine satellite QKD and intercontinental key relay — key distribution between continents where no fibre exists.
  4. Longer term: quantum repeaters and entanglement-based networks — device-independent security and a path toward a quantum internet.
