4How It Works

Data about individuals (such as name, date of birth, address) are encrypted and stored on a distributed ledger. Each transaction or update creates a new block that links back to the previous one, forming an immutable chain. Smart contracts can automate verification processes based on predefined rules.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Decentralized identifier (DID) registry — A tamper-resistant place to register identifiers and public keys, so anyone can resolve them without a central authority. Key parts: DID method specification, DID document (public keys, service endpoints), blockchain or distributed ledger (or non-ledger alternative like did:web), resolver software.
  • Verifiable credential (VC) layer — The signed digital claims that issuers give to holders and verifiers can check. Key parts: issuer software, VC data model, cryptographic signatures (e.g. Ed25519, BBS+), schemas and revocation lists.
  • Holder wallet — Stores the user's private keys and credentials, and presents proofs on request. Key parts: secure key storage (hardware element or secure enclave), credential database, presentation protocol (e.g. OpenID for Verifiable Credentials), user interface.
  • Proof and privacy engine — Lets a holder prove only what is needed — for example, that they are over 18 without revealing a birth date. Key parts: zero-knowledge proof systems, selective disclosure algorithms, challenge–response protocols.
  • Trust and governance framework — Defines who may issue which credentials, how disputes are handled, and what is legally recognised. Key parts: trust registries, accreditation rules, legal recognition (e.g. EU eIDAS 2.0), privacy and data-protection policies.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the identity model and governance. Decide which claims (age, licence, degree) are needed, who can issue them, and how privacy and liability are handled.
  2. Choose a DID method and registry. Select a blockchain or other verifiable data registry — for example did:ion on Bitcoin, did:ethr on Ethereum, or a non-ledger method such as did:web. Specify how DIDs are created, updated and deactivated.
  3. Deploy issuer infrastructure. Each issuer runs a node, creates its own DID, and publishes its public keys and a revocation mechanism to the registry.
  4. Issue verifiable credentials. The issuer signs a credential containing the holder's DID and the claims, then delivers it to the holder's wallet.
  5. Store credentials in a wallet. The wallet generates and protects the holder's private keys, encrypts credentials, and keeps them under the user's control.
  6. Present a proof. When a verifier asks, the wallet creates a verifiable presentation — optionally using a zero-knowledge proof to reveal only the required fact.
  7. Verify and check revocation. The verifier resolves the issuer's DID, validates the signature, and checks the current revocation list or accumulator.
  8. Support key rotation and recovery. Provide ways to update keys, revoke a lost wallet, and recover an identity without a central administrator.
  9. Integrate with existing systems. Connect the wallet to login flows, payment systems and physical access control so the credential is actually useful.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS W3C standards for DIDs and verifiable credentials — They give a common data model so wallets and issuers from different vendors can interoperate. DID 1.0 became a W3C Recommendation in 2022.
  • EXISTS Public blockchain or distributed ledger — Provides a censorship-resistant registry for DIDs and revocation data. Some designs use non-ledger registries instead.
  • PARTIAL Efficient zero-knowledge proofs — They enable selective disclosure and unlinkable presentations, but are still complex and costly for some devices.
  • EXISTS Secure hardware for key storage — Phones and dedicated wallets can protect private keys in a secure element, reducing theft risk.
  • PARTIAL Legal recognition of digital identity wallets — The EU's eIDAS 2.0 regulation requires member states to offer wallets, but most countries lack equivalent rules.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: Pilots and sandboxes by governments, banks and universities using verifiable credentials — Practical experience with issuance, wallets and revocation at small scale.
  2. Next: EU member states deploy eIDAS 2.0 digital identity wallets under the 2026 deadline — Millions of users get a government-recognised wallet, driving issuer adoption.
  3. Next: Cross-border and cross-ledger interoperability profiles mature — A credential issued in one country or system can be verified in another.
  4. Longer term: Mainstream replacement of passwords and physical ID cards for everyday transactions — Lower identity theft, less data sharing, and user-controlled digital identities.

18Glossary
Blockchain
A decentralized digital ledger that records transactions across multiple computers in a secure, tamper-proof manner.
Smart Contracts
Self-executing contracts with the terms of the agreement directly written into code. They automate processes and enforce agreements within blockchain networks.
Cryptographic Techniques
Mathematical methods used to secure information by transforming it into a format that only authorized parties can access.
Distributed Ledger
A database of transactions shared across multiple computers in a network, where no single entity has control over the entire ledger.
