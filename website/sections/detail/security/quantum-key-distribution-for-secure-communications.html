PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) is a method of securely distributing cryptographic keys using principles of quantum mechanics to ensure that any interception or unauthorized access can be detected.

Category
Security
Best use
Data encryption, secure networks
Stage

2Problem It Solves

Traditional encryption methods are vulnerable to quantum computing attacks that could potentially break current cryptographic systems. QKD provides a solution by leveraging quantum mechanics to ensure secure and unbreakable communication channels.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

QKD uses entangled particles such as photons to transmit information. Any attempt by an eavesdropper to intercept the communication will alter the state of these particles, which can be detected at the receiving end, thus ensuring the security of the key exchange.

5Materials Used
  • photonic crystals
  • superconducting materials
  • semiconductors
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing QKD devices involves creating highly precise optical components, such as lasers and detectors, which need to operate at low temperatures and in controlled environments to maintain their functionality.

7Build Process

The build process includes designing and fabricating quantum optics, integrating them into a secure communication system, and calibrating the entire setup for optimal performance. This involves complex engineering and stringent quality control measures.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. Long-term operation requires stable power supplies and cooling systems.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Photon source — generates the quantum states (single photons or weak coherent pulses) that carry the key. Key parts: laser, attenuator, phase modulator.
  • Quantum channel — transmits photons with minimal loss and disturbance between the two parties. Key parts: optical fibre, free-space telescope, satellite link.
  • Detector — measures incoming photons and records their quantum state. Key parts: single-photon avalanche diode, superconducting nanowire detector.
  • Classical authenticated channel — public link used for basis reconciliation, error correction and authentication. Key parts: internet connection, message authentication code.
  • Post-processing — turns raw detection events into a shared secret key and estimates eavesdropping. Key parts: sifting, error correction, privacy amplification.
  • Key management — stores keys and supplies them to encryption devices on demand. Key parts: key store, interface to encryptor.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Survey the link. Measure distance and loss; choose optical fibre for metropolitan links or free-space/satellite for longer or mobile paths.
  2. Install endpoints. Set up photon sources and single-photon detectors at each end, with precise timing and alignment.
  3. Establish classical authentication. Set up an authenticated public channel so an eavesdropper cannot impersonate either party.
  4. Transmit quantum signals. Send photons one by one (or as weak pulses) and record which detector fires and at what time.
  5. Sift the raw key. Compare measurement bases over the classical channel and keep only the events where both sides used the same basis.
  6. Correct errors and check security. Run error correction to reconcile differences; estimate the error rate to bound how much an eavesdropper might know.
  7. Amplify privacy. Compress the corrected key into a shorter string that is provably secret except with negligible probability.
  8. Integrate with encryption. Feed the key into a classical encryptor (one-time pad or AES) for data transmission.
  9. Operate and monitor. Continuously generate fresh keys and watch for anomalies that could indicate an attack or hardware fault.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Single-photon detectors — they must detect individual photons with low noise and high timing resolution.
  • PARTIAL Low-loss optical fibre or free-space links — fibre loss limits range to a few hundred kilometres without repeaters; free-space needs clear line of sight.
  • EXISTS Quantum random number generators — keys must be based on true randomness, not pseudo-random algorithms.
  • EXISTS Information-theoretic authentication — the classical channel must be authenticated without relying on unproven computational assumptions.
  • NOT YET Quantum repeaters — they would extend entanglement-based QKD beyond the reach of direct fibre or trusted-node networks.
  • PARTIAL Standardisation and certification — interoperability and security certification are still maturing, slowing large-scale deployment.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: Metropolitan point-to-point QKD links and small trusted-node networks — high-security key exchange for banks, data centres and government links over tens to hundreds of kilometres.
  2. Next: National trusted-node backbones and satellite QKD for intercontinental key exchange — wider coverage without quantum repeaters, though each trusted node must be physically secure.
  3. Next: Quantum repeaters and entanglement distribution — long-distance QKD without trusted nodes, plus building blocks for a quantum internet.
  4. Longer term: Integration with quantum-safe cryptography and quantum networks — hybrid systems that combine physics-based key distribution with post-quantum algorithms for defence in depth.

18Glossary
Entanglement
A phenomenon in quantum mechanics where particles become interconnected such that the state of one particle can instantaneously affect the state of another, no matter the distance between them.
Quantum Channel
A communication channel based on principles of quantum mechanics used to transmit information securely through QKD.
