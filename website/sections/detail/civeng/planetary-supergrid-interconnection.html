PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Ultra-high-voltage direct current links spanning thousands of kilometres let desert solar and high-latitude wind serve distant load centres, smoothing the diurnal and seasonal mismatch that no amount of local storage fully solves. Submarine intercontinental links are the limiting engineering case.

4How It Works

Converter stations rectify alternating current to high-voltage direct current, transmit over overhead or submarine cable, and invert back at the receiving end, with voltage-source converters providing grid-forming support.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Converter stations — the AC/DC interface at each end of the link. Key parts: converter transformers, valve halls (thyristor or IGBT modules), DC switchgear, AC filters and reactive compensation, cooling plant.
  • Overhead line — the cheap, high-capacity land portion of the route. Key parts: lattice towers or monopoles, aluminium conductor bundles, composite or glass insulators, earth wire.
  • Submarine cable — carries DC power under water, where overhead lines are impossible. Key parts: copper or aluminium conductor, extruded polymer insulation, lead or aluminium moisture barrier, steel armouring and factory joints.
  • DC protection and switching — clears faults on a network where current has no natural zero crossing. Key parts: hybrid DC circuit breakers, fault-current limiters, fast fault detection.
  • Control and grid-forming systems — keeps a converter-fed network stable without the inertia of spinning generators. Key parts: grid-forming converter controls, wide-area measurement and communications, black-start capability.
  • Installation fleet — lays and buries thousands of kilometres of cable. Key parts: cable-laying ships, sea ploughs, work-class ROVs, jointing and repair equipment.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Plan the route. Map generation, demand and seabed bathymetry to pick corridors that balance cost, loss and environmental impact.
  2. Survey the seabed. Multibeam sonar and sub-bottom profiling identify cables crossings, trenches, wrecks and protected habitats.
  3. Manufacture cable. Conductor, insulation, moisture barrier and armour are built up in one continuous factory run, since a joint at sea is far more vulnerable than one on land.
  4. Lay the submarine section. A cable ship pays cable out under controlled tension; near shore a plough or jetting tool buries it below the seabed for protection from anchors and trawls.
  5. Build the overhead section. On land, towers and conductors are erected, or the route diverts underground through cable tunnels where land is scarce.
  6. Construct converter stations. Valve halls, transformers and filters are built at each terminus and connected to the existing AC grid.
  7. Commission and test. Power is ramped up, protection settings are proven, and converter controls are tuned to support the connected grids.
  8. Operate as a network. Trading and dispatch schedules move power between time zones, while converters provide voltage and frequency support as well as bulk transfer.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-voltage direct current transmission — the only practical way to move bulk power thousands of kilometres with acceptable losses.
  • EXISTS Ultra-high-voltage overhead lines — overhead DC lines above 3,000 km at up to 1,100 kV are already operating in China.
  • EXISTS Long submarine DC cable — the longest subsea links now run roughly 700–800 km at around 525 kV using extruded insulation.
  • PARTIAL Multi-terminal DC grids and DC breakers — point-to-point links dominate; switching faults on a meshed DC network is only now being proved at high voltage.
  • PARTIAL Grid-forming inverters and inertia substitutes — a converter-only grid has no rotating mass, so stability must be created electronically.
  • NOT YET Low-loss superconductive or advanced cables — would be needed for links far longer than any submarine route currently built.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: point-to-point HVDC links of a few gigawatts, mostly within one continent — sharing power between neighbouring countries and moving offshore wind to shore.
  2. Next: multi-terminal DC hubs, such as the North Sea and Chinese meshed DC projects, connecting several countries at once — the building blocks of a regional supergrid.
  3. Next: regional supergrids joining continents across short sea crossings — using evening demand in one region to absorb midday solar from another.
  4. Longer term: intercontinental links carrying desert solar and high-latitude wind — near-continuous renewable supply without relying on long-duration storage alone.
  5. Far term: a planetary network of interlinked continental grids — seasonal balancing across hemispheres — the technical limit is cable loss, not distance.
