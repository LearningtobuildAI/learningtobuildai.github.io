1Definition

Water from carbonaceous asteroids is the cheapest plausible source of propellant, radiation shielding and life support for anything operating beyond low Earth orbit. No mission has yet returned volatile material at meaningful scale.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Target prospecting and selection — find a carbonaceous (C-type) near-Earth asteroid rich in hydrated minerals and reachable with low velocity change. Key parts: ground and space telescopes, spectroscopic surveys, small precursor spacecraft.
  • Capture or anchoring — secure the asteroid or the mining equipment in microgravity so that extraction can proceed. Key parts: containment bag or enclosure, anchoring harpoons or drills, robotic arms.
  • Solar heating and extraction — heat water-bearing regolith until ice and hydrated minerals release water vapour. Key parts: inflatable solar concentrators, heating chamber or retort, insulation and seals.
  • Vapour collection and purification — condense the water vapour and separate it from other volatiles such as carbon dioxide and ammonia. Key parts: cold traps, radiators, filters and separators.
  • Electrolysis and storage — split water into hydrogen and oxygen and keep them as cryogenic liquids or high-pressure gas. Key parts: water electrolyser, cryocoolers, tanks and plumbing.
  • Power and thermal control — supply electricity for heating, pumping and refrigeration in deep space. Key parts: solar arrays, batteries, radiators.
  • Propulsion and transfer — reach the asteroid, then deliver the propellant to a depot or use it in place. Key parts: solar electric or chemical thrusters, navigation sensors, docking interface.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Survey and choose a target. Telescope surveys identify C-type asteroids whose spectra show water-bearing clays and whose orbits are easy to reach.
  2. Launch and cruise. A robotic spacecraft flies to the asteroid using solar electric propulsion or chemical stages, taking months to years.
  3. Rendezvous and characterise. The craft matches the asteroid's orbit, maps its shape and spin, and measures water content with spectrometers or a small lander.
  4. Capture or anchor. A small asteroid might be enclosed in a bag; a larger one requires anchoring so a robotic excavator can dig into regolith without pushing itself away.
  5. Heat the regolith. Concentrated sunlight, focused by inflatable mirrors, raises the material to roughly 100–200 °C so water sublimates out of the minerals.
  6. Collect the water vapour. The vapour is drawn into cold traps, where it freezes onto surfaces chilled by radiators, then melts into liquid water.
  7. Electrolyse and store. An electrolyser splits the water into hydrogen and oxygen, which are liquefied by cryocoolers and kept in insulated tanks.
  8. Return or depot. The propellant is either used by the mining craft itself or delivered to a storage depot in cislunar space for future missions.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Asteroid rendezvous and sample return — JAXA's Hayabusa2 and NASA's OSIRIS-REx returned grams of asteroid material in 2020 and 2023, but no mission has mined or processed volatiles.
  • NOT YET In-space cryogenic propellant storage and transfer — hydrogen boils away unless actively cooled; long-term storage and transfer of cryogens in space has not been demonstrated at scale.
  • NOT YET Low-gravity anchoring and regolith handling — drilling or digging on a small body tends to push the spacecraft off unless it is firmly anchored, a problem not yet solved in practice.
  • PARTIAL Solar concentrators for high-temperature heating — inflatable solar mirrors have been tested in space, but not for sustained heating of asteroid material.
  • EXISTS Water electrolysis in microgravity — the International Space Station produces oxygen from water by electrolysis, proving the chemistry works in orbit.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous robotic operation — communication delays of minutes to hours mean the mining craft must make many decisions on its own, which is beyond current flight software.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: sample-return missions have brought back grams of asteroid material for laboratory study — knowledge of which asteroids contain water and how their surfaces behave.
  2. Next: a prospecting mission to a C-type near-Earth asteroid measures its water content in situ — selection of a target worth mining.
  3. Next: a small-scale extraction demonstration returns a few hundred kilograms of water from an asteroid — proof that the heating, collection and storage steps work in space.
  4. Longer term: commercial extraction and delivery of propellant to a cislunar depot — affordable deep-space transport, because propellant no longer has to be launched from Earth.
