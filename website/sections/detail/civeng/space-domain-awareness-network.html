9Engineering Breakdown
  • Ground-based radar — tracks objects in low Earth orbit day or night, measuring range and velocity. Key parts: phased-array radar, dish radar, transmitter and receiver.
  • Optical telescopes — tracks objects in higher orbits by reflected sunlight, needing dark skies. Key parts: wide-field survey telescopes, precision tracking mounts, CCD or CMOS detectors.
  • Space-based sensors — observes objects from orbit, free of weather and daylight limits. Key parts: telescope payloads, star trackers, onboard processing.
  • Data fusion and catalogue — combines observations from many sensors into one catalogue of orbits. Key parts: orbit determination software, observation correlation algorithms, master object database.
  • Conjunction assessment — predicts close approaches and issues collision-avoidance warnings. Key parts: orbit propagation models, collision probability calculation, alert system.
  • Attribution and manoeuvre detection — detects unannounced manoeuvres by comparing predicted and observed positions. Key parts: change-detection algorithms, historical orbit records, operator databases.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Define coverage requirements. Decide which orbits and object sizes must be tracked, and how quickly warnings must be issued.
  2. Select and build sensor sites. Radar sites need clear horizons and power; optical sites need dark, dry, high-altitude locations with good latitude coverage.
  3. Install and calibrate sensors. Radars and telescopes are installed and calibrated against known objects or calibration satellites to ensure accurate pointing and timing.
  4. Build the data pipeline. Observations are transmitted to a central processing facility, where they are cleaned, time-stamped and correlated with existing tracks.
  5. Maintain the catalogue. Initial orbits are determined for new objects, and orbits are regularly updated as new observations arrive.
  6. Develop analysis software. Software screens for conjunctions, calculates collision probabilities, and flags manoeuvres that differ from predicted paths.
  7. Integrate the network. Sensors, processing and user interfaces are linked so that data flows automatically from observation to warning.
  8. Deliver services. Conjunction warnings, catalogue data and attribution reports are provided to satellite operators and government agencies.
  9. Expand and improve. New sensors, better algorithms and additional data-sharing agreements are added over time to improve coverage and accuracy.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Radar tracking technology — provides all-weather, day-night tracking of objects in low Earth orbit.
  • EXISTS Optical tracking technology — tracks objects in higher orbits where radar is less effective.
  • EXISTS Orbit determination and propagation software — turns raw observations into predicted paths and close-approach warnings.
  • EXISTS High-performance computing — processes large volumes of observations and screens many object pairs for collisions.
  • PARTIAL Space-based sensors — a few demonstration satellites exist, but not yet a persistent global network.
  • PARTIAL International data-sharing frameworks — some data is shared between agencies, but coverage and timeliness vary.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: ground-based radar and optical networks operated by governments and commercial firms — conjunction warnings and basic attribution for satellite operators.
  2. Next: expansion of commercial sensor networks and dedicated space-based sensors — more frequent tracking of smaller objects and better coverage of higher orbits.
  3. Next: automated collision avoidance integrated with satellite operations — routine, low-latency manoeuvres to avoid debris without human delay.
  4. Longer term: orbital traffic management and active debris removal — a sustainable orbital environment for large constellations and future space activities.
