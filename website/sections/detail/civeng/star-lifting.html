PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Star lifting is the extraction of matter from a star's outer layers to extend its main-sequence lifetime, alter its spectrum, or harvest metals and helium for construction. The extracted plasma doubles as reaction mass and as feedstock for megastructure assembly.

4How It Works

Proposals use magnetohydrodynamic pumping, in which powerful magnetic fields tap the star's own plasma and guide it toward the poles, or a directed beam that ablates a small region of the photosphere. The lifted material is captured at the poles and separated into hydrogen, helium and trace metals.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Magnetic tap and guide array — creates a controlled opening in the star's magnetic field and steers plasma toward the poles. Key parts: superconducting coils sized in the thousands of kilometres, field-shaping control electronics, cooling and shielding.
  • Remote power supply — provides the energy to start and sustain extraction, drawn from the star itself. Key parts: close-orbiting collector mirrors, concentrated-light or beamed-power relay, energy storage.
  • Polar capture station — sits at the magnetic pole to catch the outflowing plasma. Key parts: magnetic funnel or scoop, heat exchangers, plasma injector.
  • Separation and storage plant — sorts hydrogen, helium and trace metals and stores them. Key parts: electromagnetic or centrifugal mass separator, cryogenic hydrogen tanks, containment for heavier elements.
  • Control and monitoring network — watches the star's activity and adjusts the extraction rate before the star destabilises. Key parts: magnetohydrodynamic simulation, sensors across the array, automated feedback.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Study the target star. Decades of data on its magnetic cycle, rotation and convection are needed before any structure is placed, because the tap has to work with the star's own plasma flows rather than fight them.
  2. Assemble the array off-world. Coils and collectors are built in space from asteroid or lunar material, since launching them from Earth at this scale is impractical.
  3. Position and hold the array. The structure orbits close to the star or holds station above a pole, using light pressure and magnetic forces to stay in place despite the outward push of the star's wind.
  4. Establish power. Collector mirrors concentrate the star's own light to energise the magnets — the system must bootstrap itself, because nothing else can supply the required power.
  5. Seed the magnetic tap. A localised field is shaped in the photosphere, or a narrow beam ablates a small patch, raising a plume of plasma in the star's ~5,500 °C surface layers.
  6. Channel and lift. The magnetic nozzle guides the plume along field lines toward the pole, where the plasma is moving fast enough to be captured rather than falling back.
  7. Capture and separate. The polar station slows the plasma, extracts its heat, and separates hydrogen, helium and metals for storage or for use as reaction mass.
  8. Sustain and regulate. Extraction is held at a low fraction of the star's mass-loss and luminosity, with feedback that reduces the rate if the magnetic cycle or convection pattern shifts.
  9. Exploit the result. Lifted mass extends the star's main-sequence lifetime by removing the dense outer layers, supplies building material, and can be expelled as exhaust to move the entire system.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Superconducting magnets at stellar scale — coils thousands of kilometres across must survive intense heat, radiation and mechanical force.
  • NOT YET Megastructure assembly in space — the hardware must be built and positioned without being launched from Earth.
  • PARTIAL Stellar dynamo and convection modelling — predicting how a star reacts to having its outer layers drained is not yet reliable.
  • NOT YET Autonomous in-situ resource use — the whole operation must run without continuous human presence.
  • PARTIAL High-temperature materials and heat rejection — structures near a star need to shed enormous heat loads while staying intact.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: helioseismology and solar physics missions mapping the Sun's interior and magnetic cycle — the physics needed to predict how a star responds to any intervention.
  2. Next: robotic assembly of large structures in space from off-Earth materials — the practical means to build anything on this scale.
  3. Next: a small-scale stellar tap that lifts a measurable but tiny flux from a star's polar region — proof that magnetic extraction can be controlled rather than triggering instability.
  4. Longer term: sustained, regulated extraction on a star — lifetime extension, bulk materials, and the ability to move a whole stellar system.
