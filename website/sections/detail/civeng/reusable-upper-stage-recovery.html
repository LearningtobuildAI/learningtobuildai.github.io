1Definition

Recovering the second stage is the final barrier to complete launch reuse, since the upper stage is the largest single expendable cost. Designs range from propulsive return with a heat shield to metallic aeroshells with actively cooled leading edges.

4How It Works

The stage reenters nose-first behind a regeneratively cooled heat shield or a metallic aerospike, then performs a propulsive landing burn on the same engines used for orbital insertion.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Reentry heat shield — protects the stage as it comes back through the atmosphere at orbital speed. Key parts: ceramic tiles or metallic panels, leading-edge material, mechanical attachments that allow thermal expansion.
  • Engine section — performs the insertion burn and later the landing burn with the same engines. Key parts: restartable, deep-throttling engines, gimbal actuators, propellant feed lines.
  • Propellant tanks and structure — holds the propellant and carries launch and reentry loads. Key parts: thin-walled cryogenic tanks, common bulkhead, stainless-steel or aluminium-lithium skin.
  • Attitude control and aerosurfaces — keeps the stage pointed correctly while it is in the plasma and during the final descent. Key parts: body flaps or grid fins, reaction control thrusters, cold-gas tanks.
  • Guidance, navigation and control — flies the reentry and lands the stage on a target within metres. Key parts: flight computers, inertial navigation and GNSS, landing radar or optical guidance.
  • Recovery and refurbishment infrastructure — gets the stage back on a pad and ready to fly again. Key parts: catch tower, landing pad or parachute recovery, transport cradle, inspection and tile-replacement equipment.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the recovery architecture. Decide whether the whole stage comes back, only the engine section returns, and whether it lands on legs, is caught by a tower, or comes down under parachutes. Thermal protection is dead weight, so every kilogram of shield is a kilogram of lost payload.
  2. Fabricate tanks and structure. Thin-gauge stainless steel or aluminium-lithium sheet is rolled, friction-stir welded into tanks, and stiffened so the stage survives both launch loads and the bending loads of reentry.
  3. Fix the thermal protection. Thousands of tiles or metallic panels are bonded or bolted to the windward side, with gaps sized so the hot structure can expand without cracking the shield.
  4. Integrate engines and control surfaces. Deep-throttling restartable engines are fitted along with flaps or grid fins and small thrusters that hold the stage at the right angle during reentry.
  5. Launch and separate. The stage fires to place its payload in orbit, then reserves enough propellant for a deorbit burn and a landing burn — a reserve that directly cuts payload mass.
  6. Deorbit and reenter. A short retro burn drops the perigee into the atmosphere, and the stage flies through the plasma sheath, shedding roughly 7.8 km/s of orbital velocity while the shield surface reaches around 1,500 °C.
  7. Fly the terminal descent. Thrusters settle the propellant to the bottom of the tanks, guidance steers to the recovery point, and the engines relight at low throttle for a controlled touchdown or a mid-air catch.
  8. Recover, inspect and refly. Engineers inspect tiles, engines and structure, replace what is damaged, and return the stage to the pad — the cost of this turnaround decides whether recovery pays.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Reusable high-temperature heat shields — ablative shields survive one reentry only; a reusable shield must take many cycles with minimal repair, which has proven difficult since the Shuttle era.
  • EXISTS Propulsive landing of large rockets — boosters have landed and refly routinely since 2015, but they reenter at far lower speed than an orbital stage.
  • EXISTS Restartable, deep-throttling cryogenic engines — a landing burn needs multiple ignitions and precise low thrust.
  • PARTIAL Cryogenic propellant management in orbit — propellant must be settled and kept liquid during a long coast before the deorbit and landing burns.
  • PARTIAL Fast inspection and refurbishment — if a stage needs months of work between flights, the savings in hardware vanish.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: orbit-capable upper stages regularly reenter and make controlled soft splashdowns, while first-stage boosters land and refly — real flight data on reentry loads, tile performance and terminal guidance.
  2. Next: subscale demonstrators prove vertical takeoff and landing of a second-stage-shaped vehicle — confidence that a full-size upper stage can be flown to a pinpoint landing.
  3. Next: first intact recovery and reflight of an orbital-class upper stage — a genuine path to a fully reusable launch stack, cutting the hardware cost per flight.
  4. Longer term: routine reuse with short turnaround and orbital refuelling — launch cost dominated by propellant and operations, supporting large-scale space infrastructure.
