PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

A mass driver is a linear accelerator that launches payloads from a planetary surface or an airless moon without chemical propellant. On the Moon or an asteroid it can reach orbital velocity outright, while on Earth it can serve as a reusable first stage.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

A long track of pulsed coils or rails accelerates a sled carrying the payload, which separates at the muzzle and continues ballistically or under its own power. Efficient pulsed power, track wear and the length required in atmosphere are the limiting factors.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Pulsed power supply — delivers enormous bursts of current in milliseconds, then recharges between shots. Key parts: capacitor banks or flywheel/homopolar generators, high-current switches, on-site generation or grid connection.
  • Accelerator track — converts that current into a travelling force on the sled. Key parts: coil segments (coilgun) or continuous rails (railgun), superconducting coils if cryogenic cooling is used, support and alignment structure.
  • Armature and payload bucket — the moving part that carries the payload and releases it cleanly at the muzzle. Key parts: conducting armature, lightweight sabot/bucket, release mechanism.
  • Guideway — keeps the sled on a straight, precisely aimed path. Key parts: evacuated tube for atmospheric use, bare track on an airless body, aiming and thermal-expansion joints, pylons or tunnel.
  • Payload vehicle — the cargo itself, which must circularise its orbit after release. Key parts: small thruster or kick stage, thermal protection if released in atmosphere, guidance and telemetry.
  • Control and timing — synchronises switching with sled position to keep acceleration smooth. Key parts: position sensors, real-time controllers, diagnostics and wear monitoring.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the site. An airless, low-gravity body such as the Moon allows a bare track aimed near the equator; on Earth the track must sit in an evacuated tube, ideally on high ground, because air drag and heating grow with the square of speed.
  2. Build the guideway. Pylons or a tunnel carry a straight track aligned to millimetre tolerances over kilometres, with joints that absorb day–night thermal movement.
  3. Lay the accelerator. Coil segments or rails are installed along the whole length in modules, so a failed section can be replaced without rebuilding the line.
  4. Build the power plant. Capacitor banks, switches and generators sized to the shot energy are installed at the breech end, with enough generation or storage to recharge between launches.
  5. Make the sled. A light armature is bonded to a bucket that holds the payload rigidly under high acceleration.
  6. Test sub-scale. Short prototype tracks fire instrumented slugs to measure force, alignment and wear without risking a payload.
  7. Creep up in speed. Successive shots raise muzzle velocity, revealing heating, arcing and rail erosion before they become failures.
  8. Release and circularise. At the muzzle the payload separates and fires a small stage to raise its perigee, so the ballistic arc becomes a stable orbit.
  9. Operate and maintain. Launches are spaced to allow cooling, track servicing and recharging, which sets the practical launch cadence.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-energy pulsed power and switching — capacitor banks and switches exist, but the combination of energy density, repetition rate and lifetime needed for routine launches does not.
  • PARTIAL Electromagnetic launchers at scale — naval electromagnetic catapults and laboratory railguns demonstrate the principle, but nothing has reached orbital velocity outside a vacuum chamber experiment.
  • EXISTS Superconducting magnets and cryogenics — they make coilgun tracks more efficient, though they add cooling mass to the system.
  • PARTIAL Large evacuated tubes — vacuum systems exist at kilometre scale in particle accelerators, but not as cheap, reusable launch infrastructure.
  • EXISTS Payloads tolerant of high acceleration — the launcher suits bulk cargo, propellant and raw materials far more than people or delicate instruments.
  • EXISTS Orbital circularisation stages — something must add the few hundred metres per second needed to turn the launch arc into an orbit.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: electromagnetic catapults and laboratory launchers prove force and control — confidence that a long track can accelerate a heavy sled.
  2. Next: small-scale demonstrators — a short lunar prototype or an Earth-based suborbital shot — real data on wear, switching and vacuum operation.
  3. Next: a lunar launcher delivering cargo to low lunar orbit — routine delivery of water, propellant and structural material off the Moon, where orbital speed is about 1.7 km/s and escape about 2.4 km/s.
  4. Longer term: an Earth launch-assist system, or a full lunar export facility feeding orbital industry — very cheap bulk freight, at the cost of a track hundreds of kilometres long and enormous pulsed power.
