4How It Works

Crop chambers fix carbon dioxide into biomass while releasing oxygen; urine and grey water pass through biological and physicochemical processing steps, with loop mass balance tuned to the crew's metabolic load.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Crop growth chamber — grows edible plants that simultaneously fix carbon dioxide, release oxygen and produce food. Key parts: sealed growth racks with pressure and humidity control, high-efficiency LED arrays, hydroponic or aeroponic nutrient delivery, root-zone and canopy sensors.
  • Air revitalisation loop — keeps oxygen and carbon dioxide within safe limits by cycling gases between crew, plants and machines. Key parts: CO2 adsorption or membrane separation, Sabatier reactor to make methane and water from CO2 and hydrogen, water electrolysis for oxygen, trace-contaminant filters.
  • Algae or photobioreactor — a compact, high-rate gas exchanger that also yields protein-rich biomass. Key parts: illuminated bioreactor vessels, gas exchange and sparging, harvesting and dewatering.
  • Water recovery loop — reclaims urine, condensate and grey water back to potable standard. Key parts: urine distillation or forward osmosis, catalytic oxidation, ion exchange and activated carbon beds, microbial monitoring.
  • Waste and nutrient recycling — returns plant residue, packaging and human waste to usable plant nutrients. Key parts: biological digester or composting reactor, wet-oxidation or incineration step, nutrient dosing into the hydroponic solution.
  • Environmental control and monitoring — holds temperature, humidity, pressure and light cycles steady and tracks the loop mass balance. Key parts: sensor suite (O2, CO2, humidity, trace gases), mass-flow controllers, control software and leak detection.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the loop mass balance. Work out how much oxygen, water and food each crew member needs per day — of the order of a kilogram of oxygen and a few kilograms of water — then size every loop so nothing accumulates or runs short over months.
  2. Build sealed growth chambers. Assemble pressure-tight racks with LED arrays delivering the high light levels crops need, plus hydroponic channels and root-zone irrigation.
  3. Select the crop mix. Choose species that combine high edible yield, quick harvest, and easy processing — wheat, soy, potato, sweet potato and leafy greens are the usual candidates — and stagger planting so harvests overlap.
  4. Install the air loop. Connect CO2 scrubbers, a Sabatier reactor and an electrolyser to the plant chambers so carbon moves from crew to plants to food and back, with buffers for night-time when plants stop photosynthesising.
  5. Build the water loop. Add urine processing, condensate collection and polishing beds, with sampling to confirm water meets drinking standards.
  6. Close the solid-waste loop. Feed inedible plant mass and human waste through biological or chemical oxidation to recover nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium for the nutrient solution.
  7. Integrate control and monitoring. Link all loops to a model of the whole system so operators can see where mass is accumulating and respond to failures such as a contaminated crop or a stalled bioreactor.
  8. Test with a crew in a sealed analog. Run multi-month closed-chamber trials with people inside, measuring how much resupply mass is actually avoided and where the loops leak.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-efficiency horticultural lighting — crops need far more light than sunlight-free habitats can supply any other way, and waste heat must be rejected.
  • EXISTS Physicochemical life-support systems — the ISS already recycles water and generates oxygen chemically, providing the machinery the biological loops are built around.
  • PARTIAL Reliable plant growth in reduced gravity — plants do grow in orbit, but yield, seed-to-seed cycles and root-zone behaviour under microgravity and partial gravity are still being proven.
  • PARTIAL Stable, safe microbial and bioreactor systems — biological reactors must run for years without drifting into unusable or hazardous states.
  • PARTIAL Food safety and crew acceptability — crops grown in recycled waste streams must be verifiably safe and palatable enough that crews actually eat them.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: small plant-growth facilities on orbit and closed ground chambers such as ESA's MELiSSA work and Russia's BIOS-3 experiments — data on crop yields, gas exchange rates and crew interaction with plant systems.
  2. Next: multi-loop ground demonstration with a crew sealed in for months to a year — evidence that air, water and food loops can be integrated and controlled together.
  3. Next: plant modules attached to a lunar surface habitat — partial closure of life support where resupply is expensive, plus experience with reduced gravity and dust.
  4. Longer term: transit and surface habitats with high loop closure — crewed missions far from Earth, where resupply mass is the binding constraint.
