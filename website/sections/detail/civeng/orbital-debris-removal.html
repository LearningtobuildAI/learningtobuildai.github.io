1Definition

Active removal of large defunct satellites and spent rocket bodies is the highest-leverage action against runaway collisional cascades in low Earth orbit, where a single breakup can seed thousands of trackable fragments.

4How It Works

A servicer rendezvouses with a tumbling object, captures it with a robotic arm, net or harpoon, and either deorbits it or raises it to a graveyard orbit, all within a strictly limited propellant budget.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Servicer spacecraft bus — carries the capture hardware to the target and survives years in low Earth orbit. Key parts: solar arrays and batteries, propulsion (electric and chemical), star trackers and reaction wheels, flight computer.
  • Rendezvous and proximity navigation — closes the gap from tens of kilometres to metres without ground intervention at the end. Key parts: ground radar tracking and orbit determination, optical cameras and LiDAR, relative navigation software, collision-avoidance logic.
  • Capture mechanism — grapples an object that was never designed to be caught. Key parts: robotic arm or tentacles, net or harpoon (single-shot), magnetic or mechanical docking plate (only if the target carries one).
  • Target characterisation — works out the object's shape, mass distribution and tumble before touching it. Key parts: onboard imaging and 3D reconstruction, spin-rate and axis estimation, thermal and lighting models.
  • Deorbit or disposal module — provides the velocity change that drops the object's perigee into the atmosphere. Key parts: high-thrust chemical engine and propellant tanks, drag sail or electrodynamic tether alternatives, separation and release mechanism.
  • Ground segment — tracks everything in orbit, plans the approach and manages licensing and liability. Key parts: space-surveillance catalogues, conjunction-assessment tools, mission control and telemetry, regulatory and insurance interfaces.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick and track a target. Choose a large, high-risk object — typically a defunct satellite or a spent upper stage of several tonnes — and track it for months to build an accurate orbit and brightness curve.
  2. Design the servicer around it. Mass, inertia, spin state and any usable attachment feature determine capture method, propellant load and structural strength.
  3. Fabricate the spacecraft. Build the bus, install avionics, propulsion and sensors, and integrate the capture mechanism.
  4. Test on the ground. Air-bearing tables and flat floors rehearse proximity manoeuvres; nets and harpoons are fired at representative targets; the whole spacecraft goes through thermal-vacuum and vibration testing.
  5. Launch and commission. Ride-share to a nearby orbit, then deploy solar arrays and check sensors, typically over several weeks.
  6. Far-range rendezvous. Ground navigation brings the servicer within a few tens of kilometres; onboard cameras take over and the distance is closed cautiously, with planned retreats if anything is uncertain.
  7. Inspection and tumble matching. The servicer holds station a few tens of metres away, builds a 3D model, then slowly matches the target's rotation so the two are effectively still relative to each other.
  8. Capture. A robotic arm grasps a structural feature, or a net or harpoon secures the body; the combined stack is then stabilised and its spin damped.
  9. Disposal. The servicer burns to lower the target's perigee to roughly 200–300 km so it re-enters within about 25 years, then either rides down with it or releases it and moves to the next target — and itself disposes safely at end of life.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Space surveillance and tracking catalogues — you cannot remove what you cannot find and re-find; radar and optical networks already track objects down to about 10 cm in low Earth orbit.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous relative navigation — the final metres must be flown faster than ground commands can loop, and no servicer has yet docked with a large unprepared object unattended.
  • PARTIAL Capture of unprepared tumbling bodies — nets and harpoons have flown in demonstrations, but none has yet secured a multi-tonne derelict and deorbited it.
  • EXISTS High-delta-v, low-cost propulsion — removing a heavy object from low Earth orbit costs hundreds of metres per second of velocity change; that drives the size and price of the servicer.
  • PARTIAL Re-entry survivability modelling — a large object released to re-enter may not burn up completely, so casualty risk has to be calculated and mitigated.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: commercial servicers flying close-approach demonstrations against real derelicts — proof that rendezvous and inspection of an uncooperative object works.
  2. Next: first funded removal of a single large object — capturing it and lowering its orbit — a demonstrated unit cost per object removed.
  3. Next: multi-target 'sweeper' missions carrying several deorbit kits to successive objects, or magnetic docking plates fitted to new satellites before launch — removal at a cost per kilogram that operators could plausibly pay.
  4. Longer term: routine removal funded by a fee on every satellite launched, targeting the largest derelicts first — stabilising the low-orbit population before collisions outpace the natural decay of debris.
