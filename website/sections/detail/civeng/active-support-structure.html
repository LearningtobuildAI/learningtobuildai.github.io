PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Active support replaces passive material strength with controlled forces, allowing structures far larger than any material could otherwise hold. Compression members are held by thrown or recirculated mass streams and tension members by rapidly moving cables.

4How It Works

A stream of pellets or a loop of cable circulates through a sleeve, deflecting at each end and transferring momentum into the structure, so the member behaves as though it had enormous strength. Control bandwidth must be high enough to damp oscillation, making this a control problem rather than a materials one.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Mass stream or rotor loop — the moving momentum carrier — its kinetic energy, not the strength of a static member, holds the structure up. Key parts: pellets or a continuous composite rotor, guide sleeve, return path.
  • Accelerator and pump stations — keep the stream at speed, replacing energy lost to friction, deflection and any load it carries. Key parts: electromagnetic drive coils, linear motor sections, power conditioning.
  • Deflector and return stations — turn the stream around at each end and hand its momentum to the structure. Key parts: curved magnetic deflectors, structural arc, thrust-bearing interface.
  • Containment sleeve — evacuated or shielded tube that guides the stream and carries the attached payload — the visible structure. Key parts: segmented tube shell, joints, mounting hardpoints.
  • Control and damping system — senses stream position and phase and actively damps oscillation, so the whole assembly behaves as a stiff member. Key parts: position and phase sensors, fast actuators, real-time control computers.
  • Power and thermal system — supplies the megawatts to gigawatts the drives need and removes heat from magnets and bearings. Key parts: power supply, energy storage and ride-through, cooling loops.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the geometry and load. Choose the span, the altitude and the useful load, then work out the required stream speed and mass flow so that momentum transfer balances gravity or the applied force.
  2. Fabricate the sleeve. Make the containment tube in many identical sections, each stiff enough to hold its shape between supports but light enough that most of the load is carried by the stream rather than the shell.
  3. Build the end stations. At each end, assemble a curved deflector arc, its magnets and the bearing surface that transfers the stream's redirected momentum into the structure.
  4. Install the drives. Line the sleeve with electromagnetic pump sections and their power and cooling feeds, spaced so the stream never slows below the speed the design requires.
  5. Thread and spin up the stream. Feed the rotor or pellet stream into the sleeve, then raise its speed gradually with the drives, watching for the resonances that appear as it approaches operating velocity.
  6. Pump down and cool. Close and evacuate the tube — or, in orbit, fit shielding — and bring magnets and bearings to operating temperature.
  7. Tune the control loop. Measure stream position and phase at many points along the run, then tune the actuators and damping until oscillation stays bounded under the design loads.
  8. Load the structure. Attach the payload — tower sections, platforms or a launch track — and increase the load in steps, verifying that deflection and vibration stay within limits.
  9. Operate and maintain. Continuously replenish pellets or re-tension the rotor, replace worn deflector surfaces, and keep the drives running during power interruptions using stored energy.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-strength, low-density materials — carbon-fibre and steel rotors work in principle, but mass ratios decide whether the sleeve can hold together.
  • PARTIAL Electromagnetic launchers and linear motors — the principle is proven in maglev and accelerators; continuous high-duty-cycle drives at the needed scale are not.
  • EXISTS Vacuum and containment engineering — accelerator beam pipes and vacuum pipelines show how to hold a long evacuated channel; a megastructure-sized one has not been built.
  • PARTIAL High-bandwidth control and sensing — a stream moving at kilometres per second must be kept phased; active damping is demonstrated only at bench and maglev scale.
  • PARTIAL Space assembly and large power sources — an orbital version means building kilometres of structure in space and supplying gigawatts continuously.
  • PARTIAL Impact shielding for orbital versions — micrometeoroids and debris would puncture a thin vacuum tube unless it is armoured or segmented.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: bench demonstrations: electromagnetic deflection of fast-moving masses and active damping of flexible structures — confidence that momentum transfer can be controlled tightly enough to be useful.
  2. Next: a sealed test loop where a recirculating stream holds a load for hours — proof that the control loop keeps the stream stable under changing load.
  3. Next: a small free-flying demonstrator in orbit, a few hundred metres tall — experience with assembly, power and maintenance in vacuum.
  4. Longer term: a full-scale orbital ring or launch loop, thousands of kilometres long — low-cost access to orbit and a platform for very large structures — proposed concepts of this kind date from the early 1980s but remain paper designs.
