PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

An asteroid can be excavated and spun up so that its own mass supplies radiation shielding and gravity, avoiding the launch of structural material from Earth. The regolith becomes the habitat shell and the shielding at the same time.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The body is mined from the inside, the interior pressurised and sealed, and the asteroid spun so the inner surface experiences centripetal gravity. Spin-up needs either mass drivers throwing excavated rock or a tangential thrust phase, and the shell must be reinforced against the resulting tension.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Target body and anchoring — the asteroid itself is the raw material and the radiation shield; equipment must hold station on a body with almost no gravity. Key parts: rigid or net-like anchoring grips, harpoons or pitons for a loose surface, station-keeping thrusters.
  • Excavation and material handling — hollows out the interior and processes the spoil. Key parts: low-gravity cutting and drilling tools, crushers and conveyors, volatile extraction (water, carbon compounds).
  • Spin-up system — spins the body by throwing excavated rock or firing thrusters, and later trims the rate. Key parts: mass drivers or centrifugal launchers, tangential thrusters, attitude control, tether or cable tension members.
  • Pressure shell and structure — makes the cavity gas-tight and takes the loads from spin and internal pressure. Key parts: sealed liner (metal, composite or cast basalt), hoop reinforcement cables or wound bands, airlocks and bulkheads.
  • Interior fit-out — turns a cavity into habitable volume. Key parts: decks and floors stacked along the spin axis, lighting and thermal control, water and food production areas.
  • Power and life support — keeps people alive and the shell pressurised for years. Key parts: solar arrays or fission reactors, closed-loop air and water recycling, regenerative food growing.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Find and characterise a body. Telescopes and a rendezvous probe map shape, spin state, internal structure and composition. Most small near-Earth asteroids are loose rubble piles rather than solid rock, which changes every later step.
  2. Rendezvous and anchor. In near-zero gravity, mining equipment cannot simply sit on the surface; it must be bolted, netted or thrust-clamped to the body, or operate from a free-flying platform alongside it.
  3. Mine the interior. Tunnels are cut inward and the excavated rock is stockpiled. If the body is a weak aggregate, tunnelling may be replaced by containing and compacting it rather than carving it.
  4. Build the pressure shell. A gas-tight liner is installed against the cavity walls and reinforced with tension members — cable hoops, wound bands or imported composite — because rock has almost no tensile strength.
  5. Spin it up slowly. Mass drivers throw spoil off tangentially, or thrusters apply torque, until the inner surface reaches the target gravity. Accidental tumbling must be damped throughout, and the shell must survive the loads.
  6. Pressurise and test. The cavity is filled with breathable gas at roughly half an atmosphere and leak-tested; internal pressure adds hoop stress comparable to the spin load on a shell this large.
  7. Fit out the interior. Decks, power, life support, workshops and growing areas are installed, with heavy equipment placed low and the axis left open for docking.
  8. Operate and maintain. Spin rate, gas composition, shielding thickness and structural strain are monitored continuously over decades; water and carbon are recycled from the remaining asteroid material.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Asteroid prospecting and mining — sample-return missions have touched and characterised asteroids, but nobody has yet extracted material and kept it working in space.
  • NOT YET Reliable low-gravity excavation and anchoring — drilling, holding position and moving spoil on a tumbling, near-weightless body is untested.
  • PARTIAL Large pressurised structures in space — space station modules exist at tens of metres, not the hundreds of metres needed for useful artificial gravity.
  • NOT YET Spin gravity at human-tolerable rates — anything above a couple of rotations per minute tends to make people motion-sick; at that rate, one g needs a radius of roughly 220 m.
  • PARTIAL Closed-loop life support — station systems already recycle most water and some air, but not food or long-term reliability over decades.
  • PARTIAL Multi-megawatt in-space power — running a habitat, mining tools and spin-up hardware needs far more power than current spacecraft generate.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: sample-return missions to near-Earth asteroids (Hayabusa2 in 2020, OSIRIS-REx in 2023) — ground truth on rubble-pile structure, volatiles and regolith behaviour.
  2. Next: robotic prospecting and small extraction demonstrations on a single asteroid — proven anchoring, digging and volatile processing in microgravity.
  3. Next: an uncrewed test cavity — mined, lined, sealed and pressurised at a few metres scale, possibly as a free-flying structure built from asteroid material rather than a hollowed body — real data on sealing, leak rates and hoop loads before people are involved.
  4. Longer term: a crewed outpost inside a small spun body at partial gravity, then a full one-g habitat a few hundred metres across — settlement volume whose shielding and structure were never launched from Earth — if the tensile-strength and life-support problems can be solved.
