PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Server racks placed in low Earth orbit receive near-continuous solar power and reject waste heat by radiation rather than water evaporation, avoiding land, water and grid constraints on Earth. Early spacecraft have already flown GPUs and returned results to ground stations.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Compute payload — racks of processors and GPUs running jobs in orbit, either autonomously or on commands from the ground. Key parts: radiation-tolerant server boards, GPUs and accelerators, error-correcting memory and watchdogs, shielding and cold plates.
  • Power system — generate electricity from sunlight, since there is no grid. Key parts: solar arrays with sun tracking, batteries for eclipse periods, power conditioning and distribution.
  • Thermal control — reject all waste heat by radiation, because vacuum has no air to carry it away. Key parts: deployable radiator panels, heat pipes or pumped fluid loops, surface coatings that emit well in infrared.
  • Communications — move data in and results out. Key parts: optical inter-satellite terminals, downlink to ground stations, radio command and telemetry.
  • Spacecraft bus — hold everything in the right attitude and keep the orbit stable. Key parts: structure and separation interface, attitude control (reaction wheels, thrusters), star trackers and orbit determination.
  • Ground segment — schedule jobs, collect results, and keep the spacecraft healthy. Key parts: mission operations centre, ground station network, job scheduler and data pipeline.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose an orbit. A dawn-dusk Sun-synchronous orbit keeps the craft in near-continuous sunlight and holds the radiators permanently facing away from the Sun, which is what makes the physics work.
  2. Size the power and thermal systems together. Sunlight at Earth is about 1,360 W/m²; at realistic array efficiency and packing, tens of kilowatts means hundreds of square metres of solar panel. Each kilowatt of that power becomes waste heat that must be radiated: at a few hundred kelvin a radiator emits roughly 400 W per square metre of surface, so radiator area ends up comparable to array area.
  3. Select and harden the electronics. Low Earth orbit sits below the worst radiation belts, but cosmic rays and the South Atlantic Anomaly still cause single-event upsets. Parts are chosen for tolerance, shielded where mass allows, and paired with error-correcting memory, watchdogs and periodic reboots.
  4. Build the spacecraft bus. Structure, attitude control, batteries and optical terminals are integrated and tested separately from the compute payload.
  5. Integrate the racks. Servers are mounted with vibration isolation, wired for power and data, and their heat is coupled into the fluid loop that feeds the radiators.
  6. Test on the ground. Thermal-vacuum chambers reproduce the vacuum and temperature swings, shake tables reproduce launch loads, and the whole system is run end-to-end against a ground station.
  7. Launch and commission. The craft rideshares to orbit, deploys its arrays and radiators, and is checked out for weeks before taking real work.
  8. Operate and dispose. Jobs are scheduled around downlink windows, software is updated in flight, and the satellite is deorbited or moved to a graveyard orbit at end of life rather than left as debris.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Radiation-tolerant high-performance computing — commercial processors and GPUs fly today in experimental payloads, but error rates rise sharply with performance and time in orbit.
  • PARTIAL High-bandwidth optical downlinks — laser terminals have demonstrated hundreds of gigabits per second from small satellites; the bottleneck becomes the number and location of ground stations.
  • PARTIAL Deployable radiators and two-phase cooling loops — these are proven on crewed stations and large satellites, but scaling them into the hundreds of kilowatts a serious data centre would need is unproven.
  • EXISTS Cheap, frequent launch to low Earth orbit — without low launch cost, the mass of radiators and solar arrays for each kilowatt of compute is prohibitive.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous operation and job scheduling — with only intermittent ground contact, the payload must manage failures and workloads without a human in the loop.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: GPU payloads flown on small satellites, with results returned to ground stations — proof that off-the-shelf compute can survive launch and run in orbit.
  2. Next: dedicated compute satellites of tens of kilowatts with laser crosslinks — useful niches where the data is already in orbit — processing Earth-observation imagery before downlinking it instead of sending the raw pixels.
  3. Next: clusters of compute satellites sharing work over an inter-satellite mesh — redundancy and the ability to run larger models than one spacecraft can host.
  4. Longer term: orbital capacity large enough to compete with terrestrial data centres on cost per job — a genuine option for compute in regions where land, water or grid connection are the binding constraint — if launch and radiator mass fall far enough.
