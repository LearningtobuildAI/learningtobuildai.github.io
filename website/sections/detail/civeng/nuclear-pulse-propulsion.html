PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Nuclear pulse propulsion detonates small nuclear devices behind a pusher plate to reach velocities no chemical rocket can approach. It remains the highest specific impulse concept that relies only on demonstrated physics and existing materials.

4How It Works

A magazine of shaped charges is ejected and detonated at a fixed standoff distance, and the resulting plasma and radiation momentum is absorbed by a massive plate coupled to the vehicle through shock absorbers. Momentum transfer per pulse sets thrust, and the plate rather than the device is the engineering bottleneck.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Pusher plate and shock absorbers — the actual engine: it converts the blast of each detonation into a smooth push. Key parts: thick steel or aluminium plate tens of metres across, spray-on ablative oil coating, pneumatic or hydraulic shock absorbers, thrust ring tied into the vehicle spine.
  • Pulse units — metered nuclear charges shaped to throw most of their energy rearward at the plate rather than sideways. Key parts: fission primary, fusion-boosted stage, channel filler that turns to directed plasma, casing and standoff hardware.
  • Magazine and ejection system — stores the charges and fires them out to a fixed standoff distance at a steady rate. Key parts: armoured magazine, ejection gun, timing and safety interlocks.
  • Thrust vectoring and attitude control — keeps the plate square to the thrust axis and steers by offsetting pulses or using small thrusters. Key parts: pulse-timing controller, vernier thrusters, gimballed ejection mount.
  • Shadow shield and crew/payload protection — stops neutrons and gamma rays from reaching people and electronics. Key parts: shadow shield between magazine and crew, water or polyethylene layer, radiation-hardened avionics.
  • Thermal and structural spine — ties plate, magazine and payload together and dumps the heat each pulse deposits. Key parts: truss frame, radiators, flash-resistant cabling.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the mission budget. Choose a target velocity change, then work out how many pulses and how much mass that implies. Unlike a chemical rocket, thrust is set by pulse rate and plate size, not by a combustion chamber.
  2. Design the pulse unit. Size a small device of roughly 0.1–1 kilotonne yield and surround it with a heavy filler so the energy leaves as a narrow plasma jet aimed at the plate.
  3. Build the pusher plate. Fabricate a very thick steel or aluminium disc and mount it on long-stroke shock absorbers. These must stretch a millisecond impulse of thousands of tonnes of force into an acceleration the structure and crew can survive.
  4. Assemble magazine and ejection gun. Build an armoured magazine that feeds units to a gun firing them rearward about once per second, with interlocks that prevent a detonation too close to the plate.
  5. Add shielding and control. Fit a shadow shield on the vehicle axis so the plate itself blocks line-of-sight radiation, and install the timing computer that keeps pulses centred.
  6. Validate with conventional explosives. Test the plate and shock absorbers using chemical charges, as the Project Orion studies did in 1959. This proves the mechanical coupling without any nuclear yield.
  7. Test a low-yield pulse in space. Flight validation requires detonating a device in vacuum — which the 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty and 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibit, so no such test has ever been flown.
  8. Assemble in orbit and fly. Because a pulse vehicle is enormous, sections would be launched separately and joined in orbit before the first pulse is fired.
  9. Scale to a deep-space or interstellar precursor. With the plate and pulse unit proven, thrust scales with pulse rate and yield, giving velocities of thousands of kilometres per second.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Compact nuclear devices with shaped, directed output — nuclear devices are mature, but shaping most of their energy into a narrow plasma jet has only been studied on paper.
  • PARTIAL Shock absorbers for repeated huge impulses — gas-damped and hydraulic systems tested on the ground show the principle works, but nothing has run for thousands of pulses.
  • PARTIAL Lightweight ablative plate coatings — each pulse vaporises a thin layer of oil, so the plate is consumed slowly over the mission and needs a resupply of coating.
  • EXISTS Instrumentation and control for rapid pulse timing — firing something every second for months and keeping the thrust axis true demands reliable high-rate control electronics.
  • NOT YET Legal regime permitting nuclear detonations in space — treaties bar nuclear explosions in orbit and in space, so the concept cannot be flight-tested as the law stands.
  • PARTIAL Heavy-lift launch and orbital assembly — a useful vehicle masses thousands of tonnes, far beyond any single launcher, so it must be assembled in orbit.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: paper studies and small-scale conventional-explosive tests of plate and shock absorbers — verified models of how a flat plate responds to a plasma impulse.
  2. Next: uncrewed pulse concepts that replace bombs with small magnetically compressed plasma rings — a far lower-yield, repeatable pulse that is easier to test and to control.
  3. Next: a deep-space robotic demonstrator firing pulses outside protected orbits — real flight data on thrust, ablation and structural fatigue.
  4. Longer term: crewed interstellar precursor moving at a few thousand kilometres per second — reach to the outer solar system in months and to nearby stars over decades — if the legal and material barriers can be cleared.
