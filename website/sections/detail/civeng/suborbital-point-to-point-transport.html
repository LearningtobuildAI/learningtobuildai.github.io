4How It Works

A vehicle launches onto a ballistic or depressed trajectory, coasts through near-vacuum for the majority of the flight and lands at a distant spaceport; thermal protection, acoustic loading and range safety drive the design.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Reusable launch vehicle — accelerates the payload to near-orbital velocity, coasts above the atmosphere, then lands under power. Key parts: propellant tanks holding liquid methane and liquid oxygen, rocket engines, reaction-control thrusters, grid fins and aerodynamic surfaces, landing legs or a catch arrangement.
  • Thermal protection system — survives re-entry heating that is close to what an orbital vehicle sees, because the coast is near-orbital. Key parts: ceramic heat-shield tiles, ablative backing layers, attachment and inspection system.
  • Payload cabin or cargo bay — keeps people or freight alive and intact through several g of acceleration, vacuum and vibration. Key parts: pressurised shell, acceleration couches or cargo mounts, life support and air supply, galley and toilet for short flights.
  • Point-to-point ground infrastructure — two distant sites must each launch, catch or land, refuel and service the vehicle quickly. Key parts: launch mount and hold-down, propellant storage and loading arms, landing pad or catch tower, crane and servicing equipment.
  • Range, tracking and abort systems — keeps the corridor clear and the vehicle under control over thousands of kilometres of ocean and land. Key parts: tracking radar and telemetry, flight-termination system, airspace and maritime coordination, weather monitoring.
  • Operations and passenger handling — turns a rocket flight into a repeatable service, from ticketing to boarding to turnaround. Key parts: terminal and boarding bridge, baggage or freight handling, inspection and refurbishment workflow.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Size the vehicle to the route. A near-antipodal hop of around 17,000 km needs the vehicle to reach close to orbital velocity, about 7–8 km/s, so it must be a fully reusable super-heavy rocket rather than a small sounding rocket.
  2. Design for re-entry from near-orbital speed. The heat shield is sized for a much harsher entry than a suborbital tourism hop, which drives the whole vehicle's mass and shape.
  3. Build and fly the reusable stack. Stainless-steel or composite tanks are welded, engines installed, and the vehicle flown repeatedly to prove that landing and inspection can be done cheaply.
  4. Demonstrate uncrewed point-to-point delivery. Freight or instrumented payloads are flown between two distant pads to measure landing accuracy, structural loads, noise and turnaround time before any passengers are carried. Military logistics studies have examined exactly this use.
  5. Qualify human factors and escape. Seats, restraint, acceleration limits, cabin pressure loss, emergency egress and abort options are tested and certified, since no civil airworthiness code yet covers a rocket airliner.
  6. Adapt or build the ground network. Sites must sit far enough from cities to limit noise and blast risk, yet close enough to be useful, with propellant supply at both ends and offshore platforms as one option.
  7. Set up the regulatory route. Aviation-style certification, overflight permissions across many countries and a traffic-management regime for high-altitude vehicles are needed before tickets can be sold.
  8. Operate a first route. One city pair is flown on a schedule with high-tempo reuse, and cost per seat is driven down by flying each vehicle many times a year.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Fully reusable super-heavy launch vehicles — boosters have landed and reflown, but rapid, low-cost reuse of an entire stack is still being proven; the economics of the concept depend on it completely.
  • PARTIAL Thermal protection that survives near-orbital re-entry repeatedly — reusable heat shields work, but tiles still need careful inspection and refurbishment between flights.
  • PARTIAL Precision propulsive landing at a distant site — the vehicle must arrive within metres of a pad after a ballistic arc of thousands of kilometres.
  • NOT YET Very high launch cadence — global launch rates are measured in hundreds per year, while an airline-like service needs many flights per site per day.
  • NOT YET Passenger certification and airspace rules for rockets — no regulator has an airworthiness framework for paying passengers on a suborbital transport flight.
  • NOT YET Quiet, city-adjacent spaceports — launch and sonic-boom noise currently rule out most sites near the populations that would use the service.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: reusable super-heavy vehicles in flight test and government studies of rocket-delivered cargo — data on landing accuracy, reuse cost and turnaround time.
  2. Next: uncrewed point-to-point flights between two fixed pads, carrying freight or instruments — proof that the trajectory, thermal loads and ground operations work end to end.
  3. Next: a crewed demonstration on one route, with test crew and no paying public — human-rating evidence and a first regulatory precedent.
  4. Longer term: a certified commercial route, most likely for time-critical freight and premium passengers — a niche service that can grow only if cost per flight falls far enough to compete with jets.
