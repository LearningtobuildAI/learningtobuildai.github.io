PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

A propellant depot is a tanker station in low Earth orbit or at a Lagrange point that stores cryogenic propellant and transfers it to visiting spacecraft. It converts launch capacity into deep-space delta-v without redesigning any single vehicle.

4How It Works

Liquid hydrogen, oxygen or methane is stored under multilayer insulation and, for long durations, active cryocooling to limit boil-off. Docking, fluid transfer in microgravity and gauging without settling are the hard parts, and they are being demonstrated on orbit now.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cryogenic tanks — hold liquid oxygen, methane or hydrogen at tens of kelvin for months without venting the propellant away. Key parts: aluminium-lithium or composite pressure shells, internal baffles and slosh control, vapour-cooled shields.
  • Thermal control — fight the ~300 K environment around the tanks. Key parts: multilayer insulation blankets, sunshade or radiator panels, active cryocooler (reverse turbo-Brayton type), boil-off reliquefaction loop.
  • Fluid management in microgravity — make liquid behave without gravity, so it sits over the outlet and can be measured. Key parts: propellant management devices or liquid acquisition screens, ullage/settling thrusters, pressure control, mass gauging sensors.
  • Transfer interface — connects depot to tanker and to the customer vehicle. Key parts: cryogenic quick-disconnect couplings, chill-down and purge lines, leak detection, autonomous docking mechanism.
  • Avionics and station-keeping — holds the depot in a stable orbit and runs the transfer autonomously. Key parts: guidance and navigation, reboost thrusters, thermal and pressure control computer.
  • Resupply logistics — the tankers and ground operations that keep the depot filled. Key parts: reusable tanker upper stages, launch site cryogenic loading, mission planning.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the tank and thermal shield. Engineers size tank volume against the boil-off budget over the mission life, trading extra insulation and cryocooler power against launch mass.
  2. Fabricate the tank. Shells are spun or welded from lightweight alloy or wound composite, then proof-tested and cleaned for oxygen service.
  3. Fit internal fluid management. Propellant management devices, baffles and gauging sensors are installed inside the tank before closure.
  4. Apply insulation. Multilayer insulation blankets and, where needed, sprayed foam and vapour-cooled shields are wrapped around the tank.
  5. Integrate cryocoolers and plumbing. Cold heads, reliquefaction lines, fill and drain couplings and vent valves are attached and leak-checked.
  6. Launch. The depot flies to orbit as a payload, typically empty or lightly loaded, inside a launcher fairing or as part of a stage.
  7. Check out on orbit. Solar arrays and the sunshade deploy, the cryocooler is commissioned and the depot holds station while awaiting a tanker.
  8. Transfer propellant. A tanker docks, lines are chilled down to prevent flashing, a small thrust settles the liquid over the outlet, and fluid is pumped or pressure-transferred across.
  9. Serve a customer. A departing spacecraft docks, takes on propellant and departs; the depot is topped up by later tanker flights.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Reusable heavy-lift launch — filling a depot takes many tanker flights, so cheap reflight is what makes the economics work.
  • EXISTS Autonomous rendezvous and docking — depots must be met repeatedly without a crew aboard.
  • PARTIAL Cryogenic fluid management in microgravity — settling liquid, gauging mass and transferring without loss has only been shown at small scale.
  • PARTIAL Long-life space cryocoolers — zero-boil-off storage needs coolers that run for years at tens of watts of lift.
  • NOT YET Standard refuelling interfaces — without agreed couplings and protocols, each spacecraft needs a bespoke depot.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: in-orbit refuelling and fluid-transfer demonstrations — confidence that docking, chilling and transferring liquids works in microgravity.
  2. Next: large-scale cryogenic transfer between two spacecraft — the basis for the Artemis lunar-lander architecture, which needs orbital refuelling.
  3. Next: a persistently stored depot in low Earth orbit — routine departure of loaded spacecraft to higher orbits and the Moon.
  4. Longer term: depots at cislunar or Lagrange points — reusable deep-space tugs and lower cost per kilogram to Mars and beyond.
