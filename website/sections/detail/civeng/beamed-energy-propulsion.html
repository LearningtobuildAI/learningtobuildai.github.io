PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Beamed propulsion uses a ground-based or orbital laser or microwave array to push a vehicle or lightsail, so the energy source stays behind and the craft carries no propellant. It is the only near-term route to gram-scale probes at a fraction of light speed.

4How It Works

A phased array focuses coherent light onto a reflective sail or a heat-exchanger thrust plate, and the beam is held on target by active tracking. Diffraction sets the maximum useful range for a given aperture, which is why very large ground arrays and extremely thin sails go together.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Beam source — generates the coherent light or microwaves that carry the energy. Key parts: high-power laser or microwave emitters, power conditioning and energy storage, cooling system.
  • Phased array and beam director — combines many emitters into a single steerable beam and holds it on target. Key parts: phase modulators, adaptive optics, large aperture mirrors or antenna elements, pointing and tracking mounts.
  • Sail or thrust plate — receives the beam and converts it into thrust, either by photon pressure or by heating a propellant. Key parts: ultra-thin reflective membrane (lightsail), heat exchanger and propellant injection (thermal mode), support structure and tensioning.
  • Vehicle — carries the payload and maintains attitude so the sail stays facing the beam. Key parts: lightweight structure, attitude control thrusters or reaction wheels, communications and power, payload or instrument.
  • Tracking and control — senses the sail's position and feeds corrections to the beam director. Key parts: radar or lidar, optical cameras, real-time feedback computer.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Build the beam array. Many laser or microwave emitters are mounted side by side and phase-locked so their beams add up coherently into one powerful beam.
  2. Fabricate the sail. A reflective film just tens of nanometres thick is deposited on a lightweight frame, then tensioned and packaged for launch.
  3. Integrate power and cooling. The array needs massive electrical power and cooling; capacitors or flywheels store energy for short pulses if the source is pulsed.
  4. Test in a vacuum chamber. A small sail is suspended in vacuum and hit with a scaled-down beam to measure thrust, stability and beam-riding behaviour.
  5. Demonstrate beam-riding. For launch, a small craft is pushed by a ground beam through the lower atmosphere; for space sails, a beam from a ground station propels a satellite in low orbit.
  6. Scale to operational use. The array is enlarged and the sail made lighter until the system can launch payloads to orbit or accelerate a gram-scale probe to a fraction of light speed.
  7. Operate and track. During a mission, tracking radar or lidar keeps the beam centred on the sail, adjusting phase and pointing thousands of times per second.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-power, efficient lasers or microwave sources — lab and industrial lasers exist, but scaling to megawatts with high wall-plug efficiency remains a challenge.
  • PARTIAL Ultra-thin, low-mass reflective materials — thin films are made today, but they must survive launch and stay flat under beam pressure.
  • PARTIAL Phased array beam combining and adaptive optics — used in astronomy and defence, but not yet at the power and precision needed for propulsion.
  • PARTIAL Precision tracking and targeting — keeping a beam on a fast-moving sail requires radar/lidar and control loops far faster than current space-tracking systems.
  • EXISTS Lightweight spacecraft components — microelectronics and miniaturised instruments already exist, though they must be hardened for high-radiation or interstellar environments.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: laboratory demonstrations of laser sails and microwave thermal thrusters, plus pulsed-laser launch experiments — proof that a beam can push a small object in a controlled way.
  2. Next: orbital demonstration of beamed propulsion for small satellites, such as orbit raising or deorbiting — operation in space, where beam-riding and tracking can be tested without atmospheric interference.
  3. Next: ground-to-orbit launch of a small payload using a beamed thermal thruster — a reusable, propellant-free first stage that could cut launch costs.
  4. Longer term: large ground array accelerating a gram-scale lightsail to a fraction of light speed, as envisioned by concepts such as Breakthrough Starshot — interstellar probes able to reach nearby stars within a human lifetime.
