PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Solar power satellites harvest sunlight in geostationary or low orbit and beam it to rectenna arrays on the ground, delivering continuous baseload power unaffected by weather, night or season. The end product is dispatchable electricity rather than fuel.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Large photovoltaic or concentrating arrays feed microwave or laser transmitters aimed at kilometre-scale rectifying antennas. Efficiency across the atmosphere is limited by beam spreading and conversion losses, which is why designs favour geostationary geometry, very large apertures and short wavelengths.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Solar array — collects sunlight continuously in orbit, where there is no night, cloud or atmosphere. Key parts: lightweight photovoltaic blankets or concentrator panels, deployment booms, sun-tracking gimbals.
  • Power conversion and transmitter — converts DC electricity into a microwave beam and shapes it into a steerable aperture. Key parts: DC-to-RF amplifiers (solid-state or klystron/magnetron), phased-array antenna panel, waste-heat radiators.
  • Structure and attitude control — holds a structure kilometres across pointing precisely at one spot on Earth. Key parts: modular truss or inflatable frame, robotic assembly interfaces, thrusters and reaction wheels.
  • Beam control and safety — aims the beam at the receiver and proves it cannot wander onto people or aircraft. Key parts: pilot beam from the ground, retrodirective phase-conjugation electronics, beam-shutoff interlocks.
  • Rectenna and grid interface — converts the arriving microwave power back to DC electricity and feeds the grid. Key parts: kilometre-scale array of dipole elements, rectifying diodes, inverters and transformers, land and fencing.
  • Assembly and orbit infrastructure — gets thousands of tonnes of hardware from the ground into the right orbit and puts it together. Key parts: heavy-lift reusable launchers, orbital tugs, robotic arms and free-flyers.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Close the link budget. Calculate what fraction of sunlight ends up as grid electricity: array efficiency, DC-to-RF conversion, beam spreading over 35,786 km to geostationary orbit, atmospheric loss, and rectenna conversion. Typical designs land well under 20% end to end, which sets how vast the satellite must be.
  2. Make modular tiles. Rather than one giant machine, manufacture identical solar-and-transmitter panels on production lines, each able to be handled by a robot and plugged into a power and data bus.
  3. Launch the pieces. Heavy-lift reusable rockets would carry hundreds or thousands of tonnes of panels, trusses and cables to low orbit; the cost per kilogram, not the physics, is the main gate.
  4. Assemble in orbit. Robot arms or free-flying robots connect modules into a phased array and raise the finished satellite from low orbit to geostationary altitude, or assemble it there directly.
  5. Calibrate the beam. A pilot signal sent up from the rectenna lets the array measure and correct the phase of every element, so the beam focuses on the receiver instead of spreading.
  6. Build the rectenna. On the ground, lay out the dipole-and-diode array over many square kilometres — thin, mostly transparent to sunlight, so the land can often be shared with farming.
  7. Beam at low power first. Start at a small fraction of design power with aircraft and spectrum authorities monitoring, verifying that a failed array or drifting satellite leaves the beam defocused and harmless.
  8. Ramp up and connect. Raise power in steps as efficiency and safety are proven, then hand a steady, dispatchable supply to the grid like any baseload plant.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Very low launch cost (reusable heavy-lift) — a power satellite weighs tens of thousands of tonnes; today's launch prices make the electricity hopelessly expensive.
  • PARTIAL Robotic in-space assembly and servicing — space stations and servicing missions have joined modules robotically, but nothing kilometre-scale has been built autonomously.
  • PARTIAL High-efficiency microwave power transmission — microwave beaming has been demonstrated between ground stations and, in 2023, from a small satellite to Earth, but not at megawatt scale through the atmosphere.
  • PARTIAL Lightweight high-power solar arrays — arrays with far higher power per kilogram than today's are needed to keep launch mass down.
  • NOT YET Spectrum, orbital-slot and safety rules — power beaming at gigawatt levels crosses aviation, communications and radio-astronomy interests that no framework yet covers.
  • EXISTS Grid-scale rectenna sites and grid access — the receiving land is ordinary real estate and the grid connection is conventional — the constraint is public acceptance, not technology.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: component demonstrations: in-space wireless power transfer from a small satellite (2023) and ground-based microwave transmission tests — proof that the transmitter, beam steering and rectenna work outside the laboratory.
  2. Next: a dedicated demonstration satellite beaming kilowatts to a ground rectenna, with independent safety verification — real end-to-end efficiency numbers and a regulatory precedent.
  3. Next: robotically assembled megawatt-class platforms in low or geostationary orbit — in-space assembly, servicing and station-keeping experience at scale.
  4. Longer term: a pilot geostationary plant delivering hundreds of megawatts to one grid — a price and performance baseline for deciding whether fleets are worth building.
  5. Longest term: multiple plants supplying firm power to markets with poor renewables — continuous, weather-independent clean electricity — if launch cost keeps falling.
