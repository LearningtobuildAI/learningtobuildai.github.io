1Definition

A fission reactor generating electricity for high-power ion thrusters delivers far higher exhaust velocity than chemical rockets, enabling heavy cargo transfers across the inner solar system on reasonable timescales.

4How It Works

A compact reactor drives a closed Brayton or Rankine cycle that powers gridded ion or Hall-effect thrusters; low thrust applied continuously for months builds large velocity changes with a fraction of the propellant mass.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Reactor core — produces heat by fissioning uranium fuel. Key parts: uranium fuel elements (often high-assay low-enriched uranium), control drums or rods, beryllium reflector, pressure vessel.
  • Power conversion — turns reactor heat into electricity for the thrusters. Key parts: closed Brayton turbo-alternator-compressor (helium–xenon) or Rankine loop, recuperator and heat exchangers, magnetic bearings, alternator and power conditioning.
  • Heat rejection — dumps waste heat to space — the largest structure on the ship. Key parts: radiator panels, liquid coolant loops, pumps, deployable booms.
  • Electric thrusters — accelerate propellant ions to very high exhaust velocity. Key parts: gridded ion or Hall-effect thruster units, power processing units, gimbal mounts, neutraliser.
  • Propellant system — stores and meters the propellant the thrusters expel. Key parts: high-pressure tanks (xenon, krypton or argon), feed lines and valves, flow controllers.
  • Shielding and structure — keeps the reactor's neutrons and gamma rays away from payload and electronics. Key parts: shadow shield (e.g. lithium hydride and tungsten), truss separating reactor from payload, spacecraft bus.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Size the reactor and shield together. The design sets how much power is needed and how much shielding is required to keep the payload within radiation limits.
  2. Fabricate the fuel and core. Uranium fuel is clad and assembled with control drums and reflector into a compact core.
  3. Build the power conversion loop. A closed Brayton turbo-alternator on magnetic bearings is assembled and leak-tested with its helium–xenon working fluid.
  4. Make the thrusters. Ion optics grids or Hall thruster channels are machined and assembled, then tested on the ground with their power processing units.
  5. Assemble the spacecraft. The reactor is mounted on a boom with its shadow shield, radiators are deployed on booms, and propellant tanks and thrusters are attached.
  6. Ground-test the reactor. The core is run at full power in a vacuum chamber, as NASA's Kilopower/KRUSTY test did in 2018, to prove it works before flight.
  7. Launch shut down. The reactor flies subcritical and only starts once safely in space.
  8. Start up and commission. The core is brought to power, the conversion loop is warmed, and each thruster is fired and checked.
  9. Cruise on continuous thrust. The ship thrusts for months at low acceleration, spiralling out of Earth orbit and building up the velocity a chemical rocket could not afford.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Space fission reactors at hundreds of kilowatts — SNAP-10A flew in 1965 and Soviet TOPAZ reactors flew later, but at only a few kilowatts; Kilopower/KRUSTY proved a larger core on the ground.
  • PARTIAL High-power electric thrusters — ion and Hall thrusters have flown on Dawn, BepiColombo and Psyche at a few kilowatts; tens to hundreds of kilowatts have only been ground-tested.
  • NOT YET Lightweight, high-temperature radiators — rejecting megawatts of waste heat needs radiators far larger and lighter than anything flown.
  • NOT YET Long-life reactor and thruster materials — years of continuous operation demand components that resist erosion and radiation damage.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous long-duration control — months of unattended thrusting with minimal ground contact is beyond today's routine operations.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: fission reactors and ion thrusters have flown, but never together — the two core technologies are proven separately.
  2. Next: a flight demonstration of a 100 kW-class reactor and thruster system — data on integrated operation, heat rejection and shielding.
  3. Next: an operational nuclear-electric cargo tug in Earth orbit — moving heavy payloads to the Moon and Mars without giant chemical rockets.
  4. Longer term: megawatt-class ships for crewed Mars and outer-planet missions — fast, reusable deep-space transport.
