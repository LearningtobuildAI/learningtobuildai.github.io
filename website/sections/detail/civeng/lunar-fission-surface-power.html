PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Reactor core and fuel — holds a self-sustaining fission chain reaction in a small, very light package. Key parts: highly enriched uranium metal alloy fuel (the Kilopower approach), refractory metal cladding, boron carbide control and shutdown rods, beryllium reflector.
  • Heat transport — moves heat from the core to the converters without pumps or moving coolant. Key parts: sodium heat pipes bonded into the core, cold-end heat pipes.
  • Power conversion — turns heat into electricity. Key parts: free-piston Stirling convertors, linear alternators, power conditioning and controller.
  • Heat rejection — dumps waste heat to space — in vacuum, radiators must be big, and there is two to three times more waste heat than electricity. Key parts: radiator panels, embedded heat pipes, deployment hinges and drives.
  • Shielding and siting — protects crew, equipment and the lander from neutrons and gamma rays. Key parts: regolith burial or berm, neutron and gamma shield mass, siting distance and shadowing.
  • Control and autonomy — runs unattended for years, including through the lunar night. Key parts: reactor instrumentation, autonomous startup and shutdown logic, fault protection.
  • Lander interface — carries a fuelled, subcritical reactor to the surface and sets it down. Key parts: structural mount, separation and lowering mechanism.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Make the fuel. Uranium is enriched, alloyed and cast into a monolithic core rather than the fuel rods of a terrestrial reactor.
  2. Build in the heat pipes. Sodium-filled heat pipes are bonded into the core so heat leaves by evaporation and condensation, with no pumps.
  3. Assemble core and reflector. The core is surrounded by a beryllium reflector, with boron carbide rods that keep it shut down before launch and can stop it afterwards.
  4. Build the convertors. Free-piston Stirling engines with linear alternators are assembled and run for thousands of hours to prove lifetime.
  5. Fabricate the radiators. Lightweight panels with embedded heat pipes are made to fold for launch and unfold on the surface.
  6. Test the whole system on the ground. Electric heaters first, then a fuelled critical test in a shielded facility — the approach taken by the 2018 KRUSTY experiment in Nevada, which ran a 1 kW-class reactor.
  7. Qualify for launch. Launch safety analysis and containment are prepared; the reactor stays subcritical until it is safely on the surface.
  8. Land, deploy and start up. A lander sets the unit down, radiators unfold, it is buried or bermed with regolith for shielding, and the reactor is brought critical autonomously and run through a 14-day lunar night.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Space fission reactors — a handful have flown — SNAP-10A in 1965 and Soviet radar-satellite reactors — but none has operated on a planetary surface, and ground tests such as KRUSTY are the current state of the art.
  • PARTIAL Long-life free-piston Stirling convertors — they must run for a decade without maintenance; terrestrial Stirling machines exist but not at this lifetime and reliability.
  • PARTIAL Lightweight high-temperature radiators — rejecting tens of kilowatts in vacuum needs large area that must still fit on a lander.
  • PARTIAL Nuclear launch approval and fuelled-payload handling — the pacing item is qualifying fuelled hardware for launch and landing, not the reactor physics.
  • PARTIAL Lunar landing of multi-tonne payloads — commercial lunar landers are flying, but delivering and placing a shielded reactor is a step beyond current demonstrated payloads.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: a 1 kW-class ground demonstration completed (KRUSTY, 2018); 40 kW-class conceptual designs funded by NASA and the US Department of Energy — confidence that a small fast reactor with heat pipes and Stirling convertors can work.
  2. Next: a complete flight-configured unit tested on the ground, including launch qualification — a design that can actually be put on a lander.
  3. Next: a reactor demonstrated on the lunar surface and run through the night — proven surface power for habitats and instruments independent of sunlight.
  4. Longer term: multiple units scaled to hundreds of kilowatts or megawatts — outposts, in-situ propellant production and, eventually, power for industry on the Moon and Mars.
