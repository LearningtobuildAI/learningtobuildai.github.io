PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

An electromagnetic accelerator on the lunar surface could launch regolith or refined material into orbit without propellant, because the Moon's low gravity and absent atmosphere remove the two dominant costs of Earth launch. Studies treat it as a prerequisite for space-based construction at scale.

4How It Works

A linear synchronous motor along a track accelerates a carrier to lunar escape or transfer velocity; the payload is released at the track end and captured in orbit by a receiving tug.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Power system — supplies the large electrical pulse needed to accelerate the carrier. Key parts: fission reactor or solar array, energy storage (flywheels or capacitors), power conditioning and switching.
  • Launch track — supports and guides the carrier along a straight path. Key parts: segmented steel or composite rails, precision alignment system, thermal control to handle lunar day–night swings.
  • Accelerator — drives the carrier up to launch speed. Key parts: linear synchronous motor coils, armature on the carrier, control electronics for timing and current.
  • Carrier and payload — holds the material during acceleration and releases it in orbit. Key parts: lightweight structural frame, regolith container, release mechanism.
  • Orbital receiving system — catches the launched payloads and moves them to where they are needed. Key parts: tug spacecraft, capture net or docking fixture, propulsion for orbit transfer.
  • Surface mining and loading — digs, processes and feeds regolith or refined material to the launcher. Key parts: excavators, beneficiation plant, conveyors and loaders.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a site. Pick a flat, stable area near the equator with a clear launch corridor and accessible regolith, away from heritage sites.
  2. Land power and base equipment. Deliver a fission reactor or large solar farm with energy storage, plus habitats and construction machinery.
  3. Prepare the ground. Level and compact the regolith, then build foundations that will not shift under repeated launches.
  4. Assemble the track. Lay and align segmented rails over several kilometres, using the Moon’s low gravity to allow a lighter structure than on Earth.
  5. Install accelerator coils. Place linear motor coils along the track, wire them to the power system and connect the control network.
  6. Fabricate carriers. Build lightweight carriers that can survive high acceleration and hold containers of regolith.
  7. Test and calibrate. Launch increasing masses at progressively higher speeds, tuning the acceleration profile and release timing.
  8. Integrate mining and loading. Automate digging, crushing and loading so the launcher can fire repeatedly without human intervention.
  9. Deploy the orbital tug. Position a spacecraft in lunar orbit to rendezvous with each payload, capture it and carry it to a construction site.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Heavy lunar landing and surface operations — robotic landers have reached the Moon, but landing hundreds of tonnes of equipment and building a base is not yet routine.
  • PARTIAL In-situ resource utilisation (mining and processing regolith) — mining and processing have been demonstrated in labs and small field tests, but not at production scale on the Moon.
  • PARTIAL Space fission reactors or very large solar arrays — compact fission reactors are being developed for space, and solar farms would need to be assembled over a large area.
  • EXISTS High-power linear motors and pulsed power — the basic technology is used in trains and launchers on Earth, but must be adapted for vacuum, dust and temperature extremes.
  • NOT YET Orbital rendezvous and capture of high-speed payloads — no spacecraft has yet caught a payload moving at kilometres per second in lunar orbit.
  • NOT YET Precision construction in lunar conditions — building a kilometres-long, millimetre-aligned track in vacuum and wild temperature swings has never been attempted.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: robotic prospecting and small mining demonstrations on the lunar surface — knowledge of regolith properties and the basics of digging and processing.
  2. Next: a small demonstrator launcher that throws a few kilograms into lunar orbit — proof that electromagnetic launch works in the lunar environment.
  3. Next: a full-scale mass driver powered by a surface reactor, launching hundreds of tonnes per year — a steady supply of bulk material in lunar orbit at far lower cost than Earth launch.
  4. Longer term: orbital construction of large structures such as solar power satellites or habitats using lunar material — space infrastructure at a scale that Earth-launched rockets cannot economically support.
