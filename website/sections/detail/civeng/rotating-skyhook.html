PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

A skyhook is a long rotating tether whose lower tip moves slowly enough at perigee to be caught by a suborbital vehicle, then lifts the payload toward orbital velocity as it swings upward. It multiplies existing launch capability rather than replacing it.

4How It Works

The tether rotates end over end while its centre of mass follows an orbit, and tip velocity is matched to the capture vehicle by phasing. Momentum is transferred to the payload on every catch and must be restored by solar-electric propulsion.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Tether — the long, tapered load-bearing element that carries the payload and provides the reach. Key parts: high-strength fibre strands, braided or tape construction, deployment spool, redundant load paths.
  • Hub and counterweight — the tether's centre of mass, which orbits like a satellite, and the ballast that balances the two arms. Key parts: structural hub, ballast mass, avionics bay.
  • Capture interface — the lower tip hardware that meets the incoming suborbital vehicle. Key parts: grapple or net, damping and reel mechanism, approach beacons.
  • Momentum management — restores the energy and angular momentum that every payload catch removes. Key parts: solar arrays, electric thrusters, conductive tether path (electrodynamic option).
  • Control and dynamics — keeps the tether spinning, stable and phased for the next catch. Key parts: attitude sensors, libration damping control, orbital tracking and trajectory software.
  • Launch-vehicle interface — the suborbital vehicle that climbs to the tip, releases the payload and turns back. Key parts: suborbital booster, payload cradle, guidance and timing system.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the design. The radius, taper and spin rate are chosen so the lower tip moves slowly enough at its lowest point for a suborbital vehicle to catch it, and fast enough at the top for the payload to be released near orbital velocity.
  2. Make the tether material. High-strength fibres are spun into yarn and braided into a tether with redundant strands and a tapered cross-section — thick at the hub where tension is highest, thin at the tips.
  3. Build the hub and deployer. The hub carries the spool, thrusters, solar arrays and avionics, and must deploy the tether without tangling or snagging.
  4. Launch and assemble in orbit. The tether is far longer than any rocket fairing, so it is launched as a coiled package or in segments and deployed under controlled tension, or joined piece by piece.
  5. Spin up. Electric thrusters and electrodynamic drag work against gravity-gradient forces to spin the tether up to its operating rate and place the orbit.
  6. Install the capture hardware. A grapple or catching net is fitted at the lower tip, with beacon, radar and optical aids for the incoming vehicle.
  7. Test with inert payloads. Small, expendable masses are caught and released first, to check timing, damping and tether loads before anything valuable is carried.
  8. Operate and reboost. Each catch drains momentum from the rotating system, so between catches the hub fires its solar-electric thrusters to restore spin and orbital altitude.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Very high strength-to-mass tether fibres — the tether holds itself and the payload under tension, so specific strength sets the maximum practical length.
  • PARTIAL Long, tangle-free orbital deployment — small tethers have been deployed in orbit, but a kilometre-scale spinning tether has never been built.
  • PARTIAL A reusable suborbital vehicle with precise timing — the catch demands a few kilometres per second and arrival at a moving tip within a narrow window.
  • PARTIAL High-power solar-electric propulsion — momentum and altitude must be restored between catches without consuming chemical propellant.
  • PARTIAL Autonomous tip rendezvous and capture — orbital docking autonomy exists, but catching a fast-moving, rotating tip is a new guidance problem.
  • NOT YET Micrometeoroid and debris survivability — a long tether presents a large target area and must survive years of impacts and thermal cycling.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: ground rigs and short orbital tether experiments that deploy, stabilise and cut a tether — confidence in deployment dynamics, materials and electrodynamic reboost.
  2. Next: a sub-scale rotating tether in low Earth orbit that catches and releases an inert test mass — proof that tip rendezvous and momentum transfer work in practice.
  3. Next: a full-scale skyhook paired with a reusable suborbital vehicle — payloads reaching orbit for a fraction of the rocket velocity otherwise needed.
  4. Longer term: a network of tethers at different orbits, plus high-energy versions for escape trajectories — routine orbital transfer and cheaper departure toward the Moon and beyond.
