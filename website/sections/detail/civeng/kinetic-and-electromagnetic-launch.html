4How It Works

A centrifuge or a linear electromagnetic track accelerates the payload before release. From Earth the payload must survive thousands of g and atmospheric heating, so a small rocket stage still finishes the climb to orbit.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Accelerator — gives the payload its speed before release — either a spinning arm or a linear electromagnetic track. Key parts: rotating arm and counterweight (centrifugal type), linear motor coils and stator track (electromagnetic type), bearings or magnetic levitation.
  • Energy supply and pulsed drive — delivers a large burst of energy in seconds or a steady spin-up over minutes. Key parts: flywheel or capacitor bank, motor-generator or power electronics, grid connection or dedicated generation.
  • Vacuum and release system — removes air drag, then lets the payload leave at the right instant. Key parts: evacuated chamber or enclosure, seal or membrane at the exit, release and timing mechanism.
  • Payload carrier — carries the payload through huge acceleration and, from Earth, through the lower atmosphere. Key parts: structural shell, thermal protection, small rocket kick stage, avionics and telemetry.
  • Aiming and control — points the release vector so the payload meets its target trajectory. Key parts: attitude and azimuth control, radar and optical tracking, flight termination logic.
  • Launch site — the ground installation, sized around the machine and its safety exclusion zone. Key parts: concrete and steel foundations, power and cooling plant, range safety and recovery areas.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the site. The machine needs cheap, abundant electricity and a wide safety zone. A lunar track would sit in vacuum and use solar arrays or a small reactor.
  2. Build the accelerator. A centrifuge is a large arm inside a circular vacuum chamber; an electromagnetic version is a long track of powered coils.
  3. Install the power system. Capacitors or flywheels store the energy for a launch and deliver it in a fraction of a second.
  4. Integrate the payload. The payload is hardened against thousands of g and bolted into an aerodynamic carrier with its own small motor.
  5. Pump down and spin up. Air is removed from the chamber and the arm is brought up to speed — or the coils are pulsed in sequence.
  6. Release. The carrier exits through a sealed port at the intended angle and speed; from Earth this is several times the speed of sound at low altitude.
  7. Kick stage. The small onboard rocket fires to raise the low release point into a stable orbit, since the machine alone cannot reach orbital velocity.
  8. Recover or discard. On Earth the carrier may parachute back for reuse; a lunar launcher would fire payload canisters that are caught or consumed in orbit.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Payloads that survive extreme acceleration — some components can be ruggedised, but delicate satellites and humans cannot.
  • EXISTS High-power pulsed energy storage — capacitor banks and flywheels exist, but not yet packaged for routine launch duty.
  • EXISTS Small restartable kick stages — a ground launcher releases below orbital velocity, so a rocket must finish the job.
  • PARTIAL Thermal protection for low-altitude hypersonic flight — re-entry shields work at high altitude; dense air at launch speed is a harsher case.
  • PARTIAL Lunar power and surface construction — a lunar mass driver would need a large power source and automated building at a base.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: suborbital test shots from a centrifugal accelerator, such as those flown by SpinLaunch — data on release, aerodynamics and payload survival.
  2. Next: an orbital demonstration using a machine plus a kick stage — proof that the concept can reach orbit, even with small payloads.
  3. Next: regular launch of rugged small payloads or bulk materials — a niche alternative to rockets for fuel, water or raw materials.
  4. Longer term: a lunar electromagnetic launcher powered by surface solar or nuclear power — exporting lunar material to orbit without burning propellant.
