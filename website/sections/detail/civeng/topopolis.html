PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

A topopolis is a tube of habitat rotating about its own long axis and wound around a star or star system, producing interior area far exceeding any single ring. It scales by extension rather than by building at a larger radius.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Each segment rotates to supply gravity while the tube as a whole orbits, and segments are joined by bearings that permit relative rotation. The concept trades structural tensile limits against the sheer quantity of material required.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Habitat tube — the pressurised shell whose inner surface is the living area; spinning it about its long axis supplies artificial gravity. Key parts: reinforced pressure shell, soil and water layer for shielding and growing, sunlight ducts or mirrors, end-cap airlocks.
  • Bearings and joints — let neighbouring lengths of tube spin at different rates while still passing people and cargo between them. Key parts: large-diameter magnetic or fluid bearings, rotary air seals, transfer tunnels and docking ports.
  • Orbit track — holds the coiled tube in a stable path around the star so it neither falls in nor drifts away. Key parts: station-keeping thrusters or mass drivers, orbit and attitude sensors, debris shielding.
  • Spin and momentum management — spins segments up to operating speed and keeps the whole system balanced. Key parts: electromagnetic mass drivers, flywheels and counterweights, angular-momentum exchange between segments.
  • Power and thermal control — collects starlight and dumps waste heat, which is hard in vacuum. Key parts: large collector mirrors, photovoltaic or thermal converters, radiator panels.
  • Material supply and life support — builds and sustains the habitat from non-terrestrial material. Key parts: asteroid and moonlet mining, refining and fabrication plants, closed-loop air, water and food recycling.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the orbit and the radius. A small tube radius keeps structural stress low. At 1 km radius, 1 g needs a rim speed near 99 m/s — about one rotation per minute — which ordinary steel could tolerate in hoop tension; the same habitat at 100 km radius would need far stronger material.
  2. Mine material where it already is. Bulk mass comes from asteroids or small moons rather than being lifted from a planet, because the required tonnage is astronomical.
  3. Refine and form it in space. Ore is processed with solar heat and power into metal sheet, structural members and shielding slag at a construction station.
  4. Fabricate tube segments. Short pressurised cylinders are welded or wound up individually, with windows, bearing races and attachment points built in.
  5. Spin and balance each segment. Segments are spun up separately and their internal mass arranged so each is dynamically balanced before anything is joined.
  6. Couple segments with bearings. Neighbouring segments meet at high-capacity bearings that allow relative rotation while carrying the loads between them.
  7. Extend the tube into a coil. The chain of segments is placed in orbit and lengthened turn by turn around the star; each turn is an independent orbit, so the structure is never a single rigid ring and the joins are not carrying the whole system's orbital loads.
  8. Commission and maintain. Atmosphere, ecosystems and shielding are brought online, and worn segments are swapped out individually — a closed loop where old material is recycled into new tube.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Large closed-loop life support — partial recycling has been demonstrated in orbit, but a habitat this size must approach full closure of air, water and food.
  • NOT YET In-space mining and manufacturing at scale — building from launched material alone is unaffordable; the structure must be made from asteroid or lunar mass.
  • NOT YET Very large low-friction bearings — kilometre-scale joints that spin continuously for decades under load do not exist.
  • PARTIAL Radiation shielding and structural materials — cosmic rays and micrometeoroids require metres of mass shielding, which the tube itself can supply if designed for it.
  • PARTIAL Cheap access to orbit and beyond — reusable launch has cut costs, but nothing yet approaches the cost per kilogram such a project needs.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: orbiting stations and microgravity research; small rotating-habitat experiments — experience of living and working in space for long periods.
  2. Next: free-flying rotating stations at a few metres to a few hundred metres radius — verified artificial gravity, bearings and large pressure structures.
  3. Next: cylindrical habitats built mainly from off-Earth material, of the kind proposed in 1970s studies — industrial-scale space manufacturing and closed ecosystems.
  4. Then: a single long tube in orbit — one link of the future topopolis — proof that spinning segments can be coupled and extended.
  5. Longer term: extension into a coil of many turns around the star — habitable area to rival or exceed a planet's surface without any single enormous structure — for example, a tube 1 km in radius and one astronomical unit long would enclose roughly 900 million square kilometres, about twice Earth's surface area.
