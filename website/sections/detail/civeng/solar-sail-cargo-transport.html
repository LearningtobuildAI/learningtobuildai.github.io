1Definition

Large reflective sails use continuous photon pressure for propellant-free cargo delivery, well suited to slow, high-delta-v resupply of cislunar or interplanetary depots where launch mass is the binding constraint.

4How It Works

A deployed thin-film sail gains thrust from sunlight; attitude control by tilting the sail changes the thrust vector, letting the craft spiral outward over months without consuming any reaction mass.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sail membrane — the reflective surface that intercepts sunlight and turns it into thrust — kilometres of area for useful cargo masses, but only grams per square metre. Key parts: polyimide film a few micrometres thick, aluminium reflective coating tens of nanometres thick, ripstops, seams and reinforced corner attach points.
  • Deployment structure — holds the membrane flat and taut without a rigid frame heavier than the sail itself. Key parts: thin-walled composite booms, flattened and rolled on spools, boom tips and tensioning lines, spin-stabilisation or hub-and-spoke rigging.
  • Spacecraft bus — carries the cargo and keeps the vehicle alive. Key parts: lightweight structural frame, solar cells and batteries, avionics, radio and star trackers.
  • Thrust-vector control — tilts the sail so the thrust points where the trajectory needs it. Key parts: electrochromic or liquid-crystal reflectivity-control panels on the membrane, moving trim masses or gimballed booms, small chemical or electric thrusters for the final approach.
  • Cargo and docking interface — the payload canister and the means to hand it over. Key parts: pressurised or unpressurised cargo module, berthing mechanism and alignment sensors, depot-side grapple or tug.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Fix the trajectory first. Because thrust depends on sail area and orientation, the spiral or transfer is designed before any hardware: how big the sail must be, how many months the trip takes, and how much the last few hundred metres per second cost in propellant.
  2. Cast and coat the film. Polyimide is cast to a few micrometres thick, then aluminised on one face to make it reflective and to control how it sheds charge.
  3. Cut and seam the gores. The film is cut into panels, joined with ripstops so that a micrometeorite hole stops at one seam, and reinforced wherever a boom or line attaches.
  4. Make the booms. Thin-walled composite tubes are cured in a flattened profile so they can be rolled onto a spool, then spring back to a stiff straight section on release.
  5. Pack the sail. The membrane is Z-folded around the stowed booms into a volume small enough for a launch fairing, with release ties that fire in sequence.
  6. Assemble the bus. Structure, solar cells, avionics, reflectivity-control panels and the cargo canister are integrated and shaken and thermally tested.
  7. Deploy on orbit. Release ties fire, booms unroll over minutes to hours, and the team checks that the membrane is flat and free of snags before trusting it with thrust.
  8. Calibrate the thrust. Orbit tracking measures the tiny acceleration and how it changes with sail tilt — the data that turns the membrane into a navigable engine.
  9. Fly the transfer. Guidance trims the sail continuously for months, harvesting a fraction of a millimetre per second squared and summing it into kilometres per second — with no propellant consumed.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Ultrathin deployable sail films and booms — sails of tens to a few hundred square metres have flown, including interplanetary demonstration missions; cargo-class sails are orders of magnitude larger.
  • EXISTS Autonomous low-thrust trajectory optimisation — the thrust is weaker than any perturbation, so the vehicle must compute and retrim its own path over months.
  • PARTIAL Attitude control that steers thrust without using propellant — reflectivity-control panels and trim masses have been demonstrated, but only on small sails.
  • PARTIAL Long-duration survivability in space — radiation darkens the film, micrometeorites perforate it and plasma charges it, slowly eroding thrust over a multi-year cargo run.
  • NOT YET Propellant-free rendezvous and berthing — a sail cannot stop at a depot; something must close the final metres and latch the cargo.
  • NOT YET Beamed-energy or laser assist — a ground- or space-based laser could raise thrust by orders of magnitude and shrink the sail, but only concepts and small-scale experiments exist.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: small sails (tens to about a hundred square metres) flying and trimming their orbits by photon pressure alone — proof that the force is measurable, steerable and repeatable.
  2. Next: large sails of hundreds to a few thousand square metres on dedicated interplanetary small-satellite missions — long-duration thrust data and realistic degradation rates.
  3. Next: a cargo demonstrator delivering a small payload to a cislunar or near-Earth-object depot — the rendezvous, berthing and logistics chain around the sail itself.
  4. Longer term: routine propellant-free resupply of depots, with sails as the slow, cheap lane and chemical or electric tugs as the fast one — a logistics architecture where launch mass goes to payload rather than to propellant.
