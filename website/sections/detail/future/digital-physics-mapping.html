PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Discrete substrate models — propose a finite, rule-based structure — a cellular automaton, causal set or spin network — in place of smooth spacetime. Key parts: update rule, lattice or graph of cells/nodes, discretisation scale.
  • Information formalism — rewrites physical states as information and physical evolution as computation. Key parts: qubits and quantum circuits, entanglement entropy, holographic error-correcting codes.
  • Simulation stack — runs candidate rules at sizes large enough to be compared with real physics. Key parts: classical high-performance clusters, tensor-network algorithms, quantum computers and quantum simulators.
  • Correspondence tools — checks whether the discrete model reproduces relativity, the Standard Model and conservation laws at everyday scales. Key parts: continuum limit derivations, symmetry analysis, effective field theory matching.
  • Observational probes — looks for a faint signature of discreteness in nature. Key parts: gamma-ray and neutrino astronomy, atomic clocks and interferometry, gravitational-wave detectors.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a substrate and rule. Pick a finite structure — a grid of cells, a network of qubits — and a local, reversible update rule that evolves it step by step.
  2. Derive the continuum limit. Show mathematically that smooth spacetime, Lorentz symmetry and known particles emerge as large-scale approximations of the rule, or identify exactly where they fail.
  3. Encode it on hardware. Map the rule onto tensor-network algorithms or quantum processors, because the state space grows far beyond what ordinary computers can hold.
  4. Validate against exact results. Reproduce cases where both the discrete model and standard physics give the same answer, to confirm the encoding is correct.
  5. Extract a testable signature. Find where the discrete model predicts something different — an energy-dependent speed of light, a minimum length, a bound on information density.
  6. Search the sky and the lab. Compare those predictions with high-energy astrophysics, clock and interferometer data; a null result tightens or kills the model.
  7. Iterate or abandon. If no deviation appears, revise the substrate and rule, or conclude that information is not the fundamental level.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Quantum information theory — it supplies the language — qubits, entanglement, entropy — in which an information-based physics is written.
  • PARTIAL Holographic duality (AdS/CFT) — a concrete, well-studied case where a theory of gravity equals a quantum information description, but only for special space-times.
  • PARTIAL Quantum computers and simulators — they are the only plausible way to simulate candidate substrates at useful size; error correction is still immature.
  • NOT YET A theory of quantum gravity — digital physics is a candidate for one, not a solution it can borrow from.
  • EXISTS Precision tests of fundamental symmetries — discreteness must show up as a tiny deviation from standard physics, and only precision measurement can settle the question.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: discrete models reproduce fragments of physics — holographic codes, tensor networks, cellular-automaton interpretations of quantum mechanics — a shared language between gravity, thermodynamics and information.
  2. Next: quantum simulators run small discrete space-times and lattice gauge theories — bench-top experiments on whether geometry can emerge from a rule.
  3. Next: sharp, quantitative predictions for observable discreteness — falsifiable targets for gamma-ray, neutrino and gravitational-wave observatories.
  4. Longer term: either a full correspondence to known physics or a clear refutation — if it succeeds, a single computational description underlying all of physics; if not, the idea is retired.
