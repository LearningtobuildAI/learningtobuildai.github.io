PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Brain mapping — records the wiring diagram — which neuron connects to which, and how strongly. Key parts: electron and expansion microscopy, tissue slicing and staining, automated image stitching, synapse detection software.
  • Activity recording — captures what the brain is doing, not just how it is wired. Key parts: electrode arrays, voltage and calcium imaging, functional MRI, optogenetic stimulation.
  • Cell and synapse models — mathematical rules for how each neuron and connection behaves. Key parts: membrane and ion-channel equations, plasticity and learning rules, neurotransmitter dynamics.
  • Emulation compute — the hardware that would run a brain-sized simulation. Key parts: GPU and accelerator clusters, neuromorphic chips, high-bandwidth interconnect (optical links proposed), exabyte-scale memory.
  • Simulation software — turns the model into a running, behaving system. Key parts: neuron simulation engine, connectome database, validation and comparison tools.
  • Embodiment and interface — gives the emulated mind senses and the ability to act. Key parts: cameras and microphones, robotic or virtual body, bidirectional neural interface.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Map a complete small brain. Serial electron microscopy and machine-learning image analysis have produced the full connectome of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (302 neurons, first published in 1986) and, in 2024, the adult fruit fly's roughly 140,000 neurons.
  2. Map a mammalian cortex. Denser tissue needs better staining, faster imaging and vastly more automated reconstruction; only millimetre-scale volumes have been done so far.
  3. Record the living state. A connectome alone is not a mind. You must record the ongoing activity and the strength of every synapse in a living, intact brain — currently impossible at that resolution without damaging the tissue.
  4. Write down neuron and synapse rules. Convert anatomy and recordings into equations for each cell type: how it sums inputs, fires, and changes its connections with use.
  5. Build the emulation engine. Distribute millions to billions of model neurons across many processors, with communication between them fine-grained enough to stay in biological time.
  6. Validate against behaviour. Run the model and compare its outputs with the animal it came from — the only practical test that the emulation is faithful rather than merely plausible.
  7. Scale to a human brain. A human cortex holds roughly 86 billion neurons and hundreds of trillions of synapses, requiring computing and memory far beyond today's machines.
  8. Capture and transfer a living mind. Ideally without destroying the original: high-resolution, non-destructive scanning plus a method to move the state onto the synthetic substrate. No route to this is known.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Whole-brain connectomics at mammalian scale — insect and small-animal circuits are mapped; a whole human brain has not been imaged at synapse resolution.
  • NOT YET Non-destructive in-vivo recording of all synapses — current methods either sample a tiny fraction of cells or require fixing and slicing the tissue.
  • PARTIAL Exascale computing with biological-time simulation — large-scale brain simulations already run, but far slower than real time and at reduced detail.
  • PARTIAL Validated models of learning and memory — plasticity rules are understood in fragments; no complete, tested account exists.
  • NOT YET A test for whether an emulation is the same person — there is no accepted scientific method to verify continuity of experience after a transfer, and the question may not be empirically decidable.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: complete connectomes of insects and partial maps of mouse cortex (e.g. FlyWire 2024, MICrONS) — testable models of real, if small, nervous systems.
  2. Next: behaviourally validated emulation of a small circuit, such as a fly's motion-detection pathway — confidence that mapping plus simulation can reproduce function at all.
  3. Next: hybrid systems: brain tissue coupled to silicon, and neural prosthetics that replace damaged regions — medical uses, and evidence that biological and synthetic processing can interoperate.
  4. Longer term: whole-brain emulation of a mammal at real time — the first concrete test case for the idea.
  5. Speculative: gradual or scanning-based transfer of a human mind onto a non-biological substrate, with photonic or neuromorphic computing as one proposed host — would raise questions of identity, consent and rights that science alone cannot answer.
