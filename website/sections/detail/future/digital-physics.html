PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Digital physics is a theoretical framework that models the physical universe as a computational process or a vast network of discrete elements. It explores whether the fundamental laws of nature can be described by digital information and computation.

4How It Works

By representing physical phenomena using algorithms, digital physics seeks to understand how classical and quantum mechanics might emerge from underlying computational processes. This involves creating simulations that mimic real-world systems at various scales, from subatomic particles to complex macroscopic structures.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Discrete substrate — replaces smooth space and time with a finite set of states and an update rule. Key parts: lattice, causal set or cellular-automaton rule, state space, Planck-scale spacing (~1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m).
  • Quantum information layer — treats qubits and their entanglement — not fields — as the fundamental description. Key parts: Hilbert space, entanglement structure, quantum error-correcting codes.
  • Continuum limit — shows how known physics emerges when the discrete model is viewed at large scales. Key parts: renormalisation, symmetry recovery, correspondence to quantum field theory.
  • Holographic description — writes the interior of space as encoded on a lower-dimensional boundary. Key parts: boundary theory, bulk reconstruction, black-hole entropy accounting.
  • Simulation stack — runs the model, either approximately on classical machines or natively on quantum hardware. Key parts: tensor-network algorithms on supercomputers, quantum simulators (superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms), error-corrected logical qubits.
  • Falsification apparatus — looks for the tiny signatures a discrete or simulated universe would leave. Key parts: gamma-ray burst photon-timing telescopes, precision atomic clocks and atom interferometers, low-noise interferometers such as the Fermilab Holometer.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick a discrete substrate. Choose a finite rule set — a lattice, a causal set of discrete events, or a cellular automaton — rather than continuous spacetime.
  2. Write an update rule with the right symmetries. The rule must generate three-dimensional space, locality and the symmetries of the Standard Model, or it is dead on arrival.
  3. Recover known physics in the continuum limit. Show that at scales far above the Planck length the model reproduces quantum field theory, general relativity and the measured particle masses.
  4. Account for gravity and black-hole entropy. A working model must produce the observed entropy of black holes and something like the holographic bound on information in a region.
  5. Simulate it classically. Run small versions on supercomputers with tensor-network methods to check predictions against experiments.
  6. Map it onto quantum hardware. Encode the rule on a quantum simulator so systems too entangled for classical machines can be run.
  7. Hunt for deviations. Derive observable consequences — a minimum length, slightly different photon speeds at extreme energies, or holographic noise — and set limits with existing instruments.
  8. Compare and discard. Models that predict effects already ruled out are dropped; survivors are pushed to larger simulated systems and finer experiments.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Fault-tolerant quantum computers — simulating fundamental discrete models natively needs far more logical qubits than today's machines.
  • EXISTS Tensor-network and lattice simulation methods — these let classical supercomputers approximate strongly entangled quantum systems today.
  • NOT YET A testable theory of quantum gravity — without one there is no agreed calculation that connects a discrete rule to observed physics.
  • PARTIAL Precision tests of Lorentz invariance — observations of distant gamma-ray bursts already constrain some forms of spacetime discreteness.
  • PARTIAL Quantum information theory of holography — it supplies the main worked examples of how geometry could emerge from entanglement.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: quantum information treated as fundamental; classical simulation of toy discrete models — concrete, checkable statements about spacetime discreteness.
  2. Next: larger quantum simulations of lattice gauge theories and holographic toy models — evidence for or against specific discrete rules.
  3. Next: a quantum-gravity framework that predicts a measurable deviation — a real experiment rather than a thought experiment.
  4. Longer term: dedicated precision experiments reaching Planck-scale sensitivity — either a detection of discreteness or durable limits that rule whole families of models out.
