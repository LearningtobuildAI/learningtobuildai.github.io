PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Stellar engineering, also known as starlifting or stellar fusion, refers to theoretical methods aimed at harnessing the immense energy resources available within stars. These techniques involve manipulating celestial bodies on a large scale and potentially extracting their energy content.

2Problem It Solves

Theoretical approaches to stellar engineering aim to address the global energy crisis by providing an almost limitless source of clean, sustainable energy. It also offers potential solutions for space exploration and colonization by enabling long-term habitation and resource extraction on other planets or moons.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

In theory, stellar engineering could involve two primary methods: 'star lifting,' which involves physically moving parts of a star or its surrounding material to extract energy; and 'stellar fusion,' where the conditions within a star are altered to initiate controlled nuclear fusion for energy production. However, these processes are currently beyond our technological capabilities.

5Materials Used
  • stellar matter
  • fusion catalysts
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Currently in a theoretical phase, no manufacturing processes exist. The materials required would be derived from stellar matter, which is not currently accessible with our technology.

7Build Process

The build process for such technologies is purely speculative at this stage. Any potential future process would involve advanced space travel and manipulation techniques that are far beyond current engineering capabilities.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Star-side matter handling — extracts or redirects plasma from a star's surface or wind, the only known way to change a star's output. Key parts: construction-scale magnetic nozzle or scoop, plasma conduit, magnetic confinement coils.
  • Collector swarm — a very large number of independent orbiting panels that intercept starlight and convert it. Key parts: thin-film photovoltaic or thermal panels, lightweight support trusses, attitude control.
  • Orbital manufacturing base — makes the swarm from material already in space rather than launching it from Earth. Key parts: asteroid or moonlet mining robots, refining and smelting units, additive manufacturing lines, mass drivers or launchers.
  • Power transmission — gets energy from the swarm to wherever it is used. Key parts: microwave or laser emitters, relay stations, rectenna receivers.
  • Waste-heat radiators — thermodynamics forces any collector to radiate most absorbed energy as heat, so radiators set the real limit. Key parts: large-area radiator panels, coolant loops.
  • Navigation and control network — keeps millions of bodies in non-intersecting orbits around a moving star. Key parts: star-relative sensors, inter-satellite links, autonomous collision avoidance.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Survey a candidate star and its small bodies. Map stellar type, activity and the asteroid or comet population that would supply material. There is no point starting on a star without a supply of mass.
  2. Build the first self-replicating factory. Land robotic miners and refiners on one small body and have them produce the first collector and more mining machines from local material.
  3. Grow the population exponentially. Each generation of machines builds the next, so collector numbers rise geometrically. Space-based mining removes the launch cost that would otherwise dominate.
  4. Place collectors on many separate orbits. Spreading collectors across a wide range of orbits avoids the mechanical and collision problems of one rigid shell.
  5. Assemble star-lifting hardware. If the goal is to change the star itself, construct magnetic scoops at the poles to draw plasma off the surface into space, where it is either collected or expelled as exhaust.
  6. Transport and convert the extracted plasma. Route captured material to collectors or use its momentum to push the star, as in a Shkadov-style thruster that reflects the stellar wind asymmetrically.
  7. Match waste heat to the new output. Radiator area must grow with the energy absorbed, so every increase in collecting power demands a larger infrared-emitting surface.
  8. Scale in steps, verifying stability. Grow the swarm while monitoring that stellar activity, orbits and radiators remain within limits before committing to the next order of magnitude.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Fully automated off-world manufacturing — the mass needed is astronomically larger than anything rockets can lift from Earth, so machines must build themselves from asteroid material.
  • PARTIAL Low-cost access to space material — asteroid sample return and small-scale in-space manufacturing have flown, but nothing has mined or refined material in bulk.
  • PARTIAL Large-scale space power conversion and beaming — spacecraft solar arrays work and beamed power has been demonstrated at kilowatt scale, far short of the needed megawatt-to-terawatt range.
  • NOT YET Megastructure orbital dynamics and control — keeping millions of independent collectors in stable, non-colliding orbits around an evolving star has never been attempted.
  • PARTIAL Materials stable over geological timescales in space — radiation, thermal cycling and micrometeoroids degrade current materials far faster than a swarm would need.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: robotic asteroid sample return and small in-space manufacturing demonstrations — the first tested steps toward making hardware from space material.
  2. Next: autonomous mining and refining of an asteroid or lunar body into propellant and structures — construction mass that does not have to be launched from Earth.
  3. Next: in-space assembly of kilometre-scale solar arrays with power beamed to a receiver — the building block of a collector swarm at useful scale.
  4. Longer term: a swarm of thousands of independent collectors returning a measurable fraction of one star's light — a Kardashev-scale energy supply, and a real infrared signature to search for.
  5. Speculative: star lifting, or a Shkadov-style thruster that moves a star — deliberate control of a star's luminosity and position — the engineering study of this remains entirely theoretical.
