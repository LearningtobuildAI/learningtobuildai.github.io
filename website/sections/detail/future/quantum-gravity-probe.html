A Quantum Gravity Probe is a hypothetical device designed to measure the minute effects of gravity on subatomic particles, aiming to explore the intersection between quantum mechanics and general relativity.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Quantum sensor — the mass or atom wave whose phase shifts in a gravitational field. Key parts: cold-atom source in vacuum, laser pulse optics, or a levitated microsphere in an optical trap.
  • Interferometer and readout — turns tiny phase shifts into a measurable signal. Key parts: laser system, squeezed-light source, photodetectors, atomic clock or frequency reference.
  • Isolation and shielding — keeps seismic, magnetic, thermal and acoustic noise from swamping the signal. Key parts: seismic isolation stages, magnetic shields, cryogenics, high-vacuum envelope.
  • Baseline structure — separates the sensor elements by a known distance. Key parts: long vacuum tube or free-fall drift, precisely surveyed reference points.
  • Calibration and analysis — distinguishes new physics from conventional disturbances. Key parts: known movable test masses, gravity models, parameter-estimation software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick the observable. Choose a signature that quantum-gravity theories actually predict at reachable energies — a deviation from Newtonian gravity at short range, a loss of quantum coherence, or entanglement between two masses caused only by their gravity.
  2. Design the sensor. Decide between atom interferometry (two atom clouds in free fall), levitated optomechanics (a nanoparticle held in a laser trap), or suspended masses. Each sets the required vacuum, laser and cryogenic budget.
  3. Build the vacuum and baseline. Make a long, straight, evacuated tube or a drop tower, held at pressure low enough that residual gas does not scatter the sensor.
  4. Install the quantum optics. Add lasers, optical cavities, atom sources and detectors, aligned to fractions of a wavelength over the whole baseline.
  5. Isolate from the environment. Mount on seismic isolation, shield magnetically and thermally, and characterise every residual vibration and gravity gradient.
  6. Calibrate with known masses. Move a measured mass and confirm the instrument recovers the expected Newtonian signal before claiming any anomaly.
  7. Run and analyse. Collect data for long periods, look for a signal that grows with mass and shrinks with distance in the predicted way, and test alternative explanations.
  8. Replicate elsewhere. Reproduce the result on an independent apparatus — the step that separates a discovery from an artefact.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Laser cooling and atom interferometry — provides the quantum sensors that respond to gravity over metre-scale baselines.
  • EXISTS Quantum noise reduction (squeezed light) — already used in gravitational-wave detectors to measure displacements below the standard quantum limit.
  • PARTIAL Levitated and cryogenic optomechanics — needed to hold a macroscopic-enough mass in a genuine quantum superposition long enough to see gravity act on it.
  • PARTIAL Sensitivity to gravity over short baselines — probes so far reach distances far above any predicted quantum-gravity scale.
  • NOT YET Testable low-energy predictions — most quantum-gravity theories make no falsifiable prediction at energies any experiment can reach.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: long-baseline atom interferometers under construction, for example the 100 m MAGIS-100 at Fermilab and the AION programme in the UK — sensitivity to tiny gravitational and inertial signals in a new frequency band.
  2. Next: first experiments looking for entanglement created by gravity alone between two quantum masses — a direct test of whether gravity itself is a quantum field — the sharpest practical question in the field.
  3. Next: space-based interferometers such as LISA, planned for the 2030s, plus quantum equivalence-principle tests in free fall — very long baselines free of seismic noise.
  4. Longer term: kilometre-scale or space-deployed quantum sensors comparing clocks, atoms and masses — either a measured deviation from general relativity or the tightest limits yet on quantum gravity.

A fundamental theory in physics that describes the physical properties of nature at the scale of atoms and subatomic particles.
general relativity
A theory of gravitation developed by Albert Einstein, describing gravity as a curvature of spacetime caused by mass and energy.
spacetime
A mathematical model that combines three dimensions of space and one dimension of time into a single four-dimensional continuum.
