4How It Works

The process begins with DNA sequencing of the user's skin microbiome. This data is then used to identify beneficial bacterial strains that can be synthesized and formulated into skincare products. These probiotics are designed to interact positively with the user’s specific skin flora, potentially addressing issues such as acne or eczema.

Manufacturing involves complex biotechnological processes including DNA sequencing, bacterial synthesis, and formulation into skincare products. These steps require specialized equipment and expertise in molecular biology and bioengineering.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sampling and sequencing — collects and reads the DNA of skin microbes to identify which species are present. Key parts: skin swabs or tape strips, DNA extraction kits, 16S rRNA or metagenomic sequencers.
  • Bioinformatics analysis — turns sequence data into a picture of the microbial community and its functions. Key parts: reference databases, statistical and machine-learning tools.
  • Strain library and engineering — provides safe bacteria that can be selected or modified to correct a microbiome imbalance. Key parts: culture collection of skin commensals, genetic engineering tools, assays for beneficial traits.
  • Formulation and delivery — keeps live bacteria alive and delivers them to the skin in a usable product. Key parts: hydrogel or emulsion base, preservatives compatible with live cells, packaging that protects from oxygen and moisture.
  • Testing and quality control — verifies identity, purity, viability and safety of the final cocktail. Key parts: microbial culture and viability assays, genome sequencing for strain confirmation, sterility and pathogen tests.
  • Clinical evaluation — measures whether the product changes the skin microbiome and improves acne or eczema. Key parts: skin microbiome profiling before and after, symptom scoring, controlled trials.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Sample the skin. Swab the affected area and a control site, then store samples cold for transport.
  2. Sequence the microbiome. Extract microbial DNA and sequence marker genes or the whole metagenome to list species and genes.
  3. Diagnose the imbalance. Compare the community to healthy references to find missing beneficial strains or overgrown problem species.
  4. Select or engineer strains. Choose safe bacteria from a library that produce antimicrobial compounds or soothe inflammation, or modify them to do so.
  5. Culture the bacteria. Grow each selected strain under controlled conditions, then harvest and concentrate the cells.
  6. Formulate the product. Mix the live cells into a topical base that keeps them viable and allows them to colonise the skin.
  7. Quality control. Check strain identity, cell viability, and absence of contaminants before release.
  8. Test on the user. Apply the personalised cocktail, then re-sample the skin to confirm the microbiome shifted and symptoms eased in trials.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Skin microbiome sequencing and analysis — 16S rRNA and metagenomic methods can already profile skin communities.
  • PARTIAL Culturing and stabilising live bacteria for topical use — some probiotic cosmetics exist, but keeping personalised blends alive and active is difficult.
  • PARTIAL Genetic engineering of skin commensals — laboratory work has modified skin bacteria to produce beneficial molecules, but not yet in approved products.
  • PARTIAL Understanding of microbiome-host interactions — which strains help acne or eczema is still being mapped, so rational design is limited.
  • NOT YET Clinical evidence for personalised probiotics — no large trials have shown that a custom cocktail beats standard treatments.
  • NOT YET Regulatory pathway for live biotherapeutics — rules for personalised live products are unclear, slowing commercialisation.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: research links skin microbiome imbalances to acne and eczema; generic probiotic cosmetics are sold — consumer awareness and basic tools for microbiome testing.
  2. Next: commercial kits that sequence a user's skin microbiome and recommend generic probiotics or prebiotics — larger datasets to find which strains matter for which condition.
  3. Next: personalised cocktails of selected natural strains, tested in small clinical trials — evidence that custom blends can shift the microbiome and reduce symptoms.
  4. Longer term: engineered skin bacteria that sense inflammation and release treatments on demand — a closed-loop topical therapy for chronic skin disease.
  5. Longer term: regulatory approval and scalable manufacturing for personalised live biotherapeutics — mainstream clinical use beyond cosmetics.
