PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cell source and expansion — provides the patient's own skin cells so the graft is not rejected. Key parts: small donor-site biopsy, keratinocytes (surface cells), fibroblasts (dermal cells), culture media and flasks.
  • Bioink — a printable carrier that holds cells in place and later becomes the matrix. Key parts: hydrogel precursors (collagen, fibrin, alginate, hyaluronic acid), crosslinking agent, cells at controlled density.
  • Bioprinter — deposits the layers in a defined pattern onto a surface or directly into a wound. Key parts: print head (extrusion, inkjet or laser-assisted), sterile enclosure, motion stage, imaging to map the wound.
  • Dermal scaffold — gives the graft mechanical strength and a template for blood vessels to grow into. Key parts: printed or moulded matrix, pore structure, optional sacrificial support that dissolves.
  • Maturation system — grows the printed construct into a layered tissue before grafting. Key parts: bioreactor, air–liquid interface stage, temperature and gas control.
  • Release testing — proves the graft is sterile, viable and genetically normal before it touches a patient. Key parts: sterility and endotoxin tests, cell viability counts, histology.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Take a biopsy. About 1–2 cm² of the patient's own skin is taken from an uninjured site and transported to a clean lab.
  2. Separate the cell types. Enzymes break the tissue down; keratinocytes and fibroblasts are isolated and grown in separate cultures.
  3. Expand the cells. Over roughly 2–4 weeks the cells multiply to tens of millions, enough to seed a graft covering a much larger area than the biopsy.
  4. Formulate the bioink. Cells are mixed into a hydrogel whose viscosity and stiffness suit the printer and the target layer — fibroblasts for the deep layer, keratinocytes for the surface.
  5. Print the layers. The printer builds the construct layer by layer in a sterile enclosure, placing dermal cells in the base and surface cells on top, shaped to the wound.
  6. Stabilise and mature. The printed gel is crosslinked so it holds shape, then kept in a bioreactor; lifting it to an air–liquid interface makes the surface cells flatten and keratinise.
  7. Test and package. Samples are checked for sterility, viability and normal chromosomes, and the graft is packed for surgery within a short window.
  8. Graft and dress. Surgeons place the sheet on the prepared wound bed, secure it and cover it so the graft can take and the vessels can grow in.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Autologous cell culture — cultured epidermal autografts for burns have been used clinically since the 1980s, so cell expansion itself is proven.
  • PARTIAL Bioprinting of living cells — laser-assisted and extrusion bioprinting of skin cells has been demonstrated for grafts and for lab testing, but not yet at routine clinical scale.
  • NOT YET Vascularisation of thick tissue — grafts thicker than about a millimetre starve for oxygen unless blood vessels reach them quickly; printing pre-formed vessels is unsolved.
  • NOT YET Skin appendages — hair follicles, sweat and oil glands are not reproduced, so the graft is functional skin but not complete skin.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory route for living cell products — living-cell therapies need bespoke safety and manufacturing rules, and the pathway is still developing in most countries.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: autologous cell sprays and cultured epidermal sheets are used to close burns, alongside acellular and donor-cell skin substitutes — routine wound cover where a patient's own cells are available.
  2. Next: bioprinted bilayered autologous grafts reach late-stage clinical trials for burns and scars — a defined, reproducible replacement that includes both the dermal and surface layers.
  3. Next: printing directly into the wound during surgery — coverage of irregular burns in one procedure rather than weeks of lab culture.
  4. Longer term: vascularised grafts carrying follicles and glands — skin that restores sweating, sensation and hair, useful for chronic wounds and reconstruction, not only burns.
