Bioengineered Skincare involves the application of biotechnology techniques such as CRISPR and synthetic biology to develop advanced skincare treatments that can target specific skin issues at a molecular level.

4How It Works

CRISPR technology is used for precise gene editing, allowing for targeted modifications in skin cells. This enables the development of products that can enhance skin health by addressing underlying genetic factors contributing to conditions like aging and damage.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Ingredient discovery and design — finds or invents molecules that act on a skin process such as collagen breakdown, oxidation or inflammation. Key parts: genomic and metagenomic databases, peptide and protein design software, screening assays on cultured skin cells.
  • Host cell engineering — turns a microbe or plant cell into a factory for that molecule. Key parts: synthetic gene constructs, CRISPR or plasmid-based genome editing, codon optimisation, high-throughput strain screening.
  • Fermentation and bioprocessing — grows the engineered cells and recovers a pure ingredient at industrial scale. Key parts: seed train and bioreactors, sterile media and feeding, filtration and chromatography, quality control.
  • Delivery system — gets large, fragile molecules past the outer skin barrier, roughly 10–20 µm of dead stratum corneum. Key parts: liposomes and lipid nanoparticles, microneedle patches, emulsions and gels.
  • Formulation — keeps the active stable and pleasant in a tube for months. Key parts: pH and preservative system, stability testing, sensory and packaging design.
  • Microbiome engineering — uses live skin bacteria to produce actives on the skin or crowd out harmful species. Key parts: commensal chassis strains (e.g. Cutibacterium, Staphylococcus), contained kill-switches, colonisation testing.
  • Safety and efficacy testing — proves the product is safe and actually does something, without animal tests. Key parts: reconstructed human epidermis models, irritation and sensitisation assays, volunteer clinical trials.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the skin target. Researchers pick a process to change — collagen production, pigmentation, oxidative damage or a disrupted skin microbiome.
  2. Design the molecule. A peptide, enzyme or growth factor is designed or borrowed from another organism, and its gene sequence is written.
  3. Engineer the host. The gene, or a whole metabolic pathway, is inserted into yeast, bacteria or plant cells, and the best-performing strains are selected.
  4. Ferment. The engineered cells grow in stainless-steel bioreactors on sugar and nutrients over days, secreting or accumulating the ingredient.
  5. Purify. Filtration, centrifugation and chromatography separate the molecule from the broth; identity, purity and molecular weight are confirmed.
  6. Encapsulate. The active is packaged in liposomes, nanoparticles or a microneedle patch, because most proteins cannot cross intact skin on their own.
  7. Formulate. It is blended with emollients, water and preservatives, then tested for stability and microbial safety.
  8. Test and register. Reconstructed skin models and volunteer trials establish safety and effect; the product is registered under cosmetics rules.
  9. Scale up. Fermentation moves from litres to hundreds of cubic metres, and the ingredient enters many brands' supply chains.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Recombinant protein expression in microbes — the basis for fermentation-made collagen, hyaluronic acid and enzymes.
  • EXISTS CRISPR and metabolic pathway engineering — lets producers be edited to make molecules they never evolved to make.
  • EXISTS Industrial fermentation at hundreds of cubic metres — fermentation-derived ingredients must compete on cost with petrochemical and animal sources.
  • PARTIAL Skin delivery past the stratum corneum — molecules above roughly 500 daltons penetrate poorly, so large proteins need carriers or needles.
  • EXISTS Reconstructed human skin models — animal testing is banned for cosmetics in many markets, so efficacy and safety rely on lab-grown skin.
  • EXISTS Rules separating cosmetics from medicines — a product that changes gene expression or treats disease is regulated as a drug, which caps how far editing can go in a cosmetic.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: fermentation-derived squalane, hyaluronic acid and recombinant collagens sold in mainstream products — ingredients decoupled from animals and from scarce plant sources.
  2. Next: engineered skin-commensal bacteria and precision peptides backed by volunteer trial data — products that act on the skin's biology rather than just coating it.
  3. Next: personalised formulations based on an individual's skin microbiome and genetic profile — actives matched to a customer's own skin rather than sold as one-size-fits-all.
  4. Longer term: mRNA or gene-editing actives for skin — potentially durable changes to skin cells, but such products would most likely be medicines under current rules, not cosmetics.
