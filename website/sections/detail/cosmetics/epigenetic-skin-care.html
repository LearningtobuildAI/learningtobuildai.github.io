PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Active molecule — the compound intended to alter how a gene is read — either by loosening or tightening the packaging around DNA. Key parts: histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, sirtuin activators (SIRT1–7 are NAD+-dependent deacetylases), NAD+ precursors such as nicotinamide riboside, DNA methyltransferase modulators and methyl donors.
  • Delivery system — carries the active across the stratum corneum, the skin's main barrier. Key parts: liposomes or ethosomes, nanoemulsions, penetration enhancers, dissolving microneedle patches.
  • Target cells — the cells whose gene expression should actually change. Key parts: dermal fibroblasts (produce collagen I and III), epidermal keratinocytes (barrier proteins).
  • Assay and biomarker suite — proves the molecule reached the cell and changed the intended mark, not just that skin felt smoother. Key parts: skin biopsies, chromatin immunoprecipitation and sequencing, RNA sequencing, histone acetylation and DNA methylation staining, collagen protein quantification.
  • Formulation base — keeps a chemically fragile active stable on the shelf. Key parts: emulsion or serum base, antioxidants and chelators, pH buffer, preservative system.
  • Safety assessment — checks the active is not switching genes in the wrong tissue. Key parts: cell toxicity panels, skin absorption and systemic exposure measurements, oncology risk review.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose a target mark. Map what actually changes with age in skin — for example reduced histone acetylation or altered DNA methylation at the collagen gene promoter — and pick one to reverse.
  2. Screen candidate molecules. Test libraries of HDAC inhibitors, sirtuin activators and NAD+ precursors on cultured fibroblasts and on lab-grown human skin equivalents, measuring collagen messenger RNA and protein.
  3. Test penetration. Apply the candidate to human skin explants in diffusion cells and measure how much active crosses into the dermis. Most molecules above roughly 500 daltons, or water-soluble ones, barely pass.
  4. Engineer the vehicle. Encapsulate the active in lipid vesicles or load it into dissolving microneedles so enough reaches the fibroblasts without irritating the surface.
  5. Confirm target engagement. Run biopsies rather than cosmetic swabs: check the specific histone or methylation mark changed in the intended cells, not merely that a cream was applied.
  6. Check selectivity and safety. HDAC inhibitors are also approved systemic cancer drugs, so off-target effects in other tissues and any cancer-promoting risk must be excluded before long-term human use.
  7. Run controlled human trials. Randomised trials with biopsy endpoints at 12–24 weeks, comparing against a vehicle control, to separate the active from the moisturising base.
  8. Scale up manufacture. Fix the molecule and its formulation, then move to good-manufacturing-practice production with stability testing at 25 °C and 40 °C.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Topical delivery of molecules into the dermis — microneedles and vesicle systems work in principle, but reproducible delivery of a defined dose of a small-molecule modulator is not routine.
  • PARTIAL Validated epigenetic markers of skin ageing — age-related methylation and acetylation changes are documented in skin, but no single mark is confirmed as a reliable lever for collagen output.
  • NOT YET Selective, long-term-safe HDAC and sirtuin modulators — existing HDAC inhibitor drugs are potent systemic agents; a version safe for daily cosmetic use on healthy skin does not exist.
  • EXISTS Human skin models and biopsy-based assays — skin equivalents, explant cultures and chromatin assays are standard laboratory tools, so the testing route is open.
  • EXISTS Knowledge of collagen gene regulation — retinoids already demonstrate that topical agents can raise collagen production via nuclear receptors, giving a validated benchmark.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: marketed 'epigenetic' serums built on NAD+ precursors and sirtuin-activating plant extracts — consumer familiarity, but public evidence for biopsy-confirmed changes in human skin is essentially absent.
  2. Next: topical actives with published target-engagement data — a measured change in a specific epigenetic mark in human skin — turning a marketing category into a measurable mechanism.
  3. Next: delivery breakthroughs letting larger, more selective modulators reach fibroblasts at controlled doses — real dose–response data instead of in-vitro-only results.
  4. Longer term: controlled trials with collagen endpoints, regulated as drugs rather than cosmetics — a defensible claim to modify skin ageing at the level of gene expression, possibly combined with senolytic or partial-reprogramming research.
