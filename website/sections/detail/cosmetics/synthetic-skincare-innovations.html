4How It Works

By using CRISPR and other genetic engineering tools, scientists can precisely edit genes in microorganisms or cells to produce novel compounds with desired properties. These compounds are then integrated into skincare products to enhance their efficacy and performance.

The manufacturing process involves genetic engineering of microorganisms or cells to produce the desired compounds. These compounds are then extracted and purified before being incorporated into skincare products.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Producer organism — the engineered yeast or bacterium that becomes a living factory for the ingredient. Key parts: chassis strain (often baker's yeast or a bacterium), synthetic gene pathway, promoters and regulatory elements, selection markers.
  • Genetic design toolkit — decides which genes to write and edits the genome to install them. Key parts: DNA synthesis and assembly, CRISPR-based editing and other editing enzymes, enzyme and pathway databases, screening and sequencing.
  • Fermentation — grows the organism in liquid and feeds it sugar so it makes the target molecule. Key parts: seed train and bioreactor, sterile growth medium, temperature, pH and oxygen control, fed-batch sugar feed.
  • Downstream processing — separates the ingredient from the broth and purifies it to cosmetic grade. Key parts: cell separation, filtration, chromatography or extraction, drying and quality assays.
  • Formulation — turns the pure ingredient into a stable, skin-compatible product. Key parts: emulsions and carriers, liposomes or encapsulation, preservatives and pH buffers, stability testing.
  • Safety and efficacy testing — shows the ingredient is safe and actually does something for skin. Key parts: reconstructed human skin models, in-vitro cell assays, patch and irritation tests, microbiome sampling.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Choose the target molecule. Start from a compound with a known skin benefit — a collagen fragment, a lipid like squalane, an antioxidant or a peptide — and check it can be defined chemically.
  2. Map the biosynthetic route. Find the genes in the plant, fungus or animal that normally make the molecule, or design a new set of enzymatic steps that could build it from simple sugars.
  3. Synthesise and rewrite the genes. Optimise the DNA sequence for the chosen microbe and order synthetic gene constructs, assembling them into a pathway.
  4. Engineer the strain. Insert the pathway into yeast or bacteria and use gene editing to delete competing reactions and boost the supply of precursors; repeat design–build–test cycles until the yield rises.
  5. Screen candidate strains. Grow hundreds of variants in small wells, measure titre, and sequence the winners to learn what worked.
  6. Scale up fermentation. Run the best strain in progressively larger stirred tanks up to thousands of litres, tuning feed, aeration and temperature so the culture keeps producing.
  7. Recover and purify. Separate cells from broth, then use filtration, chromatography or solvent extraction to reach a purity suitable for skin contact.
  8. Formulate and test. Blend the ingredient into a cream or serum, check it survives shelf life, and test penetration and effect on reconstructed skin models.
  9. Clear safety and labelling. Run toxicology and irritation testing — in the EU cosmetic ingredients cannot be tested on animals — and document the ingredient for regulatory submission.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Industrial fermentation — brewing, enzymes and antibiotics already run at thousands of cubic metres, giving the equipment and know-how.
  • EXISTS Cheap DNA synthesis and sequencing — pathways can be built and screened in weeks rather than years.
  • EXISTS Precise gene editing — CRISPR-based tools let engineers delete unwanted reactions and tune gene expression in microbes.
  • PARTIAL Predictable skin biology and testing models — reconstructed skin and microbiome assays exist but still predict real-world anti-ageing effects imperfectly.
  • PARTIAL Competitive biomanufacturing economics — some molecules compete with plant or petrochemical routes on price, many still do not.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: bio-identical ingredients such as fermented hyaluronic acid and yeast-derived squalane sold at commercial scale — a proven supply chain for microbe-made cosmetic ingredients.
  2. Next: precision fermentation of more complex molecules — rare plant antioxidants, specialised lipids and recombinant structural proteins — ingredients that were previously too scarce or too expensive to extract.
  3. Next: CRISPR-designed peptides and enzymes tailored to specific skin targets — ingredients with a defined mechanism rather than a general moisturising effect.
  4. Longer term: probiotic and gene-editing approaches that act on the skin microbiome or skin cells directly — treatments that shift from cosmetic care toward dermatological therapy.
