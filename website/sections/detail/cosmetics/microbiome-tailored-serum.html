9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sampling kit — collects microbial DNA from the skin surface in a form stable enough to post to a lab. Key parts: sterile swab, preservative buffer, return packaging, instructions.
  • Sequencing and analysis — reads the microbial DNA and identifies which bacteria are present and in what relative amounts. Key parts: DNA extraction reagents, high-throughput sequencer, bioinformatics pipeline, reference database of skin microbes.
  • Formulation base — a neutral, stable serum that can accept added actives without separating or spoiling. Key parts: water, humectants, emulsifiers, preservatives, pH buffers.
  • Active ingredient library — a set of prebiotics, probiotics or postbiotics that can be selected to shift the skin flora. Key parts: fermented plant extracts, oligosaccharides, bacterial lysates, vitamins.
  • Custom blending and filling — combines the base and chosen actives in the right proportions and packages the final serum. Key parts: mixing vessels, dosing pumps, sterile filling line, bottles and labels.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Order and sample collection. The customer orders a kit online, swabs a defined area of skin, and returns the sample to a laboratory.
  2. DNA extraction and sequencing. The lab extracts microbial DNA from the swab and sequences marker genes such as 16S rRNA, or in some cases the whole metagenome.
  3. Bioinformatics analysis. Software compares the sequences to a reference database to list which microbial groups are present and estimate their relative abundance and diversity.
  4. Deficiency mapping. An algorithm flags groups that fall outside a reference range and links them to skin concerns such as dryness, sensitivity or breakouts.
  5. Formulation selection. A rules-based or machine-learning system chooses actives from the library that are intended to encourage beneficial groups or suppress undesirable ones.
  6. Compounding. A technician or automated system mixes a standard serum base with the selected actives in measured amounts, following a batch record.
  7. Filling and packaging. The serum is filled into bottles, labelled with the customer's formula, and shipped with usage instructions.
  8. Follow-up testing. After several weeks the customer may send a second swab so the lab can compare the microbial profile and adjust the next batch.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS High-throughput DNA sequencing — it reads the microbial DNA from a skin swab quickly and cheaply enough for a consumer service.
  • PARTIAL Reference databases of the skin microbiome — many skin bacteria are catalogued, but their roles and normal ranges are still being mapped.
  • PARTIAL Understanding of skin microbiome function — links between specific microbes and skin health are often correlational; causality is not always established.
  • EXISTS Cosmetic formulation and preservation — a serum must stay stable and safe while carrying actives that may be sensitive to heat, oxygen or microbes.
  • PARTIAL Personalized manufacturing and logistics — making one unique batch per customer at scale requires flexible factories and tracking from swab to bottle.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory framework for personalized cosmetics — rules on custom-made products and microbiome claims vary by country and are still evolving.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: Niche commercial services offer 16S sequencing and a personalized serum based on broad microbial categories. — Early adopters can try tailored care, and large datasets of skin microbiomes begin to accumulate.
  2. Next: Metagenomic and functional analysis become standard, linking specific strains or metabolites to skin outcomes. — More precise formulations with actives that have a clearer mechanistic rationale.
  3. Next: Clinical trials compare microbiome-tailored serums with standard products for conditions such as acne or eczema. — Medical credibility, clearer claims, and broader adoption beyond the wellness market.
  4. Longer term: Integration with other personal data (genetics, environment, lifestyle) and real-time skin sensors. — Dynamic, predictive skincare that adjusts as the skin and its microbes change.
  5. Longer term: On-demand, local manufacturing of personalized serums at pharmacy or home scale. — Zero-inventory, highly responsive supply with minimal shipping.
