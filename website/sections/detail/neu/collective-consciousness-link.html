PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Neural sensor array — records the electrical activity of many neurons at once. Key parts: penetrating microelectrode arrays, flexible injected mesh electronics, amplifiers and wiring.
  • Decoding processor — converts a person's neural activity into a signal another system can read. Key parts: spike-sorting and feature extraction, machine-learning decoders, per-user calibration.
  • Stimulation interface — writes a signal into a receiving brain. Key parts: microstimulation electrodes, sensory or cortical targets, dose control to avoid seizures.
  • Link layer — moves data between two or more implants. Key parts: wireless telemetry, compression and buffering, encryption and consent gates.
  • Shared representation — a common code both brains can map onto — the central unsolved problem. Key parts: cross-subject alignment models, latent-space encoder/decoder, error feedback.
  • Delivery and biocompatibility — gets the hardware in and keeps it working for years without immune rejection. Key parts: minimally invasive surgical route, anti-inflammatory coatings, long-term power supply.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Characterise one brain's code. Record from a volunteer for months while they perform tasks, then train a decoder that maps their activity to words, intentions or movements.
  2. Implant the recording array. Electrodes reach the cortex either through a small opening in the skull or as a fine mesh injected with a syringe and allowed to unfold.
  3. Implant the stimulation array. A second set of electrodes is placed over the area to be stimulated — typically sensory or motor cortex at first, because its effects are best understood.
  4. Build a shared intermediate code. Rather than one brain reading another directly, both sides are translated into a common intermediate representation. Finding a representation that survives the translation is the hard part; today it does not exist.
  5. Close the loop fast enough. The round trip from sender to receiver must be short enough to feel like conversation, which forces low-latency decoding and streaming, not batch processing.
  6. Demonstrate one-way, then two-way. Lab work so far has sent simple signals in one direction between two people; two-way exchange with feedback is the next rung.
  7. Scale beyond two participants. Serving a group means multiplexing several streams, plus rules for whose signal is being received.
  8. Prove safety and consent. Stimulation can trigger seizures and recording degrades as scar tissue forms, so long-duration trials and revocable consent are prerequisites, not afterthoughts.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-channel-count neural recording — hundreds to thousands of channels can be recorded in people, but stable recordings lasting years remain rare.
  • PARTIAL Decoding of speech and imagined speech — decoders can now reconstruct limited spoken-word sets from cortical activity, far short of free-form thought.
  • NOT YET Precise, non-damaging neural stimulation — electrical microstimulation is crude and can cause seizures; optogenetics needs genetic modification and has only been used in animals.
  • PARTIAL Long-term biocompatible implants — the immune response and electrode degradation shorten the working life of any interface.
  • NOT YET A shared inter-brain representation — no one has shown how to translate one person's neural pattern into a signal that means the same thing in another person's brain.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: assistive brain-computer interfaces that let paralysed people type or speak through a machine — clinical proof that cortical signals can be decoded reliably enough to be useful.
  2. Next: low-bandwidth brain-to-brain lab demonstrations between two people, ongoing since around 2014 — evidence that a signal recorded from one person can drive stimulation in another — but only for very simple, trained tasks.
  3. Next: long-lived, high-channel implants with two-way sensory feedback — richer signals and the possibility of exchanging intent or sensation rather than words.
  4. Longer term: a validated shared latent code between consenting participants — narrow-band exchange of simple states such as attention, arousal or motor intention.
  5. Speculative: group-scale thought and emotion sharing of the kind the tile describes — nothing demonstrable today; full 'collective consciousness' is not a foreseeable engineering target and may be impossible in principle, since each brain's meaning is built from its own experience.
