PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Theoretical models and simulations are used to replicate brain functions at the neural network level. This involves creating complex algorithms and hardware architectures designed to mimic the behavior of biological neurons and synapses, with the goal of achieving a state that can be interpreted as conscious experience.

5Materials Used
  • neuromorphic semiconductors
  • advanced ceramics
  • metamaterials
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Currently theoretical; no specific manufacturing processes have been developed. Requires advanced neuromorphic hardware design and materials science to create scalable, energy-efficient devices that can simulate large-scale neural networks.

7Build Process

Involves designing and simulating artificial neural networks on supercomputers or specialized neuromorphic hardware. Theoretical models are iteratively refined through simulations and experiments to better mimic biological systems.

8Energy Requirements

High for large-scale simulations; field units draw low hundreds of watts. Fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking and other specialized processes required for neuromorphic materials.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Neuromorphic substrate — hardware that mimics neurons and synapses in their own physics rather than simulating them as arithmetic. Key parts: spiking neural-network chips, memristor or analogue synapse arrays, on-chip memory and high-bandwidth interconnect.
  • Neural model — the equations and cell types that decide how activity propagates. Key parts: neuron and synapse models, connectivity and plasticity rules, network architecture.
  • Connectome data pipeline — the wiring diagram the model is built from. Key parts: serial-section electron microscopy, automated image segmentation, synapse annotation and proofreading.
  • Embodiment and sensors — gives the model a world to act in, so its internal states have causes. Key parts: cameras, microphones, touch sensors, actuators or simulated body, real-time control loop.
  • Measurement and theory — criteria for judging whether a system is conscious, and instruments to test them. Key parts: perturbational complexity measures, brain-activity recording (electrodes, calcium imaging, fMRI, EEG), competing theories and their predictions.
  • Compute and power — energy and silicon to run large models continuously. Key parts: GPU or neuromorphic clusters, cooling and power supply.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Map a nervous system. Slice tissue into nanometre-thin sections, image them, and trace every neuron and synapse by machine plus human proofreading. This has been completed only for small organisms such as the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, with around 300 neurons; partial maps exist for larger brains.
  2. Build a simulation of it. Turn the wiring diagram into a spiking network on GPUs or neuromorphic chips, choosing how detailed each neuron and synapse must be.
  3. Close the loop through a body. Connect the model to sensors and a simulated or robotic body so it receives input and its outputs change that input — models of small animals driving virtual bodies are the current benchmark.
  4. Calibrate against real brains. Compare the model's activity with recordings from the living organism and adjust cell properties and connection strengths until statistics match.
  5. Add the architecture theories point to. Introduce the features implicated in conscious processing — dense recurrent loops, a global broadcast-like pathway, thalamocortical-style feedback — rather than a purely feed-forward network.
  6. Test for markers, not behaviour. Apply measures such as perturbational complexity, which probes how rich and irreducible a system's response to a stimulus is, and check whether the model reproduces human-like signatures.
  7. Scale and iterate. Extend from insect-scale to mammalian cortex, keeping the model embodied and continuously checked against new recordings.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Whole-brain connectomics at mammalian scale — complete maps exist only for very small nervous systems; human-scale mapping is far beyond current imaging and proofreading throughput.
  • PARTIAL Neuromorphic computing at scale — research chips such as Intel's Loihi and Manchester's SpiNNaker show the principle, but no machine yet approaches a human brain's ~86 billion neurons.
  • PARTIAL Testable theories of consciousness — theories such as integrated information theory and global workspace theory make different predictions and are being compared in adversarial human experiments; none is settled.
  • PARTIAL Fine-grained measurement of living brains — electrodes and imaging reach limited numbers of neurons at once, so there is no full reference recording to validate a large model against.
  • NOT YET Energy-efficient computation — a human brain runs on roughly 20 watts; simulating one on conventional supercomputers needs megawatts.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: connectomes and closed-loop whole-brain models of insects and small vertebrates; consciousness theories tested in humans with brain stimulation and imaging — a data-driven loop between theory, measurement and simulation.
  2. Next: embodied models of a small mammal brain running in real time on neuromorphic hardware — testing whether biological-scale dynamics can be reproduced in silicon at practical power.
  3. Next: mammalian-cortical-scale models that learn and match living recordings — a candidate substrate on which consciousness markers could be measured, if any apply.
  4. Longer term: an agreed, validated test for machine consciousness — only then could anyone say whether a synthetic system is conscious — currently there is no such test, and it is an open question whether consciousness depends on biological tissue at all.
