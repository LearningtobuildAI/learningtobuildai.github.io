PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Consumer EEG & fNIRS wearables are non-invasive headbands that estimate brain activity and attention states using dry electrodes for electroencephalography (EEG) and near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) optodes to measure blood-oxygen levels. These devices translate the sensed signals into simple state metrics on-device, providing insights for consumer wellness, meditation, and sleep tracking.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Dry electrodes and fNIRS optodes are placed on the scalp to sense electrical activity in the brain (EEG) and changes in blood oxygenation (fNIRS). On-device algorithms process these signals to estimate various brain states such as focus, relaxation, or alertness. The results are displayed through a mobile app or integrated interface.

5Materials Used
  • Dry electrodes
  • Near-infrared optodes
  • Microprocessors
  • Battery
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves integrating dry electrodes, fNIRS optodes, and a processing unit in a lightweight, comfortable headband. The process requires precision in sensor placement and calibration to ensure accurate signal detection.

7Build Process

Components are assembled by placing dry electrodes and fNIRS optodes on the headband. On-device processors run algorithms that translate raw signals into usable data. Calibration is performed to optimize accuracy and user comfort.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sensor array — picks up electrical brain activity and blood-oxygen changes at the scalp. Key parts: dry EEG electrodes (conductive polymer or metal pins), near-infrared LEDs (approx. 700–900 nm), silicon photodiodes, flexible printed circuit board.
  • Analog front-end — amplifies and digitises very small signals without adding noise. Key parts: low-noise instrumentation amplifiers, bandpass filters, analog-to-digital converters, multiplexer for fNIRS channels.
  • On-board processing unit — removes artefacts and turns raw signals into simple state metrics in real time. Key parts: low-power microcontroller or digital signal processor, firmware, artefact-rejection algorithms, machine-learning classifiers.
  • Wireless and power — streams data to a phone and keeps the headband running for hours. Key parts: Bluetooth Low Energy module, rechargeable lithium-polymer battery, power management integrated circuit, charging contacts.
  • Mechanical headset — holds sensors against the scalp comfortably and stably. Key parts: adjustable headband, flexible arms, foam or silicone cushions, internal cable routing.
  • Companion software — shows the user focus, sleep, or stress estimates and guides sessions. Key parts: mobile app, signal visualisation, state classification display, optional cloud processing.
10How It's Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the sensor layout. Choose EEG electrode positions (often frontal and temporal sites) and fNIRS optode separations of roughly 2–3 cm to target cortical regions; simulate light penetration and signal strength.
  2. Fabricate dry electrodes and optodes. Mould conductive polymer or machine metal pins for gel-free EEG; package near-infrared LEDs and photodiodes with optical shielding to prevent crosstalk.
  3. Build the analog front-end. Lay out a low-noise printed circuit board, shield it from interference, and test with known signals — a function generator for EEG and a tissue phantom for fNIRS.
  4. Develop signal processing and models. Implement artefact removal for eye blinks and motion, extract features such as band power and haemoglobin changes, and train machine-learning classifiers on labelled datasets.
  5. Integrate into the headset. Fit the electronics into a comfortable form factor, ensure stable skin contact, and manage heat and battery placement for wear over hours.
  6. Write firmware. Code real-time acquisition, filtering, feature computation, and wireless transmission; optimise for low power so a small battery lasts a full day.
  7. Build the companion app. Design a user interface that displays focus, sleep, and stress metrics, with onboarding and calibration steps for each user.
  8. Validate accuracy. Compare outputs against research-grade EEG and fNIRS systems in user studies; refine algorithms and calibration to reduce false readings.
  9. Scale manufacturing. Set up production and quality control, and meet regulatory requirements for a wellness device rather than a medical one.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Dry electrode technology — enables gel-free, long-term wear without a technician to apply conductive paste.
  • EXISTS Near-infrared light sources and detectors — fNIRS needs stable, low-power emitters and sensitive photodiodes that work through hair and skin.
  • EXISTS Low-power microcontrollers and Bluetooth — allow on-device processing and wireless streaming without draining the battery.
  • PARTIAL Machine learning for physiological signals — models for attention and sleep from EEG and fNIRS are still less accurate than lab-grade equipment.
  • PARTIAL Understanding of neurovascular coupling — linking blood-oxygen changes to neural activity is well-studied but varies between people and tasks.
12Where It Goes Next
  1. Now: single-modality EEG headbands for meditation, sleep staging, and basic focus training — consumer access to simple brain-state feedback at home.
  2. Next: combined EEG and fNIRS headbands that improve state classification and reduce false positives — more reliable focus and stress tracking, and richer sleep metrics.
  3. Next: closed-loop neurofeedback and adaptive environments that adjust lighting, audio, or tasks in real time — personalised wellness interventions that respond to the user's state.
  4. Longer term: clinical validation for specific indications such as sleep disorders or attention difficulties — home-based monitoring and telemedicine support, if regulators approve the claims.

18Glossary
EEG (Electroencephalography)
A technique for recording electrical activity along the scalp to measure brain waves.
fNIRS (Functional Near-Infrared Spectroscopy)
A non-invasive method of measuring changes in blood flow and oxygenation in the brain.
Dry electrodes
Electrodes that do not require conductive gel, making them more user-friendly but potentially less sensitive than wet electrodes.
On-device processing
The capability of a wearable device to process and analyze data locally rather than sending it to a remote server for analysis.
