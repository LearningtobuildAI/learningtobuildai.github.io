PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Quantum Brain Mapping is a medical technology that uses quantum sensors, specifically superconducting qubits, to create high-resolution images of the human brain. This technique aims to provide detailed insights into brain structure and function without invasive procedures.

Category
Medical Technology
Best use
Detailed brain mapping, personalized medicine

2Problem It Solves

Current medical imaging techniques such as MRI and fMRI have limitations in terms of resolution and non-invasiveness. Quantum Brain Mapping aims to overcome these limitations by providing higher resolution images that can reveal finer details of brain structure and function, potentially leading to better diagnosis and personalized treatment plans.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Superconducting qubits are used as highly sensitive detectors to measure magnetic fields generated by the brain's neural activity. These sensors can detect minute changes in the brain, allowing for precise imaging at a cellular level. The data collected is then processed using advanced algorithms to generate detailed maps of the brain.

  • superconducting materials
  • semiconductor substrates
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves creating superconducting qubits using advanced semiconductor fabrication techniques. These qubits are then integrated into imaging devices which require precise alignment and calibration.

7Build Process

The build process includes designing the quantum sensors, fabricating them on a substrate, integrating them with readout electronics, and calibrating the entire system to ensure accurate signal detection.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. Operation requires cryogenic cooling, which adds significant energy consumption.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Sensor array — measures the faint magnetic fields (roughly 10–1000 femtotesla) produced by currents in the brain. Key parts: optically pumped magnetometer cells, or SQUID gradiometers, sensor mounts.
  • Optics and drive electronics — prepares each sensor and reads out its signal. Key parts: laser or LED sources, photodetectors, low-noise amplifiers and digitizers, heater and current controllers.
  • Magnetic shielding and field control — suppresses the Earth's field and urban magnetic noise, which are millions of times larger than brain signals. Key parts: mu-metal shielded room, active cancellation coils, vibration isolation.
  • Sensor helmet — holds sensors a few millimetres from the scalp so signals stay strong. Key parts: 3D-printed rigid or flexible helmet, cable routing, head-position tracking.
  • Reconstruction software — turns magnetic readings into 3D maps of brain activity, usually with millimetre-to-centimetre localisation. Key parts: forward head model, source reconstruction algorithms, co-registration with MRI, visualisation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the array. Engineers decide how many sensors, how they are oriented, and how densely they cover the scalp, trading coverage against cost and cross-talk.
  2. Fabricate the sensors. For an atomic magnetometer, a glass cell a few millimetres across is filled with an alkali metal such as rubidium plus a buffer gas and sealed. For a SQUID, a Josephson junction is patterned on a chip with thin-film niobium.
  3. Build sensor heads. Each cell is packaged with its laser, optics, photodetector and small coils so it can run as a self-contained magnetometer.
  4. Prepare the room. A magnetically shielded room is assembled from layers of high-permeability metal and aluminium, with coil sets that cancel the residual field to a few nanotesla.
  5. Make the helmet. The helmet is printed to fit a head size category, with pockets that position each sensor close to the scalp — the key advantage over fixed cryogenic helmets.
  6. Wire up the electronics. Amplifiers, digitizers and laser drivers are connected, with synchronised timing so all channels can be averaged together.
  7. Calibrate. Known magnetic signals are applied to establish each sensor's gain, orientation and cross-talk, and the whole array is checked for drift and noise.
  8. Validate and reconstruct. Recordings are co-registered with anatomical MRI, run through source reconstruction, and compared against established measurements before clinical use.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Ultrasensitive magnetic sensing (SQUIDs and atomic magnetometers) — brain signals are about ten orders of magnitude weaker than the Earth's magnetic field.
  • EXISTS Magnetic shielding and active field cancellation — without it, ambient magnetic noise swamps the signal.
  • EXISTS Cryogenics for SQUID sensors — older MEG systems need liquid-helium cooling, which fixes sensors in a rigid helmet.
  • PARTIAL Room-temperature quantum sensors — optically pumped magnetometers work without cryogenics, allowing wearable helmets, but dense arrays are still maturing.
  • EXISTS Source reconstruction and head modelling — the measured fields must be inverted mathematically to locate activity in the brain.
  • PARTIAL Clinical evidence for personalised use — linkage from imaging findings to individual treatment decisions is still being established.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: cryogenic SQUID MEG in hospitals and research centres, plus early commercial wearable optically pumped systems — millisecond-resolution mapping of brain activity, especially before epilepsy surgery.
  2. Next: wearable helmets allowing movement, and use with children and patients who cannot stay still — routine bedside and paediatric imaging, and scanning during natural behaviour.
  3. Next: fusion of quantum-sensor recordings with MRI, EEG and stimulation — multimodal maps precise enough to guide personalised treatment of epilepsy, stroke and movement disorders.
  4. Longer term: closed-loop systems combining real-time mapping with neuromodulation — therapy steered by live brain signals, and portable scanners outside shielded rooms if sensor and shielding technology allows.

18Glossary
qubits
Quantum bits are the basic units of quantum information and computation. They can exist in multiple states simultaneously, allowing for highly sensitive detection of minute changes.
superconducting sensors
Devices that operate at very low temperatures with zero electrical resistance, enabling them to detect extremely weak magnetic fields generated by the brain's neural activity.
