PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Imaging — resolve every neuron, axon and synapse in a preserved human brain at nanometre resolution. Key parts: electron microscopes, automated sectioning or serial block-face systems, brain fixation and staining, cryo-preservation for intact tissue.
  • Reconstruction pipeline — turn petabytes of images into a wiring diagram. Key parts: image alignment, automated segmentation of neurons, synapse detection, human proofreading, data storage and compute.
  • Parameter extraction — infer not just who connects to whom, but how strongly and with what dynamics. Key parts: cell-type classification, synaptic weight estimates, neurotransmitter and receptor inference, gap-junction and glia handling.
  • Emulation model — simulate the network's activity. Key parts: neuron model (from simple point neurons to detailed multi-compartment models), synaptic and plasticity rules, numerical solvers, neural simulation software.
  • Compute substrate — run the model fast enough to keep up with real time. Key parts: GPUs or neuromorphic chips, high-bandwidth memory, interconnect, power and cooling.
  • Interface and persistence — give the emulation senses, actions and a stable place to run. Key parts: sensory input encoding, motor output decoding, embodiment or virtual environment, redundant storage and maintenance.
  • Validation — test whether the copy behaves like, or is, the original person. Key parts: comparison to recorded neural activity, behavioural tests, philosophical and ethical review.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Preserve the brain. For a theoretical whole-brain emulation the tissue would first be chemically fixed, or ideally cryo-preserved and then fixed, so the wiring stops changing. No method today preserves a whole human brain well enough to trace every synapse.
  2. Section and image. The brain would be cut into tens of thousands of thin slices and each imaged by electron microscopy, or imaged block-face layer by layer — roughly a petabyte-scale dataset per cubic millimetre.
  3. Reconstruct the wiring. Automated software aligns the images, segments neurons and traces axons and dendrites; human proofreaders correct errors. A full adult fruit-fly brain took this route and was published in 2024; a human brain is roughly six orders of magnitude larger.
  4. Infer synapse properties. The wiring diagram shows where synapses are, but not their strength, receptor type or short-term dynamics. These would have to be estimated from cell type, synapse size and molecular labels — a major source of error.
  5. Choose model fidelity. Each neuron is replaced by a mathematical model, from a simple point neuron up to a multi-compartment model with dendrites and ion channels. Higher fidelity needs far more compute but captures more of the brain's behaviour.
  6. Simulate the network. The model runs on a large computer or neuromorphic chip. Estimates for emulating a human brain in real time range from many petaflops to exaflops, and even the largest current machines have not run a whole mammalian brain at full detail in real time.
  7. Connect input and output. The emulation needs sensory streams and a way to act, either in a virtual body or through hardware. This is the point at which it would begin to behave like a person rather than a recording.
  8. Validate against the original. Activity and behaviour are compared with data from the living person or animal. Without a validated test for consciousness, this remains a proxy — matching behaviour, not proving inner experience.
  9. Run and maintain it. The emulation would need continuous power, redundant storage and backups against hardware failure. Whether a pause, copy or restart preserves identity is unresolved and is a philosophical question, not an engineering one.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • NOT YET Synapse-resolution connectomics of a whole human brain — complete connectomes exist only for small organisms — the nematode C. elegans and, in 2024, the adult fruit fly — not for any mammal, let alone a human.
  • NOT YET Readout of synaptic strength and dynamics — electron microscopy reveals structure, not the weights and time constants a simulation needs; these can only be inferred indirectly today.
  • NOT YET Real-time human-scale simulation — the largest supercomputers can simulate fractions of a mammalian brain, not a whole human one, and not faster than biology.
  • PARTIAL Preservation of an intact human brain — chemical fixation and cryo-preservation preserve structure imperfectly, and perfusion quality varies across a whole human brain.
  • NOT YET A test of machine consciousness — no accepted method exists to confirm that an emulation is conscious, so the central claim of the technology cannot currently be verified.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Today: connectomes of small brains and mammalian cortical chunks — proof that automated reconstruction can scale, and benchmarks for segmentation and synapse detection.
  2. Next: a complete mouse brain connectome and functional simulation of its cortical circuits — the first full wiring diagram of a mammal and methods for linking structure to behaviour.
  3. Next: emulation of significant human cortical circuits on large computers — useful neuroscience and, perhaps, brain-inspired computing — but not a person.
  4. Longer term: hypothetical whole human emulation with sensory embodiment — whatever it is that such a system would be — the ethical, legal and identity questions would arrive before the technology does.
