PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Tissue preparation — preserves the brain's wiring at the moment of death or biopsy, then makes it electron-dense. Key parts: perfusion or immersion fixation, heavy-metal staining (osmium, uranium, lead), resin embedding.
  • Sectioning — cuts or exposes the block in tens-of-nanometre steps. Key parts: ultramicrotome, tape or wafer collection of thousands of serial sections, focused-ion-beam milling (block-face imaging).
  • Imaging — scans every section into a digital image stack. Key parts: scanning electron microscope, multi-beam or fast detectors, vibration isolation and automation.
  • Alignment and stitching — registers thousands of images into one coherent volume. Key parts: cross-section registration software, tile stitching, distortion correction.
  • Segmentation — traces the boundary of every neuron and process. Key parts: machine-learning segmentation models, human proofreading interfaces.
  • Synapse annotation — finds the actual connections and their direction. Key parts: synaptic-cleft detection, vesicle and spine classifiers.
  • Data and compute — stores and serves datasets far larger than the source tissue. Key parts: petabyte-scale object storage, GPU clusters, visualisation and query tools.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Obtain and fix tissue. Consented surgical or post-mortem human tissue is chemically fixed within minutes so structures do not degrade; only small blocks can be handled today, typically around a cubic millimetre.
  2. Stain and embed. Heavy-metal stains bind to membranes and vesicles, making them scatter electrons differently; the tissue is then infiltrated with resin and hardened.
  3. Cut into serial sections. A diamond knife shaves sections roughly 30 nanometres thick, which are collected on tape or wafers. A block-face alternative mills away a thin layer and images the fresh surface, avoiding handling thousands of fragile sections.
  4. Image every section. A scanning electron microscope rasterises each section at a few nanometres per pixel. This resolves membranes and synaptic vesicles, but not molecules or atoms — 'atomic resolution' is not what this method delivers.
  5. Align the stack. Software registers each image to its neighbour, correcting for stretching, folding and cutting artefacts, so a single neuron can be followed from top to bottom.
  6. Segment the cells. Trained neural networks propose boundaries for every axon, dendrite and glial process; human proofreaders then fix the errors, which is the largest labour cost.
  7. Identify synapses. Regions where two processes touch and vesicles cluster are marked as connections, with direction inferred from which side holds the vesicles.
  8. Build the graph and analyse. The result is a directed wiring diagram that can be searched for cell types and motifs. Most projects map neurons only; glia, neuromodulators and the brain's electrical dynamics are largely absent.
  9. Scale up. Reaching a whole human brain would multiply the data volume by roughly a million — an order of a zettabyte of image data by simple extrapolation from published cubic-millimetre datasets — and would require imaging, storage and proofreading throughput that do not exist.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Automated serial-section electron microscopy — without reliable automation, millions of images per sample cannot be collected.
  • PARTIAL Machine-learning segmentation — models are good but still need heavy human proofreading; that labour, not imaging, is the scaling wall.
  • EXISTS Petabyte-scale data infrastructure — a single cubic millimetre already produces petabytes, so storage and serving are proven at that size but not a million times larger.
  • PARTIAL Expansion microscopy — swelling tissue inside a polymer gel lets ordinary microscopes resolve finer detail and can be combined with electron imaging, but it is not yet the backbone of large connectomes.
  • PARTIAL Ethical access to human brain tissue — whole-brain preservation requires donation and consent pathways at a scale that does not yet exist.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: complete wiring diagrams of small nervous systems and small volumes of mammalian cortex — proof that structure can be recovered at synapse level, plus public datasets and tooling.
  2. Next: whole-brain connectome of a small mammal such as a mouse — the first complete wiring diagram of a mammalian brain and much faster automated pipelines.
  3. Next: many human regions, and cross-referencing with non-invasive imaging and physiology — structure–function links, and comparison of healthy and diseased tissue.
  4. Longer term: a full human brain connectome — a static structural reference for the brain — a constraint on theories of how experience and cognition arise, not an explanation of consciousness, which also depends on dynamics, chemistry and glia that such a map would miss.
