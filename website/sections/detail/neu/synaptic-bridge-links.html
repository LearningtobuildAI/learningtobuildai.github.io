PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Neural recording — listens to the sender's brain. Key parts: scalp EEG electrodes, electrocorticography (ECoG) grids, intracortical microelectrode arrays, low-noise amplifiers and digitisers.
  • Decoding pipeline — converts recorded activity into a message. Key parts: feature extraction, machine-learning classifier, real-time inference hardware.
  • Encoding pipeline — turns that message into stimulation parameters suited to the receiver's cortex. Key parts: individual cortical maps, stimulus waveform generator, intensity safety limits.
  • Transmission link — carries the message between the two nervous systems, usually over ordinary digital networks. Key parts: low-latency network path, error correction, session authentication.
  • Neural stimulation — writes the message into the receiver's brain. Key parts: transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) coil, transcranial focused ultrasound transducer, implanted electrode array with current drivers.
  • Timing and feedback — keeps sender and receiver in step and lets them adapt. Key parts: synchronisation clock, latency monitor, behavioural feedback display.
  • Safety and consent layer — stops the interface harming or coercing a participant. Key parts: charge-density and temperature limits, electrode impedance monitoring, session logging and consent protocols.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Map both brains. Functional imaging and, where implanted electrodes exist, direct mapping locate the cortical areas that will be read from and written to.
  2. Fit the sender's sensors. An EEG cap is applied with conductive gel, or an already-implanted array is connected; signal quality is checked against a baseline.
  3. Train the sender's decoder. Over many sessions the sender performs a simple mental task, such as imagining a hand movement, while a classifier learns the pattern that distinguishes it.
  4. Calibrate the receiver's encoder. Stimulation sites and intensities are tested until they reliably evoke a faint sensation — a phosphene, a twitch — that is noticeable but not painful.
  5. Restrict to a low-bandwidth code. Because the channel is so narrow, the pair agree on a simple code: one detectable signal means 'yes', its absence means 'no', and the task is built around that.
  6. Close the loop. The decoded state is transmitted, converted into a stimulus, and delivered to the receiver while both participants see the shared task and adjust.
  7. Add error checking. Redundant trials and confidence thresholds are used so that a single misread pattern does not become a wrong message.
  8. Increase channel count. More electrodes, sharper stimulation and better decoders are added step by step to raise the number of distinguishable signals.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Reliable, long-lived neural recording — implanted arrays lose signal over months, and scalp EEG is blurred by the skull.
  • EXISTS Real-time neural decoding — classifiers already turn motor-imagery patterns into control signals for prostheses and spellers.
  • NOT YET Focal, non-invasive stimulation with millisecond precision — TMS is precise in time but coarse in space, so it excites broad patches of cortex rather than chosen neurons.
  • NOT YET A workable neural code for writing information — we can evoke crude sensations but cannot yet dictate what pattern of activity a specific perception requires.
  • EXISTS Fast, low-latency digital transport — the network side is easy; the bottleneck is entirely at the two brains.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: laboratory demonstrations between a handful of participants, such as the University of Washington human-to-human link reported in 2014 and the three-person 'BrainNet' experiment published in 2019 — proof that a signal can pass from one nervous system into another, at a few bits per minute at best.
  2. Next: sensory-substitution and sensory-restoration research that reliably writes patterned activity into cortex — a richer alphabet than a single on/off signal.
  3. Next: bidirectional closed-loop links in which both partners both send and receive, with learned mappings for each individual — communication rather than one-way triggering.
  4. Longer term: a common neural interface standard with agreed codecs, consent rules and privacy protections — any wider use — and forces society to decide what should never be shared between minds.
