PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Neuralink's technology involves the development of implantable neural interfaces that can directly interact with the human nervous system, aiming to restore motor function in paralyzed individuals and enable direct communication between the mind and external devices.

2Problem It Solves

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

Microelectrodes are implanted into specific regions of the brain. These electrodes communicate with a wireless receiver placed under the scalp, which is connected to an external device that can interpret neural signals and send commands back to the brain.

5Materials Used
  • Silicon
  • Biocompatible polymers
  • Metal electrodes
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The manufacturing process involves designing and fabricating microelectrodes, embedding them into flexible silicon substrates, and encapsulating them in biocompatible materials. The implantation process requires precise surgical techniques to ensure proper placement within the brain.

7Build Process

Neuralink's build process includes multiple stages: design of electrodes and substrate, fabrication using cleanroom technology, assembly of the implantable device, and testing for biocompatibility and functionality before clinical trials.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking. Long-term power consumption for implants could be in the range of milliwatts once optimized.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Implant — records electrical activity from individual neurons in the brain. Key parts: flexible polymer threads with microelectrodes, custom low-power amplifier chip, hermetic enclosure.
  • Surgical robot — inserts the threads into the brain with micron precision while avoiding blood vessels. Key parts: robotic arm, fine needle, imaging and guidance system.
  • Decoding software — translates neural spike patterns into commands for external devices. Key parts: signal processing algorithms, machine learning models, calibration interface.
  • Wireless telemetry — sends neural data out and receives power through intact skin. Key parts: radio transmitter, inductive charging coil, external receiver.
  • Biocompatible packaging — protects the implant from corrosion and the body from irritation. Key parts: thin-film coatings, sealing materials.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the implant. Engineers lay out the chip and the electrode patterns on flexible threads, simulating how they will record from neurons.
  2. Fabricate the threads. Metal layers are deposited and patterned on a polymer film, then etched and released to create hair-thin threads with many electrodes.
  3. Assemble the implant. The threads are bonded to the chip, and the whole assembly is encapsulated in a biocompatible polymer.
  4. Test in vitro and in animals. The implant is tested in saline and then in animal models to verify recording quality and safety.
  5. Surgical implantation. A robot makes a small hole in the skull and inserts the threads into the brain at precise depths and locations.
  6. Calibrate. The patient attempts specific movements while the system records neural activity, mapping which signals correspond to which intentions.
  7. Decode. Real-time algorithms convert the recorded spikes into control signals for a cursor, keyboard or robotic limb.
  8. Monitor and maintain. The implant is wirelessly charged and updated, and its performance is tracked over months and years.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Flexible microelectrode arrays — threads must bend with the brain without damaging tissue, unlike rigid arrays.
  • EXISTS Low-power wireless telemetry — medical implants already use radio links and inductive charging to avoid wires through the skin.
  • PARTIAL Real-time neural decoding — algorithms have controlled cursors and robotic arms in research, but stability over years is unproven.
  • PARTIAL Minimally invasive neurosurgery — robotic insertion reduces bleeding and damage, but long-term safety is still being studied.
  • NOT YET Long-term biocompatibility — the brain's immune response can degrade recordings over time, and no implant has yet proven decades of reliability.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: early clinical trials in paralysed patients enabling cursor control — proof that the implant works safely in humans for basic tasks.
  2. Next: expanded trials for speech, handwriting and robotic limb control — restoring richer communication and movement for people with paralysis.
  3. Next: higher channel counts and fully wireless implants with no external wires — longer-term use without risk of infection and more natural control.
  4. Longer term: regulatory approval for medical use and exploration of broader conditions — routine clinical availability for severe motor impairment and possibly other neurological disorders.
