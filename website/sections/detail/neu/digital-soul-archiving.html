PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Tissue preservation and sectioning — stabilise the brain and cut it into slices thin enough to image every synapse. Key parts: fixatives and cryoprotectants, ultramicrotome, automated tape-collecting lathe.
  • Volumetric imaging — capture nanometre-resolution images of neurons, organelles and synapses across the whole volume. Key parts: serial-section electron microscope, multi-beam scanning electron microscope, expansion microscopy.
  • Image processing pipeline — turn terabytes of images into a coherent 3D volume. Key parts: alignment software, stitching, contrast enhancement.
  • Segmentation and synapse detection — trace every neuron and identify where it connects to others. Key parts: machine-learning segmentation, synapse classifier, proofreading interfaces.
  • Connectome and weight mapping — assemble the wiring diagram and estimate the strength of each connection. Key parts: graph database, synaptic size and type measurements, neurotransmitter identification.
  • Emulation platform — run a simulated version of the mapped brain. Key parts: neuron and synapse models, neuromorphic hardware or supercomputer, sensory and motor interfaces.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Preserve the brain. Perfuse with fixative immediately after death to lock proteins and membranes in place, then stain heavy metals to make membranes visible to electrons.
  2. Embed and section. Saturate with resin, then cut the whole brain into tens of thousands of ultrathin slices, each collected on tape or a wafer.
  3. Image every slice. Scan each slice with an electron microscope at a few nanometres per pixel, producing petabytes of images.
  4. Align and stitch. Computationally register adjacent slices and correct distortions so that every neurite can be followed continuously.
  5. Trace neurons and find synapses. Machine-learning algorithms segment each neuron’s branches and flag the tiny junctions where two neurons meet.
  6. Estimate connection strengths. Measure synapse size, type and location to infer how strongly one neuron drives another; this is the weakest link in the chain.
  7. Add functional dynamics. Combine the structure with electrophysiology and ion-channel data to model how signals actually propagate and change over time.
  8. Simulate and calibrate. Run the model on a neuromorphic chip or supercomputer, then compare its activity with recordings from the original brain and adjust until it behaves similarly.
  9. Interface with the world. If the simulation is to act, connect it to cameras, microphones and robotic limbs so it can sense and respond.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Nanoscale brain imaging — electron microscopes can resolve synapses, but imaging a whole human brain at that resolution has never been done.
  • PARTIAL Automated image segmentation — machine learning traces neurons in small volumes, but human proofreading is still needed for accuracy.
  • NOT YET Synaptic weight measurement — structure alone does not reveal the strength or sign of a connection; we cannot yet read the exact weights that store memory and skill.
  • PARTIAL Brain-scale neural simulation — cortical columns and small circuits can be simulated, but a whole human brain in real time is far beyond current computing.
  • NOT YET Understanding of neural coding — without knowing how information is represented in spike patterns, even a perfect wiring diagram cannot be made to think or remember correctly.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: complete connectomes of small organisms such as the nematode and the fruit fly, plus partial maps of mammalian cortex — proof that dense synaptic wiring can be extracted from electron microscopy.
  2. Next: a full mouse brain connectome linked to functional recordings — testing whether structure plus activity can predict behaviour in a mammal.
  3. Next: a complete structural map of a human brain — the raw dataset needed for any emulation attempt, though weights and dynamics would still be missing.
  4. Longer term: a calibrated, embodied emulation that behaves like the original person — the theoretical goal of migrating a mind — contingent on solving weight measurement, neural coding and computing scale.
