PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Electrode array — the part in contact with neurons — a flexible mesh or stent-mounted film that records and stimulates. Key parts: conductive polymer or thin-film metal traces, flexible substrate (polymer), electrode sites, tens to thousands.
  • Delivery system — gets the array into the brain without open surgery on the cortex. Key parts: fine needle or syringe for tissue injection, catheter and guidewire for endovascular routes, deployment mechanism that unfurls the mesh or expands the stent.
  • On-implant electronics — amplifies and digitises faint neural signals next to the source. Key parts: low-power application-specific chip, multiplexing, stimulation driver.
  • Wireless link and power — carries data and power through skin and bone with no wires crossing the body. Key parts: inductive coil or radio/ultrasonic transceiver, power management, antenna.
  • External unit and decoding — turns spike trains into commands and streams feedback back. Key parts: headpiece transceiver, real-time decoding software, calibration interface.
  • Biocompatibility barrier — keeps the immune system from walling off the electrodes. Key parts: soft, tissue-matched mechanical properties, antifouling coatings.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the electrode map. Decide how many channels are needed, how densely they must sit, and which brain regions they must reach.
  2. Fabricate the mesh. Thin polymer and metal layers are patterned on a sacrificial wafer by the same lithography used for computer chips, then released as a free-standing, flexible mesh with conducting pathways and recording sites.
  3. Mount or compress it. For an endovascular device, electrodes are fixed onto a self-expanding metal stent. For an injectable mesh, the whole structure is folded and drawn into the bore of a needle of roughly 100 micrometres.
  4. Add the chip. A low-power amplifier and multiplexer is bonded to the array's tail so raw signals leave the body already digitised.
  5. Coat for the body. Antifouling and anti-inflammatory coatings are applied to reduce the scar tissue that otherwise insulates electrodes.
  6. Deliver it. Either inject through a small opening in the skull into the cortex, letting the mesh unfurl among neurons, or thread a catheter through a neck vein into a large blood vessel lying against the brain surface, where the stent opens against the vessel wall.
  7. Link and calibrate. The external unit pairs with the implant, checks electrode impedance and records baseline activity.
  8. Close the loop. Decoding software learns the person's neural patterns, and stimulation channels return information — the step that would make this a two-way interface.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Flexible thin-film neural probes — mesh and polymer arrays have been made and implanted in animals, proving the fabrication route works.
  • PARTIAL Endovascular access to the brain — stent-mounted electrode arrays have been implanted in human clinical trials, but channel counts and longevity are limited.
  • EXISTS Low-power implantable chips and wireless links — hearing implants and deep-brain stimulators already run for years inside the body.
  • NOT YET Long-term electrode stability — scar tissue and corrosion degrade recordings within months to a few years; a lifelong interface needs far longer.
  • PARTIAL High-bandwidth neural decoding — current systems read a few hundred channels for cursor or speech control; 'seamless, high-speed' links need orders of magnitude more.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: endovascular electrode arrays in human trials restoring typing and communication for people with paralysis; injectable mesh electrodes shown in animals — proof that a brain interface can be installed without opening the skull.
  2. Next: wireless mesh implants with several hundred to a few thousand stable channels — richer signals for speech and prosthetic control, without a cable through the skin.
  3. Next: bidirectional implants that write as well as read, with decade-scale stability — sensory feedback and closed-loop therapy.
  4. Longer term: a distributed, body-wide mesh linking brain to digital systems at high bandwidth — the 'neural lace' idea proper — still speculative, and gated by biology more than by electronics.
