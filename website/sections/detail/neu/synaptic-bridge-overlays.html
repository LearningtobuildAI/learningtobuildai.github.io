PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cortical interface layer — the physical contact with brain tissue — reading the firing of neurons and writing activity back. Key parts: penetrating microelectrode arrays or flexible polymer threads, on-site amplifiers, stimulation electrodes.
  • Wireless power and data link — connects the implanted layer to external compute without wires through the skull. Key parts: ultrasound or radio-frequency transceivers, energy-harvesting or inductive power receiver, telemetry encoder.
  • Neuromorphic co-processor — a silicon layer that mimics neuron and synapse behaviour to translate between brain signals and machine data. Key parts: spiking-neuron chips, on-chip memory, plasticity/learning circuits.
  • Decoding and encoding software — turns raw spike trains into meaning, and generates stimulation patterns the cortex can read. Key parts: neural decoders, stimulation pattern generator, calibration models.
  • Biocompatible packaging — keeps salt water, ions and immune cells away from the electronics for years. Key parts: hermetic encapsulation, flexible substrate, immune-response coatings.
  • Power supply — runs the implant without a cable through the skin. Key parts: subcutaneous battery or supercapacitor, transcutaneous charging coil.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Map the cortical circuit. Record and stimulate in animals and in people undergoing surgery to learn what activity patterns correspond to a memory or a movement.
  2. Fabricate the electrode array. Thin-film polymer threads carrying hundreds of micron-scale electrodes are patterned in a cleanroom, so the array bends with the brain instead of cutting it.
  3. Make the neuromorphic chip. Silicon or memristive circuits are designed so their dynamics resemble real synapses — this is the 'overlay' layer itself.
  4. Package it for the body. The chip and electronics are sealed in a flexible, hermetic shell that survives years of warm, salty, immune-active tissue.
  5. Implant. Surgeons place the array on or into the cortex; research is also exploring routes through blood vessels to avoid opening the skull.
  6. Close the loop. The decoder is trained on the individual's own signals until the chip's output is intelligible to their brain, and vice versa.
  7. Add the wireless link. Power and data are transmitted through the skin, allowing the implant to talk to a wearable or external processor.
  8. Co-adapt over time. The user and the system learn each other — the plasticity that would theoretically let extra memory or processing be usable rather than foreign.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Stable, high-channel-count neural electrodes — arrays in clinical research record from around a hundred neurons and degrade over months to a few years; an overlay would need thousands over a decade.
  • PARTIAL Real-time decoding of cortical activity — speech and movement have been decoded from human cortex in small trials, but general memory and reasoning have not.
  • PARTIAL Writing information into cortex — stimulation can evoke percepts and has been used experimentally to improve recall, but the neural code for complex thought is unknown.
  • NOT YET Wireless power and data at depth — millimetre-scale free-floating 'neural dust' has been proposed and modelled; getting power and bandwidth through tissue to many nodes is unsolved.
  • PARTIAL Neuromorphic hardware at biological scale — chips exist that emulate millions of neurons, but matching a living cortex in a body-safe package is far off.
  • NOT YET Understanding of memory encoding — we know memories are distributed and replayed, not stored in one place, so there is no known format to plug an overlay into.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: clinical brain-computer interfaces restore communication and cursor control for a small number of paralysed people — proof that cortex can be read and written in humans safely enough for research.
  2. Next: wireless implants with thousands of stable channels and multi-year lifetimes — everyday use without a plug through the skull, and enough data to build real decoders.
  3. Next: closed-loop bidirectional systems that write patterns as fluently as they read them — experimental memory and sensory prosthetics, first for injury and disease.
  4. Longer term: an external co-processor that a person can query as if it were part of their own cognition — the functions the tile describes — expanded working memory, parallel streams of thought, faster skill learning.
  5. Speculative: a true synaptic bridge, where silicon and biological synapses adapt to each other continuously — cognitive augmentation beyond restoration — with unresolved questions of identity, consent and access.
