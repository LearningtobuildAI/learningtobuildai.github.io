PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Electrode interface — the tissue-facing array that records from and stimulates neurons at single-cell scale. Key parts: penetrating microelectrode shanks or flexible threads, thin-film polymer substrate, recording and stimulation sites.
  • Encapsulation and delivery — keeps the body from attacking the device and the device from poisoning the body. Key parts: hermetic thin-film coating, anti-inflammatory surface treatment, insertion tooling or stent-based delivery.
  • Transceiver and power — moves thousands of neural channels in and out through skin without wires. Key parts: multiplexing chip, wireless power link, wireless data link, sealed package.
  • Compute core — the 'expansion' itself — silicon that adds memory and parallel processing to the biological circuit. Key parts: neural-network accelerator, local memory, real-time signal processor, security module.
  • Coding and decoding stack — software that turns spikes into usable signals and back into stimulation. Key parts: spike sorting, decoder models, stimulation encoders, adaptation and calibration.
  • Surgical and maintenance system — places the array precisely and keeps it working for decades. Key parts: robotic insertion, intraoperative imaging, calibration software.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the array. Choose how many electrodes, their spacing and stiffness so they reach neurons without tearing tissue.
  2. Fabricate in a cleanroom. Deposit and pattern metal and insulator layers on a flexible polymer or silicon wafer using the same lithography used for computer chips.
  3. Encapsulate and test. Coat every surface except the electrode sites, then soak-test for months to prove nothing leaks or corrodes.
  4. Build the transceiver. Design an application-specific chip that amplifies, digitises and multiplexes thousands of weak neural signals.
  5. Assemble the compute module. Pair the transceiver with a low-power accelerator and memory, keeping the total heat and power low enough for living tissue.
  6. Implant. A surgeon or robot inserts the electrodes into the target cortical area and seats the sealed electronics under the skull or behind the ear.
  7. Record and train. The system observes neural activity while the user performs tasks, and decoders learn which patterns correspond to which intentions.
  8. Close the loop. Add stimulation so the device can write information back, not just read it — the step that would make it an extension of memory rather than a sensor.
  9. Maintain over decades. Monitor signal quality, retrain decoders as the tissue shifts, and provide a safe way to remove or replace failed hardware.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Chronic high-channel-count neural recording — clinical arrays today record from roughly a hundred electrodes and research devices reach a few thousand, but signals fade over months to years.
  • PARTIAL Long-term biocompatibility — the brain forms scar tissue around implanted electrodes, which is the main reason recordings degrade.
  • PARTIAL Wireless power and data through tissue — demonstrated in early human trials, but bandwidth and heat limits are far below what an added compute layer needs.
  • NOT YET Decoding of abstract content, not just movement — today's decoders reliably read intended movement; reading or writing memory and reasoning is not solved.
  • PARTIAL Safe, high-resolution neural stimulation — stimulation works coarsely but cannot yet address individual neurons without damaging them.
  • NOT YET Verification of device and data security — a networked implant that influences thought would need guarantees no such system currently provides.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: clinical trials restore communication and cursor control for paralysed people using implanted arrays — proof that the brain tolerates and can drive a digital interface.
  2. Next: arrays that stay functional for many years and thousands of channels, placed with robotic surgery — richer signals and practical daily use, not lab sessions.
  3. Next: reliable two-way interfaces with fast wireless links — the possibility of adding rather than replacing function.
  4. Speculative: a silicon layer that the cortex treats as part of itself, adding memory and parallel processing — the stated goal — but it requires solving tissue integration, decoding of abstract thought and security together, none of which is close.
