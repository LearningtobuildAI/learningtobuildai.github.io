PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Mind uploading, also known as whole brain emulation or mind transfer, is a theoretical process aimed at transferring an individual's complete mental content from their biological brain to a computer system. This would allow the person’s consciousness and memories to exist within a virtual environment.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The process involves creating detailed models of the human brain at the cellular level through advanced neuroimaging techniques, followed by precise mapping of neural connections and patterns. These models are then simulated in software or hardware environments capable of replicating brain functions. Neural interfaces would be developed to read and write directly from/to the brain’s neurons.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Highly speculative; requires breakthroughs in nanotechnology, neuroimaging, AI, and computational power. Current technology is not sufficient for precise brain emulation.

7Build Process

Involves creating detailed maps of the human brain, developing sophisticated algorithms to simulate neural activity, and integrating these with advanced computing hardware capable of handling vast amounts of data.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Specimen preparation — gets a brain into a state where every synapse can be imaged. Key parts: fixation and staining, resin embedding or tissue clearing, automated sectioning.
  • Volumetric imaging — captures the tissue at nanometre resolution. Key parts: serial-section electron microscope, multi-beam electron microscope, expansion microscopy with light microscopy.
  • Image processing pipeline — turns petabytes of images into labelled cells and connections. Key parts: alignment software, machine-learning segmentation, human proofreading.
  • Connectome and state model — stores the wiring diagram plus what each synapse does. Key parts: neuron and synapse database, neurotransmitter and receptor typing, estimated connection strengths.
  • Emulation substrate — runs the reconstructed network fast enough to behave in real time. Key parts: large GPU or specialised compute cluster, high-bandwidth memory, neuromorphic hardware (experimental).
  • Simulation software — models neurons and synapses and lets the network interact with a virtual body and senses. Key parts: compartmental neuron models, spiking network simulator, sensory and motor interfaces.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Obtain and preserve a brain. A whole human brain must be fixed quickly after death and stabilised so that fine structure survives years of imaging. Note that the quantum-entanglement method sometimes described has no scientific basis; real work is physical imaging.
  2. Cut and image the tissue. The brain is sliced into tens of thousands of thin sections, or scanned block-face, and each is imaged by electron microscopy until every synaptic contact is resolvable. Imaging one cubic millimetre of tissue already takes months.
  3. Align and segment. Software stitches the images into a 3D volume, then machine-learning models trace every neuron and find every synapse, with human proofreaders correcting errors.
  4. Infer function, not just wiring. Wiring alone is not a mind: the reconstruction must estimate which synapses excite or inhibit, their strength, and the internal state of each cell.
  5. Build the simulation. Each reconstructed neuron becomes a mathematical model on a computer, wired up as in the connectome.
  6. Validate against behaviour. Small networks — retina, insect circuits — are simulated and their output compared with real recordings until they match.
  7. Close the loop. The simulated brain is connected to simulated senses and a body, and its behaviour is tested. Only then would anyone claim a mind had been copied.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Nanoscale connectomics — complete wiring diagrams now exist for the nematode worm (1986) and the fruit fly (2020s), and for small human cortex samples, but not for a whole human brain.
  • NOT YET Non-destructive brain scanning — current synapse-resolution imaging destroys the tissue; uploading a living person would need a method that does not.
  • NOT YET Functional state capture — memory and personality are thought to live partly in synapse strengths and molecular states, which cannot yet be read out at whole-brain scale.
  • PARTIAL Exascale computing — simulating tens of billions of neurons with detailed synapses needs far more compute than is available today.
  • NOT YET A validated theory of neural coding — without knowing how networks encode information, an accurate copy could not be checked.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: complete connectomes of small animals and millimetre-scale human cortex samples — methods for automated segmentation and proofreading at scale.
  2. Next: whole mouse brain connectome plus functional maps — testing whether wiring diagrams alone predict behaviour.
  3. Next: non-destructive, living-brain scanning at synapse resolution — theoretical route to copying a mind without destroying it, if it is physically possible at all.
  4. Longer term: whole human brain emulation running in real time — the claimed goal — and hard questions about whether the copy is the same person, which no engineering step can settle.

The process of creating a functional model of an entire brain in software, capable of simulating its cognitive functions.
