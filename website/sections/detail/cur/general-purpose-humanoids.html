PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

General-purpose humanoid robots are advanced bipedal robots designed to perform a wide range of physical tasks in human-built environments without requiring task-specific reprogramming. These robots leverage sophisticated vision-language-action models to interpret visual inputs and generate appropriate motor commands.

2Problem It Solves

General-purpose humanoid robots address the need for flexible automation in industries where tasks are diverse but require human-like dexterity and adaptability, such as warehouses, manufacturing facilities, and logistics centers.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These robots use deep learning algorithms, particularly vision-language-action models, which map camera input to motor commands through both simulation training and real-world teleoperation data refinement. This approach allows them to adapt to various environments and tasks with minimal intervention.

5Materials Used
  • aluminum alloys
  • carbon fiber composites
  • high-strength polymers
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves creating robust mechanical structures capable of supporting a human-like form with multiple degrees of freedom. Materials must be durable yet lightweight to enable efficient movement.

7Build Process

The build process includes designing the robot's structure, integrating advanced sensors for vision and force feedback, programming the control systems, and testing in both simulated and real-world environments before deployment.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Locomotion system — keeps the robot balanced and moving on two legs over uneven ground. Key parts: hip, knee and ankle joints, feet with force and torque sensors, whole-body balance controller.
  • Actuators — convert electrical power into precise, high-torque joint motion. Key parts: custom electric motors, harmonic or cycloidal reducers, joint encoders and drivers.
  • Perception — builds a real-time picture of the surroundings and of the robot's own body. Key parts: head-mounted cameras and depth sensors, inertial measurement unit, tactile sensors in hands and feet.
  • Hands and end-effectors — grasp and manipulate objects of unknown shape and weight. Key parts: multi-fingered hands, finger actuators, tactile skin.
  • Power and thermal — supplies energy and sheds heat from dense electronics. Key parts: lithium-ion battery pack, power electronics, cooling channels.
  • Compute and control — runs learned policies and safety checks in real time. Key parts: onboard GPU compute, real-time safety controller, wireless link for teleoperation.
  • Software stack — turns sensor data into actions and learns new tasks. Key parts: vision-language-action model, simulation training environment, teleoperation data pipeline.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Specify the kinematics. Choose degrees of freedom, reach, payload and speed, then derive the torque and speed each joint must deliver in Nm and radians per second.
  2. Build the actuators. Wind and assemble custom motors with low-backlash reducers and drivers, then test them on benches for torque, efficiency and heat.
  3. Fabricate the body. Cut and join lightweight aluminium or carbon-fibre limbs and torso, keeping mass low because every kilogram must be carried and accelerated.
  4. Integrate sensors, compute and power. Fit cameras, inertial and tactile sensors, the onboard computer and a battery pack of a few kilowatt-hours into the torso and head.
  5. Develop the control stack. Build a simulation model of the robot, train balance and walking policies with reinforcement learning, then transfer them to hardware.
  6. Collect teleoperation data. Humans operate the robot or wear motion-capture suits to gather thousands of demonstrations of grasping, carrying and placing.
  7. Train vision-language-action models. Large-scale imitation learning maps camera images and spoken or written instructions directly to joint commands.
  8. Test and harden. Run repeated trials for reliability, battery life, fall recovery and safe stopping near people, fixing failures before wider use.
  9. Pilot deployments. Place robots in warehouses or factories under supervision, doing narrow tasks while data and experience accumulate.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL High-torque-density electric actuators — human-scale limbs need joints that are strong, light and efficient enough to move the whole body.
  • PARTIAL Sim-to-real reinforcement learning — most walking and balance skills are learned in simulation, but the gap to the real world still causes failures.
  • PARTIAL Vision-language-action foundation models — general-purpose manipulation needs models that generalise across objects and tasks, not hand-coded routines.
  • PARTIAL Dexterous hands with tactile sensing — without touch, robots struggle with unknown objects and fragile items.
  • EXISTS Batteries with high energy density — lithium-ion packs are available, but running a full working shift on one charge remains hard.
  • NOT YET Safety standards and certification for humanoids — existing robot safety rules assume fixed arms, not free-moving bipeds sharing space with people.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot robots doing narrow tasks such as moving totes or tending machines in warehouses and factories, with human supervision — real operating data and early commercial contracts.
  2. Next: one robot switching between several unrelated tasks without reprogramming, in semi-structured workplaces — broader deployment in logistics and manufacturing.
  3. Next: foundation models that generalise across sites, with learning from a few demonstrations or plain instructions — use by smaller businesses and in service roles.
  4. Longer term: safe, reliable autonomy around untrained people in homes and public spaces — domestic assistance and care work.

18Glossary
vision-language-action models
Deep learning algorithms that interpret visual inputs to generate appropriate motor commands for robotic tasks.
teleoperation data refinement
The process of improving robot performance by using real-world operational data in addition to simulation training.
bipedal robots
Robots designed with two legs, enabling them to walk and move like humans or other bipedal creatures.
