1Definition

Ocean Thermal Energy Conversion (OTEC) is a technology that harnesses the natural temperature gradient between warm surface waters and colder deep ocean waters to generate electricity.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

OTEC systems typically consist of heat exchangers and turbines. Warm surface water is used to boil a working fluid, which then drives a turbine connected to a generator. Cold deep water is used to condense the vapor back into a liquid, completing the cycle. This process can be used in either open or closed loop configurations.

6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing OTEC plants involves constructing large-scale marine infrastructure, including heat exchangers, turbines, and power generation equipment. The materials used are generally similar to those found in conventional power plant construction, such as steel, concrete, and various alloys.

7Build Process

The build process for an OTEC plant includes site selection, environmental impact assessments, design and engineering, fabrication of components, transportation to the site, installation, testing, and commissioning. The complexity and scale of these projects can vary significantly based on the specific technology used and location.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Cold-water pipe — draws near-freezing seawater from 600–1000 m, the single hardest engineering problem. Key parts: large-diameter composite or polyethylene pipe, submerged intake screen, suspended or shelf-mounted support.
  • Warm-water intake — supplies the sun-heated surface layer used as the heat source. Key parts: surface seawater pumps, intake screen and trash rack.
  • Heat exchangers — transfer heat between seawater and the working fluid. Key parts: evaporator, condenser, titanium or corrosion-resistant tube bundles.
  • Power cycle — a closed Rankine cycle using a fluid that boils at low temperature. Key parts: ammonia or similar working fluid, pump, turbine, generator.
  • Platform — holds the machinery either on land, on a shelf, or afloat. Key parts: shore-based building or converted barge, mooring system, hull, station-keeping thrusters.
  • Grid connection — moves power to users. Key parts: subsea power cable, onshore substation.
  • Co-product plant — sells the deep cold water's other benefits to improve the economics. Key parts: desalination unit, aquaculture tanks, seawater air-conditioning loop.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Find a suitable site. You need a sustained temperature difference of roughly 20 °C between surface and deep water, with cold water reachable near shore — mostly tropical coasts and islands.
  2. Design the cold-water pipe. Pipe diameter scales with output — roughly a metre across per megawatt — so even small plants need a long, large, cold, moving structure that can survive waves and currents.
  3. Build the platform. Either a coastal plant with a trench to the seabed, or a floating hull or barge moored over deep water.
  4. Fabricate heat exchangers. Seawater is corrosive, so evaporator and condenser tubes are made from titanium or similar alloys; this is often the largest cost item.
  5. Install the cold-water pipe. The pipe is floated out, sunk under control, and its intake lowered to depth — deploying and clamping it is the manoeuvre that most often fails in practice.
  6. Fill and commission the cycle. Warm and cold seawater loops are started, the low-boiling-point working fluid is charged, and the turbine is brought up slowly to check parasitic pumping losses.
  7. Connect to the grid. A subsea cable carries power ashore; for floating plants it must survive the same motion as the platform.
  8. Operate with co-products. Deep water leaving the condenser is cold and nutrient-rich, so desalination, shellfish or fish farming, and seawater air-conditioning are coupled in to make the whole site pay.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Deep-ocean engineering and moorings — the pipe and floating platform borrow heavily from offshore oil and gas experience.
  • EXISTS Large corrosion-resistant heat exchangers — seawater destroys ordinary metals, and the heat exchangers dominate capital cost.
  • EXISTS Low-boiling-point working fluids and efficient turbines — without a fluid that boils on a 20 °C difference there is no cycle.
  • PARTIAL Large-diameter deep-water pipe deployment — demonstrated at pilot scale, but no one has built and kept one operating at tens of megawatts.
  • EXISTS Cheap subsea power cables — power must reach shore without erasing the plant's thin margin.
  • NOT YET Cost competitiveness at scale — output is small relative to a very large structure, so capital cost per megawatt stays high.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: pilot plants of roughly 100 kW, mainly on tropical coasts and islands, plus commercial seawater air-conditioning and deep-water aquaculture — operating experience with pipes, heat exchangers and cold-water use.
  2. Next: multi-megawatt demonstration plants coupled to desalination and aquaculture — evidence that the co-products can carry the economics.
  3. Next: 10–100 MW plants on islands and tropical coasts where diesel generation is expensive — a real niche market, since OTEC runs day and night regardless of weather.
  4. Longer term: large floating plantships with multi-metre cold-water pipes, possibly making hydrogen or ammonia at sea — baseload power and fuel for regions with no shallow-water option.

18Glossary
Working fluid
A substance used in an OTEC system to absorb heat from warm surface water and expand, driving a turbine.
Deep-water intake
The mechanism for bringing cold deep ocean water to the surface of an OTEC plant to condense vaporized working fluid.
Thermal pollution
An environmental issue caused by releasing warm water back into the ocean, potentially affecting local marine ecosystems.
