1Definition

Digital physics is an interdisciplinary field that investigates the idea that the universe operates on principles similar to those of digital computing, suggesting that all physical phenomena can be described by complex computations.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Discrete substrate — the hypothetical 'hardware' of reality — a finite set of states rather than continuous fields. Key parts: finite state per cell, fixed lattice or causal graph, finite information per region.
  • Update rule — the 'clock': a local rule that turns one state of the substrate into the next. Key parts: local transition function, reversibility (so information is never lost), lattice spacing and time step.
  • Emergence layer — the mathematics that would recover continuous space, time and quantum mechanics from the discrete rule. Key parts: continuum limit, relativistic symmetry, entanglement from shared information.
  • Information accounting — tests whether a proposed substrate holds the right amount of information. Key parts: entropy counting, holographic and black-hole entropy bounds.
  • Quantum simulation tools — hardware used to run candidate rules that no classical computer can handle. Key parts: trapped ions, neutral atoms, superconducting qubits, quantum cellular automaton circuits.
  • Discreteness probes — instruments looking for any measurable graininess in space-time. Key parts: distant high-energy photon timing, ultra-stable interferometers, precision atomic clocks.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Pick a substrate. Choose the fundamental objects: bits or qubits arranged on a lattice, or events linked by causal relations, with a finite state each.
  2. Write a local, reversible rule. The rule must depend only on nearby cells and lose no information, so that the model can support the reversible laws of physics we observe.
  3. Recover known physics. Show that at distances far larger than the cell size the model reproduces ordinary particles, fields, gravity and the symmetries of special relativity — the hardest step, since a simple lattice tends to single out a preferred frame.
  4. Count the information. Compare the model's entropy for a given region with the holographic bound (entropy grows with surface area, not volume). A naive three-dimensional grid stores far too much and is ruled out by black-hole thermodynamics.
  5. Simulate small versions. Run tiny lattices on classical supercomputers and on quantum processors, measuring entanglement growth and whether the simulated physics looks like ours.
  6. Search for graininess. If space-time is discrete at the Planck scale (about 1.6 × 10⁻³⁵ m, or 5.4 × 10⁻⁴⁴ s), light of different energies might travel at very slightly different speeds over cosmological distances, and interference patterns might blur. Observatories already test this.
  7. Constrain or confirm. Each null result lowers the maximum allowed cell size; a confirmed signal would be the first direct evidence that physics is computational.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Quantum information theory — gives the language of qubits, entanglement and information bounds in which these models are written.
  • PARTIAL Quantum computers and simulators — candidate rules are exponentially hard for classical machines; today's quantum devices are still small and noisy.
  • EXISTS Tests of Lorentz invariance — current precision measurements set the limits any discrete model must respect, and are the main way to look for a grain in space-time.
  • PARTIAL Holographic entropy bounds — they constrain how much information a region may contain, but a full derivation from an underlying discrete theory is missing.
  • NOT YET A working theory of quantum gravity — without it there is no agreed way to combine the discrete hypothesis with gravity and curved space-time.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: discrete and information-based models of physics studied as mathematics, with no experimental evidence for a digital substrate — a shared language — information, entropy and computation — across quantum theory, gravity and thermodynamics.
  2. Next: quantum simulators running larger, longer-lived lattice and cellular-automaton models — the ability to test whether a simple local rule can produce rich, stable, realistic physics.
  3. Next: tighter limits from cosmic photon timing and precision interferometry, or a first positive signal of discreteness — either a decisive push toward the Planck scale, or strong grounds to abandon naive discrete models.
  4. Longer term: a discrete model that predicts something new and is then confirmed — physics rebuilt on an informational foundation — with the concept remaining, more likely, a powerful mathematical lens rather than a proven description of reality.
