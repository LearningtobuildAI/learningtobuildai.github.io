PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Light source — supplies the laser light that carries the computation. Key parts: off-chip or flip-chip lasers, multi-wavelength comb sources, optical amplifiers, fibre or grating couplers.
  • Photonic compute core — performs matrix multiplication by splitting, phase-shifting and recombining light. Key parts: waveguides, Mach-Zehnder interferometer meshes or microring weight banks, phase shifters (thermo-optic or electro-optic), beam splitters and combiners.
  • Electro-optic interface — converts electronic data into light and back again. Key parts: modulators, photodetectors, digital-to-analogue and analogue-to-digital converters, driver and transimpedance amplifiers.
  • Weight storage and control — holds the numbers the mesh multiplies by, and keeps the mesh stable. Key parts: electronic memory feeding the phase shifters, non-volatile phase-change or ferroelectric elements (emerging), thermal tuning loops, calibration firmware.
  • Packaging and optical I/O — gets light in and out of the chip and joins it to electronics. Key parts: fibre arrays and edge or grating couplers, co-packaged optics, through-silicon vias and interposers, heat spreaders and coolers.
  • Software stack — maps neural networks onto physical light paths. Key parts: compiler that places matrix operations on the mesh, quantisation and calibration routines, host and framework interfaces.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the photonic circuit. Engineers lay out waveguides, splitters and phase shifters in software, simulating how light will interfere so that the mesh computes the intended matrix.
  2. Fabricate on silicon-on-insulator wafers. Standard CMOS lithography and etching form nanometre-scale rib waveguides, plus doped regions for modulators and germanium photodetectors.
  3. Add active materials. Phase-shifting layers such as silicon nitride, or thin films of lithium niobate or barium titanate, are deposited or bonded to give fast, low-loss tuning.
  4. Integrate light sources. III-V laser chips are flip-chip bonded or wafer-bonded to the photonic die, because silicon itself does not emit useful light.
  5. Package with electronics. The photonic chip is co-packaged with a driver ASIC, memory and fibre arrays, then actively aligned to sub-micron precision and sealed.
  6. Calibrate the mesh. Every phase shifter is tuned to route and weight light correctly; closed-loop control cancels the drift caused by temperature changes.
  7. Build the system. Converters, memory and host links are added, and the software compiler is used to map a real workload onto the hardware.
  8. Test and qualify. Optical, thermal and reliability testing confirms the accelerator can run continuously in a data-centre rack.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Silicon photonics manufacturing — CMOS-compatible photonic chips are already mass-produced for data-centre transceivers.
  • EXISTS High-speed, low-power electro-optic modulators — they set how fast data can be converted between electrons and photons.
  • PARTIAL Integrated on-chip lasers — bonding III-V material to silicon works in production, but fully integrated sources remain costly.
  • NOT YET Non-volatile optical memory — weights must persist and be updated cheaply, or the tuning power cancels the optical advantage.
  • PARTIAL Co-packaged optics and advanced packaging — joining photonics and electronics without losing alignment or overheating is the main yield problem.
  • EXISTS Analogue-to-digital conversion at scale — the converters at the edges are accurate and fast, though they dominate the energy budget.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: optical transceivers and co-packaged optics inside data centres — proven photonic manufacturing, packaging and fibre-attach supply chains.
  2. Next: photonic matrix-multiply accelerators for AI inference in pilot deployments — a demonstration that light can beat electronics on energy per operation for real models.
  3. Next: processors with optical compute and optical I/O on the same package — lower power and higher bandwidth between chips, easing the memory bottleneck.
  4. Longer term: optical logic, switching and memory beyond linear algebra — general-purpose optical computing rather than fixed-function accelerators.
