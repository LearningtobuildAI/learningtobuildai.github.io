PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Threat modelling and requirements — defines what failure looks like for a given system and which failures are unacceptable. Key parts: misuse and capability analysis, risk register, acceptable-risk thresholds.
  • Evaluation and benchmarking — measures both capability and harmful behaviour in a repeatable way. Key parts: benchmark suites, automated graders, human evaluation protocols, capability elicitation.
  • Red-teaming — deliberately attacks the system to find failures before users do. Key parts: expert human teams, automated adversarial prompt generation, jailbreak and prompt-injection tests, dangerous-capability probes.
  • Alignment training — shapes model behaviour during training rather than filtering it afterwards. Key parts: human preference data, reward models, preference optimisation such as RLHF, rule-based and constitutional training methods, refusal and honesty training.
  • Interpretability and monitoring — looks inside the model and watches its behaviour over time. Key parts: probing classifiers, feature and circuit analysis, reasoning-trace monitoring, anomaly detection.
  • Inference-time guardrails — catches harmful inputs and outputs while the system is running. Key parts: input classifiers, output filters, tool-use permissions, sandboxing, rate limits and access control.
  • Governance and assurance — makes safety claims checkable by people outside the developer. Key parts: model and system cards, risk-management frameworks such as the NIST AI RMF, conformity assessment under the EU AI Act, incident reporting and audits.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the threat model. For the specific deployment, list plausible misuse, accident and failure modes, and set which outcomes are unacceptable rather than merely undesirable.
  2. Build data and feedback pipelines. Assemble evaluation datasets and a workforce that can label harmful, helpful and borderline outputs consistently, with quality control on the annotators.
  3. Train with an alignment objective. Fine-tune the base model on demonstrations and preference comparisons so it refuses clearly harmful requests and explains uncertainty, then check that this did not degrade capability.
  4. Construct the evaluation suite. Combine public benchmarks with private held-out tests, including automated graders and human review, and freeze a version so results are comparable across model releases.
  5. Red-team iteratively. Expert teams and automated attack tools try jailbreaks, prompt injection and capability elicitation; every failure found is patched and re-tested until the suite is stable.
  6. Add inference-time guardrails. Wrap the model in input and output classifiers, restrict which tools and data it can reach, and sandbox any code execution.
  7. Probe the internals. Use interpretability tools to check whether the model represents and uses concepts the way its outputs suggest, rather than relying on behaviour alone.
  8. Document and audit. Publish or file system cards, evaluation results and residual risks; submit to external review or conformity assessment where regulation requires it.
  9. Monitor after deployment. Log real-world usage for new failure modes, feed incidents back into the evaluation suite, and retrain or withdraw the system when thresholds are crossed.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Large-scale training and serving infrastructure — evaluations, red-teaming and guardrails all need repeated access to the model at production scale.
  • EXISTS Human preference and annotation pipelines — alignment training depends on large volumes of consistent human judgements, often from contract workforces.
  • PARTIAL Interpretability methods that scale to large models — feature-level analysis works on small models and selected layers, not yet as a full audit of a frontier system.
  • NOT YET Evaluation for capabilities beyond human expertise — if a model can do something no human can verify, benchmarks and human graders cannot confirm whether it did it safely or correctly.
  • PARTIAL Formal verification of neural networks — proving properties holds for small networks and narrow input ranges, and does not currently scale to frontier models.
  • PARTIAL Regulatory and standards infrastructure — the EU AI Act and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework provide structure, but enforcement and technical standards are still being written.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: frontier developers run internal red teams, publish system cards and apply runtime filters; general-purpose AI obligations begin applying under the EU AI Act — a baseline of documented practice and incident reporting.
  2. Next: standardised third-party evaluations and repeatable audits — safety claims that buyers, regulators and insurers can compare across developers.
  3. Next: interpretability mature enough to check properties directly instead of sampling behaviour — assurance that does not depend on the model failing in a test first.
  4. Longer term: scalable oversight methods for systems that outperform their overseers — a credible path to deploying very capable systems under meaningful human control.
