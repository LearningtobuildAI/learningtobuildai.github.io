PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

These solutions work by converting electric power into chemical or physical forms for storage. Upon demand, they convert this stored energy back into electricity. Solid-state batteries use a solid electrolyte instead of liquid to increase safety and efficiency, while flow batteries store energy in external tanks of electrolytes, offering scalability.

5Materials Used
  • lithium
  • solid electrolytes
  • flow electrolytes
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

Manufacturing involves complex processes including electrode synthesis, cell assembly, and testing. Solid-state batteries require specialized equipment due to their solid electrolyte technology.

7Build Process

The build process includes raw material sourcing, battery cell fabrication, module assembly, system integration, and quality assurance tests.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Partly there

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Storage medium — stores electrical energy as chemical energy; the chemistry varies (lithium-ion, solid-state, flow). Key parts: electrodes or electrolyte, separator or membrane, current collectors.
  • Power conversion system — converts DC from storage to AC for the grid and back. Key parts: inverter, transformer, switchgear.
  • Battery management system — monitors and controls cells to maximise life and safety. Key parts: sensors, controllers, communication links.
  • Thermal management — keeps the storage medium within its safe temperature range. Key parts: liquid cooling loops, air cooling, heat exchangers.
  • Enclosure and safety — houses the system and mitigates fire or leakage risks. Key parts: containers or buildings, fire suppression, ventilation.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Produce active materials. Refine lithium, nickel, manganese or vanadium into electrode powders or electrolyte solutions.
  2. Manufacture cells. Coat electrodes onto foils, stack or wind them with a separator, fill with electrolyte and seal — or, for solid-state, sinter a solid electrolyte layer.
  3. Assemble modules. Group cells into modules with cooling plates, sensors and interconnects.
  4. Integrate into containers. Install modules, power conversion, battery management and thermal systems into standard shipping containers or buildings.
  5. Prepare the site. Build concrete pads, lay cables and connect to the grid at a substation.
  6. Install and connect. Place containers, wire them together and to the grid, and connect controls.
  7. Commission and test. Charge and discharge the system, verify safety systems and grid compliance.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Advanced battery chemistries — solid-state electrolytes and flow membranes promise better safety and longevity but are not yet mass-produced.
  • PARTIAL Low-cost manufacturing — scale is needed to bring storage costs down to compete with peaking gas plants.
  • EXISTS Power electronics — inverters and transformers are mature and widely available.
  • PARTIAL Grid integration standards — rules for connecting and dispatching storage vary and are still evolving.
  • PARTIAL Recycling infrastructure — retired batteries must be recycled to recover materials and avoid waste.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: lithium-ion dominates grid storage; flow batteries deployed in some projects — firm capacity to integrate solar and wind.
  2. Next: solid-state batteries reach commercial scale for vehicles and then grid — higher energy density, improved safety and longer life.
  3. Next: flow batteries scale up for multi-day storage — storage that can shift energy across days, not just hours.
  4. Longer term: ultra-low-cost chemistries using abundant materials (e.g. sodium, iron) — widespread deployment for seasonal storage.
