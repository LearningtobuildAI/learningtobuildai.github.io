PART 1Executive Overview
1Definition

Volumetric Tomography is an advanced additive manufacturing technique that enables the rapid creation of three-dimensional objects by selectively curing photopolymer resin within a volume using precise light projections.

Category
Additive
Stage

2Problem It Solves

Traditional additive manufacturing methods are slow due to their layer-by-layer approach, which limits speed and complexity of the objects that can be produced. Volumetric Tomography addresses these limitations by offering faster production rates and greater design flexibility.

PART 2Technical & Manufacturing
4How It Works

The process involves projecting intersecting light beams into a container filled with liquid photopolymer resin. The intersections of these light beams act as focal points where the resin rapidly solidifies, forming the desired object in a single step without the need for layer-by-layer printing. This method significantly reduces print times and can produce highly complex geometries.

5Materials Used
  • photopolymer resin
  • light sources
6Manufacturing / Creation Process

The technology requires specialized equipment for light projection and resin handling. The process is highly automated but still benefits from precise control systems to ensure accurate curing patterns.

7Build Process

A container filled with photopolymer resin is placed in a machine that projects intersecting laser beams or other light sources into the volume of resin. The light causes the resin to solidify at specific points, forming the object layer by layer until completion.

8Energy Requirements

Field units draw low hundreds of watts; fabrication is energy-intensive due to vacuum baking and resin curing processes.

PART 3Engineering & Build Guide

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Light engine — floods the resin with patterned light from one or more directions. Key parts: DLP projector or laser, high-power LED or laser diode, beam-shaping optics.
  • Resin chemistry — turns light dose into solid, but only past a threshold so unwanted light does nothing. Key parts: monomer and crosslinker, photoinitiator, inhibitor (often dissolved oxygen) or a photo-switchable initiator pair.
  • Container and stage — holds the liquid and moves it through the beam. Key parts: transparent glass or plastic vial, index-matching fluid, rotation stage or translation stage.
  • Dose-modelling software — solves the inverse problem: which projection patterns add up to the intended shape. Key parts: ray-trace or Radon-transform model, tomographic reconstruction (filtered back projection or iterative optimiser), calibration of non-linear cure response.
  • Post-processing — removes unpolymerised liquid and finishes the part. Key parts: drain and wash station, post-cure light oven, solvent or water rinse.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Define the target shape. A 3D model is converted into a target dose map — the amount of light energy every point in the volume should receive.
  2. Solve the projection set. Software computes the family of 2D images whose overlap reproduces that dose map, the same inverse problem used to reconstruct CT scans, run backwards.
  3. Formulate the resin. A liquid monomer is mixed with a photoinitiator and an inhibitor. The inhibitor soaks up the first photons so that only points crossed by many beams — above the threshold dose — actually set.
  4. Fill and index-match. The resin fills a transparent cylindrical vial; the vial sits in a matching fluid so light enters without distortion.
  5. Expose the volume. Patterned light is projected through the spinning vial for tens of seconds, or in some designs a moving patterned beam and a perpendicular light sheet cross inside the resin. All points cure at once.
  6. Drain and clean. The gelled part is lifted out, uncured liquid drains away, and the part is rinsed. Because the surrounding resin holds it in place, no support structures are needed.
  7. Post-cure. A final flood of light hardens the part fully and sets its mechanical properties.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Computational tomography and inverse-problem solvers — the projection patterns are essentially a radiotherapy treatment plan computed for a volume of resin.
  • PARTIAL Dose-threshold photopolymer chemistry — without a sharp threshold between cured and uncured, gentle stray light smears the part.
  • PARTIAL Transparent, low-scattering resins — pigments, ceramic particles or living cells scatter and blur the projected beams, limiting resolution and loading.
  • EXISTS High-brightness projectors and lasers — the whole volume must absorb enough energy in seconds, so light output sets the print time.
12Path to Full Scale
  1. Now: laboratory demonstrations printing centimetre-scale parts in tens of seconds, mainly in clear acrylate resins — a genuinely new speed class in additive manufacturing, and printing without support structures.
  2. Next: finer resolution and larger build volumes, with loaded resins (ceramic, composite) — lenses, dental and hearing-aid parts, small precision components.
  3. Next: cell-laden and biocompatible resins — tissue scaffolds and implants printed around living cells in one shot.
  4. Longer term: reliable industrial machines with calibrated process control — production use alongside established vat photopolymerisation, where speed or smooth surfaces matter more than fine detail.

18Glossary
volumetric tomography

photopolymer resin

light projection
The process of directing and shaping light beams into specific patterns to cure photopolymer resin in a 3D volume.
