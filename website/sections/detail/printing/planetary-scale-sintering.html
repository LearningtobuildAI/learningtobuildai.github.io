PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Orbital reflector array — collects sunlight in space and redirects it toward a construction site on a planetary surface. Key parts: large thin-film or segmented mirrors, lightweight support truss, attitude control and station-keeping thrusters, solar panels for housekeeping power.
  • Beam targeting and relay optics — focuses the reflected sunlight into a tight spot and keeps it aimed as the target moves. Key parts: secondary concentrator mirrors, adaptive optics or deformable mirrors, fine steering mechanisms, optical tracking sensors.
  • Regolith handling system — excavates, transports and spreads raw regolith in controlled layers. Key parts: robotic excavator or bucket-wheel, hopper and conveyor, gantry or robotic arm for deposition, leveling blade or screed.
  • Sintering head and thermal control — receives the concentrated beam and scans it across the regolith to melt or sinter it without over-evaporating. Key parts: beam raster scanner, pyrometers and thermal cameras, closed-loop control computer, shielding to protect nearby equipment.
  • Power and thermal management — keeps the orbital mirrors and ground equipment operating through lunar day and night. Key parts: radioisotope or fission power units on the surface, batteries and power conditioning, radiators for waste heat, cryogenic or high-temperature tolerant components.
  • Structure verification — checks that each sintered layer is sound before the next is added. Key parts: lidar and stereo cameras, ultrasonic or acoustic testers, sample return or in-situ mechanical testers.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Survey and site preparation. A robotic mission maps a flat area, measures regolith depth and composition, and clears loose rocks so that layers can be spread evenly.
  2. Deploy the orbital mirror. A large lightweight reflector — either a single gossamer mirror or a constellation of smaller ones — is launched and assembled in a polar or Sun-synchronous orbit, where it can see the site for long periods.
  3. Land ground equipment. Excavators, a gantry or robotic arm, a sintering head with sensors, and a power source are delivered to the site and tested.
  4. Calibrate the beam. The orbital mirror focuses sunlight onto a small test patch. Operators measure spot size, temperature and heating rate, then adjust pointing and focus to achieve uniform sintering at around 1000–1200 °C.
  5. Deposit the first layer. The regolith handler spreads a thin, even layer — typically a few millimetres to centimetres thick — over the build area.
  6. Sinter the layer. The concentrated beam is scanned across the layer. Pyrometers feed back to the control computer, which modulates scan speed and beam power to melt grain surfaces together without boiling the regolith.
  7. Repeat layer by layer. Fresh regolith is deposited on the cooled, solid layer and sintered again. Repeating this builds walls, pads or domes additively, with each pass fusing to the one below.
  8. Finish and inspect. The structure is allowed to cool slowly to avoid cracking. Lidar and acoustic tests check for voids or weak bonds; defects can be repaired by local re-sintering.
  9. Commission the structure. The finished object — a landing pad, radiation shield, road or habitat shell — is put into service, potentially with added sealing or outfitting.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • PARTIAL Concentrated solar sintering of lunar regolith — Laboratory experiments have melted and fused regolith simulant using focused sunlight and microwaves, but not yet on the Moon or at structural scale.
  • PARTIAL Large lightweight space structures — Deployable solar arrays and antennas exist, but kilometre-scale mirrors require new packaging, assembly and control techniques.
  • PARTIAL Robotic excavation and regolith transport — Rovers and landers have moved small amounts of soil on the Moon and Mars, but continuous mining and spreading at construction scale is unproven.
  • PARTIAL Precision beam steering and tracking — Laser communications and ground-based solar concentrators demonstrate fine pointing, but holding a high-power beam on a moving surface target for hours is new.
  • PARTIAL Lunar-surface power and thermal control — Radioisotope heaters and small fission concepts exist, but powering excavators, gantries and sensors through the 14-day lunar night at scale is unsolved.
  • EXISTS Materials for high-flux mirrors — Thin-film reflectors and coatings survive space radiation and ultraviolet light in current spacecraft, though scaling to very large areas remains a challenge.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: Laboratory demonstrations sinter lunar regolith simulant with concentrated light and microwaves — Proof that regolith can be fused without additives, and basic data on temperatures and strengths.
  2. Next: Small lander-mounted mirror sinters a test object on the lunar surface — Real-environment data on vacuum, dust and thermal cycling for sintering.
  3. Next: Orbital mirror demonstrates beam delivery to a surface target — Validation of long-distance power beaming and pointing control.
  4. Then: Robotic construction of a simple structure such as a landing pad or radiation wall — A repeatable additive construction process for lunar infrastructure.
  5. Longer term: Full-scale habitats or asteroid structures built by planetary-scale sintering — Large pressurised volumes and shielding made from local materials, reducing launch mass from Earth.
