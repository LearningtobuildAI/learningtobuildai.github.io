PART 3Engineering & Build Guide
Not yet possible

Written by DeepSeek (deepseek-flash) on 29 Sep 2026. General engineering knowledge, not a manufacturer's procedure. Steps for anything not yet built are a theoretical route, not a plan anyone is executing.

9Engineering Breakdown
  • Manipulator tips — Pick up, position and bond individual atoms or molecules. Key parts: atomically sharp probe tips, functionalised tip molecules, bond-forming and bond-breaking mechanism.
  • Positioning stage — Move each tip with sub-atomic precision in three dimensions. Key parts: piezoelectric actuators, flexure bearings, closed-loop position feedback.
  • Control and sensing — Determine where atoms are and guide each reaction step. Key parts: tunnelling-current or force sensors, scanning probe electronics, real-time control software.
  • Feedstock delivery — Supply the right atoms or molecules to each tip in the right order. Key parts: molecular reservoirs, transport channels, selective binding sites.
  • Environment enclosure — Keep out contaminants and hold a stable, cold, vibration-free vacuum. Key parts: ultra-high vacuum chamber, cryogenic cooling, vibration isolation.
  • Product design and simulation — Specify the atomic layout of the target and the reaction pathway to build it. Key parts: molecular modelling software, reaction path search, error-checking algorithms.
  • Thermal management — Remove heat from exothermic bond-forming reactions at the nanoscale. Key parts: nanoscale heat sinks, cooling pathways.
10How It Would Be Made — Step by Step
  1. Design the product and the tooling. Map every atom and bond in the target object, and work out a sequence of reactions that builds it without unwanted side reactions.
  2. Synthesise the first manipulator molecules. Use existing chemical methods to make tip molecules that can selectively bind and release specific atoms.
  3. Position a single manipulator. Use a scanning probe microscope to place one manipulator tip and image its position before each operation.
  4. Demonstrate mechanosynthesis. Use the tip to form simple bonds one at a time — for example, adding a carbon atom to a growing molecule — and verify the result.
  5. Bootstrap replication. Design a manipulator that can build copies of itself from simple feedstock molecules, so the system can grow.
  6. Scale to parallel arrays. Replicate many manipulators and arrange them in dense arrays on a chip-like substrate, each independently positioned and controlled.
  7. Operate the factory. Feed in feedstock, run programmed sequences across the array, and harvest atomically precise products.
  8. Maintain and error-correct. Continuously monitor for misplaced atoms and bonds, and correct errors before they propagate.
11Prerequisite Technologies
  • EXISTS Scanning probe microscopy and single-atom manipulation — Scanning tunnelling and atomic force microscopes can already image and move individual atoms, though very slowly.
  • PARTIAL Mechanosynthesis — Some experiments have used probe tips to form or break specific bonds, but no general, programmable method exists.
  • NOT YET Scalable parallel nanomanipulation — One tip is far too slow for manufacturing; thousands working in parallel without interference is unsolved.
  • NOT YET Self-replicating molecular machines — A system that can make copies of its own manipulators from simple feedstock is theoretical.
  • NOT YET Atomically precise error correction — Any practical assembler must detect and fix misplaced atoms at rates far beyond current capability.
  • PARTIAL Computational design of reaction pathways — Molecular simulation can suggest pathways, but predicting and controlling complex sequences remains hard.
12Theoretical Path to Reality
  1. Now: single-atom manipulation and synthesised molecular machines in the laboratory — basic tools and proof that atoms can be positioned on demand.
  2. Next: mechanosynthesis of small molecules using scanning probes — a general method for making and breaking chosen bonds with a tip.
  3. Next: self-assembling molecular components that form nanoscale structures — cheap production of atomically precise parts without assembling every atom individually.
  4. Longer term: parallel arrays of molecular manipulators — throughput high enough to build useful macroscopic objects.
  5. Long term: general-purpose atomic assemblers — bottom-up fabrication of a wide range of materials and devices.
