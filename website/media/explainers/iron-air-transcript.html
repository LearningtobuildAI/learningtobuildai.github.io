A battery built for days

Most people picture a battery inside a phone or car. This one is built for the electricity grid. Form Energy is developing and commercialising iron-air storage designed to deliver electricity for up to one hundred hours.

Who actually makes it?

The company is Form Energy. Its Form Factory One is in Weirton, West Virginia, on the site of a former steel mill. These diagrams explain its published technology. They are illustrations, not footage of its factory or equipment.

Manufacturer source

Reversible rusting

During discharge, iron reacts with oxygen and becomes rust, releasing stored electrical energy. Charging reverses that reaction and restores the iron. The system stores energy supplied to it; it does not create free energy.

Manufacturer source

From cells to a grid system

Form describes cells containing iron and air electrodes with a water-based electrolyte. Cells are assembled into modules, then grouped into protective enclosures. This is the public system architecture, not a proprietary manufacturing recipe.

Manufacturer source

Manufacturing at scale

The company describes electrode production and cell assembly lines at its factory. Its published equipment needs include coating, pressing, welding and automation. Exact materials, settings, quality limits and assembly instructions are not disclosed here.

Manufacturer source

Real systems, careful claims

Form reports grid-connected test systems in California and commercial agreements with utilities. Testing a system and signing an agreement are different from operating a large commercial fleet. The technology is moving from pilots into commercial deployment.

Manufacturer source

Why it matters

Multi-day storage is intended to help electricity grids through extended shortages and periods of low renewable output. Duration alone is not enough: operators also need reliable performance, suitable sites and viable costs. Read the linked manufacturer sources for the evidence and its limits.
